## What the Crowd Said in Thessalonica

Luke gives the whole episode a handful of verses, and it is worth slowing down to see how it began. Paul and Silas arrived in Thessalonica and spent three Sabbaths in the synagogue, reasoning from the Scriptures that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead, and that the Jesus they were announcing was that Christ (Acts 17:1 to 3). Some Jews were persuaded, along with a large number of devout Greeks and a good many leading women. Other Jews were jealous. They gathered some rough men from the marketplace, started a riot in the city, and attacked the house of a man named Jason, looking for Paul and Silas to bring them out to the people (Acts 17:4 to 5).

The missionaries weren't there. So the mob took Jason instead.

> *"And when they could not find them, they dragged Jason and some of the brothers before the city authorities, shouting, 'These men who have turned the world upside down have come here also, and Jason has received them, and they are all acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus.'"*
> — Acts 17:6 to 7 (ESV)

They meant it as an insult. Christians have repeated it ever since as a boast, usually with a warm smile and a picture in mind of the early church as an energetic youth fellowship. I think the boast is right. But read the charge again and notice the word that carries it, which is "king". Nobody is dragged before magistrates for being loud. These men were dragged there over a loyalty, and the crowd had said more than it knew. The rest of this essay is an attempt to hear what they said.

So the question is what the crowd saw that we have stopped seeing. To answer it we have to go to Thessalonica itself, then to what Jesus taught about power, because that is where the upside down part lives, and then to what happened when ordinary people took him at his word.

***

## A City That Owed Everything to Caesar

Thessalonica in Paul's day was a Greek seaport and trading city that Rome had made a free city. Free did not mean independent. A Dallas Theological Seminary writer puts it plainly, saying the city could mint its own currency, make its own laws and govern itself through local authorities, but that this freedom depended on its loyalty to Rome (Bergthold, 2023). The same article notes that first century inscriptions mention a temple of Caesar there. Another scholar, Callia Rulmu, points to an inscription that J. R. Harrison links to a temple to Caesar in the city (Rulmu, 2010). I would hold the details loosely, since inscriptions are fragments, but the picture is steady. A city that lived on Rome's favour showed its thanks in public worship of Rome's rulers.

Luke's account contains a small detail that rewards attention. The "city authorities" in Acts 17:6 are called politarchs, which is the Greek word he uses. For a long time that looked like a slip, because the title turns up nowhere in surviving Greek literature. Then inscriptions from the region began to appear, and one stone from an arch at the Vardar Gate, now in the British Museum, named such officials. The old Bible encyclopaedia entry by M. N. Tod says that the title "occurs nowhere in Greek literature", and that "its correctness is proved beyond possibility of question" by the inscriptions (Tod, 1915). I like this evidence because of what it says about the storyteller. A man inventing a tale does not usually bother to get an odd local job title exactly right. A man reporting what happened sometimes does.

Those politarchs had their own worry, and it wasn't theology. A free city that let a foreign agitator preach another king could lose its freedom. The authorities in Luke's account seem to know it. They didn't convict anyone. They took a security payment from Jason and the rest and let them go.

> *"And when they had taken money as security from Jason and the rest, they let them go."*
> — Acts 17:9 (ESV)

The gospel arrived in a city where the official answer to "who is lord?" was already written and carved in stone. You can see why a second answer would worry people.

## The Decrees Nobody Can Quite Name

"Acting against the decrees of Caesar" is a vague charge, and scholars have never agreed on what it pointed to. I will give three proposals, because the disagreement itself tells you something.

E. A. Judge argued that the decrees were imperial laws against predicting a change of ruler, and he also suggested that a loyalty oath to the emperor might have counted as a "decree" in the minds of the Thessalonians (as reported in Hardin, 2006, and Rulmu, 2010). We have an example of such an oath from Paphlagonia in 3 BC, in which the people swear to Augustus that they will treat his friends as friends and his enemies as enemies, and will report any plot against him (Sherk, 1984). Justin Hardin proposes something different. He suggests the charges, and the seizure of payment, relate to imperial laws repressing the voluntary associations of the Greek and Roman world, which would make Jason's house an illegal assembly (Hardin, 2006). Bruce Winter, in a book that its reviewer finds persuasive on the spread of the imperial cult and less convincing on its effect on Christians, links the charge to a decree of Claudius and to the idea that converts had stopped giving divine honours to the emperor (Charlesworth, 2017).

I don't know which of these is right, and I am not sure anyone does. What strikes me is what they share. In all three, the offence is about allegiance. Someone, somewhere in that city, had worked out that the message of this small group went well beyond private spirituality, and touched the question of who gets the last word.

The crowd's own language fits. The verb behind "turned upside down" means to disturb, to unsettle, to stir up and overthrow. Thayer's lexicon notes it can mean stirring up tumults in the state, and the same verb is used in Acts 21:38 when a Roman commander asks Paul whether he is the Egyptian who led a revolt (Strong's Greek 387, n.d.). And "the world" is the Greek word that, in writers on Roman affairs, was used as a name for the empire (Strong's Greek 3625, n.d.). So a fair paraphrase of the crowd is this. *Men who have unsettled the empire have turned up here too.*

Was the charge true? Partly, and that is the interesting part. Paul and Silas weren't plotting a coup, and Jesus himself had told Pilate that his kingdom was not from this world (John 18:36). But the claim that Jesus is Lord, and that he is coming back, really does leave Caesar with a rival. The crowd were wrong about the method. They were not wrong about the stakes.

## What Paul Wrote to the Same Church

Paul wrote to this church not long after leaving it. Winter, as his reviewer reports him, dates Paul's visit to about AD 50 to 51 (Charlesworth, 2017). Early in the letter Paul reminds them what the whole region has heard about their conversion.

> *"and how you turned to God from idols to serve the living and true God, and to wait for his Son from heaven, whom he raised from the dead, Jesus who delivers us from the wrath to come."*
> — 1 Thessalonians 1:9 to 10 (ESV)

Take the vocabulary of waiting for a Son from heaven who delivers us and put it down in a city with a temple of Caesar. In 9 BC the province of Asia adopted a calendar decree that spoke of Augustus's birthday as "the beginning of the good tidings for the world", and described him as a saviour sent "both for us and for our descendants" (Wikipedia contributors, n.d. a). Rome had its gospel. It had its saviour. The Thessalonian Christians were telling a different story with the same words.

Paul also writes, a few paragraphs on, that when people are saying "There is peace and security", sudden destruction will come upon them (1 Thessalonians 5:3). Many scholars hear in that phrase a jab at Roman propaganda about the peace of the empire. Jeffrey Weima argues exactly this (Weima, 2012). Joel White argues that it goes beyond the evidence, since the two words are not found together as a slogan in imperial sources, and the theme of security became prominent in imperial propaganda only later in Nero's reign (White, 2009). I think White's caution is fair. I would not hang a case on one phrase.

I don't need to. A church that has turned from idols to wait for a Son from heaven, and that has to put up a security payment for it, already has a loyalty that Caesar cannot share. Whether Paul meant to mock Rome or not, Rome could see the problem.

The Pax Romana was real, and so was its price. It was a peace kept by legions and crosses, an order in which power flowed downward and the weak adjusted themselves to the strong. Into that order came a small group of Jews and Greeks and others telling the story of a Galilean carpenter whom the state had executed and God had raised. They had no army, no wealth, no political machinery. They had a message, and it was enough to unsettle a city.

## How Heaven Counts Greatness

Here we reach what the crowd could not read, and what I think the church has often misread too. The kingdom Jesus announced didn't rival Caesar by copying him. It overturned the whole idea of what a kingdom is for.

Jesus said it to his own disciples, who had just been quarrelling about rank, and he rested it on his own case. The Son of Man, he said, came to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45).

> *"But it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all."*
> — Mark 10:43 to 44 (ESV)

The sentence just before it describes the world's version. Those considered rulers of the Gentiles, he says, "lord it over them" (Mark 10:42). The kingdom of God does not go into competition with that. It refuses the premise. A throne in Jesus' kingdom is reached by going down, and the man in charge is the one holding the towel.

God says through Isaiah that his thoughts are not our thoughts (Isaiah 55:8). The verse is often read as a comfort in confusing seasons, and it can be. In its own setting it is a statement about the difference in kind between God's reasoning and ours, offered to a people who had been sure they knew how things worked. The Gospels show what that difference looks like when it walks around. Peter learned it the hard way, in a scene most of us would rather forget. Jesus has just told his disciples that he must suffer and die, and Peter takes him aside to correct him.

> *"But he turned and said to Peter, 'Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance to me.'"*
> — Matthew 16:23 (ESV)

Jesus went on to say that Peter was setting his mind on the things of man and not the things of God, and notice that Peter's idea was sensible. Any person who wanted the kingdom to succeed would have said the same. Jesus calls it the mind of man. And two verses later he states the rule that governs everything else.

> *"For whoever would save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for my sake will find it."*
> — Matthew 16:25 (ESV)

C. S. Lewis wrote the pattern out in the closing pages of *Mere Christianity*. "Give up your self, and you will find your real self. Lose your life and you will save it. Submit to death, death of your ambitions and favourite wishes every day and death of your whole body in the end: submit with every fibre of your being, and you will find eternal life" (Lewis, 1996, as reproduced by Renovare, n.d.). Lewis fought this logic for years before it undid him, and he knew he was describing a death followed by a resurrection, and not a self improvement plan. I would add only that it is no technique. A man who serves in order to be made great hasn't understood the sentence.

## Mary Sang It Before Jesus Preached It

The reversal didn't begin with the Sermon on the Mount. Luke puts it in the mouth of a teenage girl in a hill town, before her son is born.

> *"My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior,"*
> — Luke 1:46 to 47 (ESV)

Her song, the Magnificat, goes on to say that God has shown strength with his arm and scattered the proud in the thoughts of their hearts (Luke 1:51). The next lines, which I give in my own words, say he has pulled rulers down from their thrones, lifted up the lowly, filled the hungry with good things and sent the rich away empty (Luke 1:52 to 53). Notice the tenses. She sings of what God has done, as if it were finished, while she is carrying a child in a country under occupation. Then Luke turns the page and opens chapter two with a decree from Caesar Augustus, and a census that sends this same family on the road to Bethlehem. The two decrees sit side by side in his telling, the emperor's and the girl's, and Luke leaves us to decide which one is the real power.

Jesus, once he began to teach, sang the same song in prose. The first sentences of the Sermon on the Mount are a list of people nobody would have chosen as the winners.

> *"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."*
> — Matthew 5:3 (ESV)

> *"Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted."*
> — Matthew 5:4 (ESV)

> *"Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth."*
> — Matthew 5:5 (ESV)

Anyone who has watched who gets the front seats at a Nigerian wedding or burial knows the usual ranking, and it isn't this one. The Beatitudes say that the kingdom has a different guest list. The poor in spirit have the kingdom now, not as a prize waiting at the end. The meek, who in any bargaining they enter are the ones who get squeezed, are the ones who end up with the earth. I don't read these as sweet sayings to be hung on a wall. They are the same rule as Mark 10:43, said in blessing, and they were as shocking to the first hearers as they would be at a campaign rally today.

## God Chose the People Nobody Chose

Paul takes the same pattern and applies it to a church. The Corinthians were a clever, status conscious crowd, and he asks them to look at themselves.

> *"For consider your calling, brothers: not many of you were wise according to worldly standards, not many were powerful,"*
> — 1 Corinthians 1:26 (ESV)

He goes on to say that not many were of noble birth, and then gives the reasoning in plain terms. God chose what the world counts as foolish, as weak, as low and despised, even the things that are not, to bring to nothing the things that are (1 Corinthians 1:27 to 28). He says the first of those in a sentence that deserves to be quoted exactly.

> *"God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise;"*
> — 1 Corinthians 1:27 (ESV)

And he states the purpose.

> *"so that no human being might boast in the presence of God."*
> — 1 Corinthians 1:29 (ESV)

That last clause explains the whole upside down method. If God's way ran through the clever and the well born, the clever and the well born would take the credit, and he would be a patron among patrons. He chooses people who can't take the credit. It tells us why the early church grew in the places it did, and it also tells us why a Christian movement that begins to pride itself on its celebrities, its buildings or its jets is in trouble before anyone has noticed.

Paul says the same thing from the other side a few verses earlier.

> *"For the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men."*
> — 1 Corinthians 1:25 (ESV)

## Giving Away and Growing Richer

The same shape shows up in money, which is where most of us feel it first.

> *"One gives freely, yet grows all the richer; another withholds what he should give, and only suffers want."*
> — Proverbs 11:24 (ESV)

The economy we live in runs the other way. Hold on to what you have, guard it, and add to it where you can. That logic isn't stupid, and in its own field it works. Every market ever built leans on some version of it. But Proverbs says that in the kingdom of God the hand held open is the one that stays full.

Older translations show something that the ESV smooths over. The King James reads, "There is that scattereth, and yet increaseth", and the little word "and" matters. The text does not present increase as a surprise that sneaks in against the logic of the sentence. It sets scattering and increasing in sequence, like planting and harvest. The scattering is how the increase comes. Anyone raised near a farm, or with grandparents who farmed, knows the ordinary rule behind it. The man who eats all his seed yams in the lean months has nothing to plant when the rains come. You have to let go of what you hold in order to have it again next year.

Some of the preaching that has grown up around this verse has done it no favours. It makes giving into a deposit and God into the bank, and when the return is slow, it advises you to deposit more. That reduces a principle of generosity to a bet. It also gets the direction wrong again, because the whole thing is still about getting, only with a prayer attached. A giving that is secretly a purchase has not turned anything upside down. It has put a religious label on the old arithmetic, and it treats God as a fund manager, which is a Western market idea with a Pentecostal accent. Proverbs 11:24 is about what abundance looks like in a world made by a God who is himself generous.

To be fair to the people who preach about breakthrough, the Bible does show people rising. Joseph goes from a pit to a palace and Daniel ends up in a king's court, and nobody should apologise for hoping that faithful people will thrive. My complaint is with the sermon that stops there. The passage that goes with Mark 10:44 is not the one about a seat at the table. It is the one about the towel.

## Why Lucifer Reads the Map Wrongly

You can't talk about the upside down mind of God without asking about someone who looked at it and decided it was beneath him.

Some care is needed here. Isaiah 14 is, in its setting, a taunt against the king of Babylon. Many interpreters across church history have read the passage as reaching past the human tyrant to the power behind him, and Jesus' remark that he saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven (Luke 10:18) has encouraged the same reading. I find that reading reasonable, and I hold it as a tradition of interpretation rather than a plain statement. What matters for this essay is the pattern in the words themselves. The king says in his heart that he will ascend to heaven, set his throne above the stars of God, sit on the mount of assembly and make himself like the Most High (Isaiah 14:13 to 14). Five times he says "I will", and every verb points upward.

That is the logic of the crowd in Thessalonica, and it is the logic of Caesar. It reads authority as something you reach by climbing, and it reads God as the top of a pyramid. It missed what Revelation shows. God's power is not held at the top of a ladder. It is spent in descent, in washing feet and in a cross. You cannot seize it, because it is the kind of authority that can only be received, and it can only be received by people who have stopped trying to grab.

Peter's rebuke belongs here. When Jesus called him Satan for setting his mind on the things of man (Matthew 16:23), he wasn't insulting him. He was diagnosing him. The things of man are the things of climbing, protecting, accumulating and being seen at the top. G. K. Chesterton argued in *Orthodoxy*, in the chapter on the paradoxes of Christianity, that the faith survives because it holds apparent opposites together without destroying either, the God who is infinite and the child in the stable, the Lion and the Lamb. I think he saw something true. A system that insists on tidy hierarchy is easier to understand and easier to teach, and it is wrong about how things are.

Elijah, on Mount Carmel, had a sharp sense of this. When the prophets of Baal had shouted and cut themselves all morning and no fire had fallen, he mocked them and suggested that their god might be busy, or away travelling, or asleep (1 Kings 18:27). There is a kind of courage in laughing at a false system in public before the fire falls. It requires certainty about whose logic is actually running the universe.

## The Lamb on the Throne

The clearest picture of this upside down mind is in the last book of the Bible, and it comes as a surprise even to John.

He is weeping because no one in heaven or on earth seems worthy to open the scroll. An elder tells him to stop.

> *"And one of the elders said to me, 'Weep no more; behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has conquered, so that he can open the scroll and its seven seals.'"*
> — Revelation 5:5 (ESV)

John turns round to look at the Lion. What he sees is something else.

> *"And between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders I saw a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain, with seven horns and with seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God sent out into all the earth."*
> — Revelation 5:6 (ESV)

He hears conquest and sees a slaughtered lamb. The wounds have not been tidied away for the occasion. They are the reason he is there. Everything I have said so far lands in this picture. The ruler gets the rule by giving himself away, and the throne belongs to the one who was killed.

N. T. Wright has argued at length in *The Resurrection of the Son of God* that the resurrection did not undo the cross, it vindicated it (Wright, 2003). The cross was not a detour on the way to glory. It was the route. And the throne in Revelation 5 is not a consolation prize for suffering. It shows the cross from the far side of Easter, as what it was all along, the exercise of divine power in the form the world calls weakness. Lucifer wanted the throne without the cross. He couldn't see that the cross is why the throne matters.

Paul says it in a flatter voice in the letter to the Corinthians, and he has written the same truth out in miniature in Philippians 2:5 to 11, where the one who was in the form of God takes the form of a servant and is then exalted. The order is the point. Descent first, and then the name above every name.

Go back now to what the Thessalonian crowd feared. They were afraid that an army was coming, or a rival power with its own legions. No army came. What came was a story about a king who wins by losing, and the mob had no way of weighing it, because their scales had been built for Caesar.

## What the Church Did to the Empire

Did the story really turn anything over? The best evidence is not in the speeches. It is in what ordinary Christians were seen doing.

Tertullian, writing in North Africa around the end of the second century, describes their gatherings in his *Apology*, and he reports what outsiders said. "See," they say, "how they love one another" (Tertullian, as presented by Christian History Institute, 2017). He says the offerings were not spent on feasts. They went to feed and bury the poor, to support orphaned children and old people who could no longer leave home, and to help believers in prison or exile. Another translator renders his claim for the community in a sentence that is worth quoting exactly. "One in mind and soul, we do not hesitate to share our earthly goods with one another" (Tertullian, 1885, p. 46, as quoted by Heidelblog, 2015). He is an advocate making a case, and I would not take every sentence as a photograph. But a man who wrote this to Roman magistrates, who could check, was staking his argument on it being broadly true.

The most striking witness comes from an enemy. The emperor Julian, who tried in the 360s to revive the old religions, wrote to a pagan high priest and complained that the "impious Galileans" were feeding "not only their own poor but ours as well" (Julian, c. 362, Letter 22, in the translation at Roman Letters, n.d.). I have only quoted the clause that I could confirm. When an opponent complains that your people are doing something his own side is failing to do, you are probably not imagining it.

Rodney Stark, a sociologist, argued that the movement also changed life for women. In a 1995 paper he held that Greek and Roman society commonly exposed unwanted girl babies, that Christians forbade it, and that the church came to have more women than men, with security in marriage, widowhood and fidelity that pagan society did not offer (Stark, 1995, as summarised from the paper at UC San Diego). He also built a model of Christian growth at roughly 40 percent a decade, which became famous. Bart Ehrman, no friend of apologetics, notes that these are rough numbers meant for illustration, that the rate would have fluctuated, and that the maths does not need mass conversions or miracles (Ehrman, n.d.). I hold Stark's figures loosely for the same reason. What I take from him is the direction. A movement that valued the weak, bore the cost of caring for the sick and refused to leave babies on the roadside would look attractive in a world that did none of those things, and that is a fair description of the people Jesus formed.

By the fourth century the emperors themselves were confessing the faith of the man Rome had crucified. It did not happen because Christians had learned to play politics on Rome's terms. The Pax Romana, kept by the legions, did not survive. The church built on a cross is still meeting in cities Paul never saw.

## Two Nigerians Who Turned Things Over

It would be easy to leave this in the first century, so let me bring it closer.

Samuel Ajayi Crowther was captured as a Yoruba youth by slavers in the early 1820s, and a British antislavery cruiser freed the captives on the ship in Freetown. He was baptised in 1825, ordained in England in 1841, and in 1864 was consecrated bishop for West Africa beyond British jurisdiction, the first African bishop in the Anglican church. He translated the New Testament and the Book of Common Prayer into Yoruba and published a Yoruba dictionary in 1857 (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.). A youth seized by slavers ended up a bishop with the New Testament in Yoruba to his name. The weapon he used was translation, which put the book into a people's own tongue.

The same record is honest about what came next. Colonial era missionary opposition undermined his authority, control of finances was handed to a white associate, his mission ship was taken from him, and priests he had ordained were suspended (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.). Those who did this were also Christians, and they were reading the sentence about the rulers of the Gentiles in a way that suited them. Heaven's logic doesn't get adopted by an institution just because the institution has a cross on its building.

Mary Slessor was born in Aberdeen in 1848, arrived in West Africa in 1876 and lived for about fifteen years in Okoyong from 1888. Local belief held that twins were a curse, so infants were often left to die. She rescued hundreds of twins and adopted abandoned children, and in 1892 she became vice consul in Okoyong, where she presided over the local court (Wikipedia contributors, n.d. b). Whatever else one says about the mission era, the picture of a woman with no army walking into a community to take a child out of the bush is the opposite of a conquest. It is also very close to what Stark describes in Rome, a movement that refused to leave the vulnerable on the roadside.

Neither story is a recipe, and both have their shadows. But they show the same upside down logic doing its work on Nigerian soil.

## Where Nigerian Pulpits Stop Too Soon

There is a kind of preaching heard in Lagos, Accra, Nairobi and the Nigerian churches of Houston and London that declares, week after week, that God wants his children at the head and not the tail, above and not beneath. The language comes from Deuteronomy 28:13, and there is nothing false in the aspiration as far as it goes. The trouble is where it stops. It stops before Revelation 5, before Mark 10:44, before Proverbs 11:24 is allowed to finish its own thought.

When the passage is cut off at the point of comfort, what is left is a theology of ascent, with the language of faith painted over it. It says the goal is to climb, to be seen at the top and to set one's throne high. That is the logic of the king in Isaiah 14, now with a praise and worship soundtrack, and sold to believers as breakthrough. Nigerian Pentecostalism has also produced leaders who understood the opposite, men and women who built large congregations by pouring themselves into other people, who ran schools and universities and clinics, and who stayed poor in spirit while the buildings rose. The difference between the two groups has little to do with charisma or talent, or even with anointing in the popular sense of the word. It comes down to whether Mark 10:44 was received as a command or displayed in the church lobby.

The other side of the ledger is easy to find. Transparency International's 2025 index, released on 10 February 2026, scored Nigeria 26 out of 100, which put it 142nd of 182 countries, two places down from the year before (allAfrica, 2026). A score like that doesn't tell us who to blame, and I won't pretend it settles any argument by itself. But it is a number about the very thing Jesus described in Mark 10:42, a society where those with authority are tempted to lord it over everyone else. The answer in Mark 10:43 is not a policy. It is a kind of person, in an office, a classroom, a church and a shop, who has decided that the basin and the towel are for them.

***

## What This Asks of You

I want to say something plainly to the reader who has been nodding along. It is cheap to admire a Lamb on a throne from the safety of an article.

Think of the room where you actually hold power. It need not be large. It could be a classroom, a ministry team, a market stall, a household with four children and one set of rules. In that room, ask where you are lording it, even gently, and what Mark 10:43 to 45 would change if you took it seriously on Monday. Ask where you are holding your hand closed and calling it prudence. Ask where you have been waiting for the towel to be offered by someone else.

I'd be surprised if the answer didn't cost something. Jason and the others in Thessalonica had to put up a security payment because of the company they kept. But I'd also be surprised if it didn't turn out to be the best bargain you ever made, because the one who sits on the throne has never asked anyone to go where he hasn't already been.

If you try it, write and tell me what happened. I'd like to know what the first Monday looks like.
