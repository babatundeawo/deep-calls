## The Prayer Point That Brought the House Down

Picture a vigil. The fluorescent tubes are too bright, the plastic chairs have been defeating spines since before the choir was formed, and the leader has just announced the next prayer point. *Everywhere I have been disgraced, Lord, raise me up to become their leader.*

The room answers at once. Feet stamp. Somebody near the front is weeping, and she is not weeping for the kingdom of God. She is weeping because she has a list, the list is long, and everyone on it is about to hear about themselves. Anyone who has spent enough nights at Nigerian prayer meetings knows this scene, or one very like it. Under all that noise a small cold question is waiting for someone to ask it.

What exactly are we asking for?

It could be that we are asking for the work of God to go forward and for the people in our care to be served. It could also be that we are asking for a front row seat at the humiliation of everyone who ever doubted us. The two prayers can use identical words, and a congregation will shout them with identical volume. God, who hears what sits under the words, knows which one is being prayed.

Before going further, though, the angry person deserves a fair hearing. Most people who pray that prayer have been genuinely wronged, and most of the sermons that scold them have never been lied about in front of a whole village.

## Why the Wronged Are Allowed to Be Angry

Whoever has been slandered, sacked unfairly, cheated of an inheritance or humiliated at a family gathering doesn't need a lecture on turning the other cheek. Not first, anyway. They need to know that the Bible has room for their rage, and it does. Scripture doesn't tidy up the grief of wronged people for polite company.

Consider the psalm written by exiles beside the rivers of Babylon, people who had watched their city burn and their children die.

> *“O daughter of Babylon, doomed to be destroyed, blessed shall he be who repays you with what you have done to us! Blessed shall he be who takes your little ones and dashes them against the rock!”*
>
> — Psalm 137:8 and 9 (ESV)

Nobody reads that aloud on a Sunday morning if they can help it. Yet it sits in the Bible, uncensored, and it is there on purpose. Psalm 94 opens with the same cry in cleaner language (“O LORD, God of vengeance, O God of vengeance, shine forth!”), and the martyrs under the altar in Revelation use the same voice.

> *“O Sovereign Lord, holy and true, how long before you will judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?”*
>
> — Revelation 6:10 (ESV)

So the question is not whether Christians may want justice. They may. Erich Zenger, the German Old Testament scholar, argued in *A God of Vengeance?* that these psalms are best read as laments, the honest speech of people suffering violence, and he thought they still have a place in how believers pray. Miroslav Volf came at the same ground from the wreckage of the war in the former Yugoslavia. His argument, as I read it, is that a person can give up violence only if they believe God will deal with violence himself. A God who did not mind injustice would not be worth worshipping, and someone who gave up revenge without believing that would simply be giving up on justice. Volf's own hard remark was that the idea comes easily in a quiet suburban house and badly in a land soaked with innocent blood.

Nigerians don't need the point explained. After military rule ended, President Olusegun Obasanjo set up a panel under the retired Supreme Court justice Chukwudifu Oputa to hear what had been done to people between 1966 and 1999. The commission finished in 2002. Its report was never officially published, and it was a coalition of pressure groups, the Civil Society Forum, that released it in January 2005 (The New Humanitarian, 2005). A family that stood in the queue to testify will understand why a country gets hungry for someone, anyone, to say that the wicked will not simply win.

## The Strongest Case for Praying Against Enemies

Because the angry person deserves a fair hearing, here is the best case for the prayer in the form its defenders would accept, and it is stronger than the sermons against it usually admit.

It runs like this. The Bible does not only permit cries for judgment on the wicked, it records them as inspired prayer. Psalms 35, 55, 69, 109 and 137, among others, ask God to break the teeth of the wicked, to cut short their days, to make their children orphans. If these are the Spirit's words, then to call such prayers sub Christian is to say that the Spirit's prayer book needed editing. Nor did the apostles retire these psalms. Peter, in Acts 1, uses the imprecations of Psalms 69 and 109 about Judas, and Paul, in Romans 11, draws on Psalm 69 when he speaks of Israel's blindness. Paul himself says that anyone who preaches a different gospel should be accursed (Galatians 1:8 and 9), and he writes of Alexander the coppersmith, who had done him great harm, that the Lord will repay him according to his deeds (2 Timothy 4:14). Jesus tells a parable in which a widow wins justice from a judge who does not care, and then asks whether God, who does care, will fail to give justice to his elect who cry to him day and night (Luke 18:1 to 8). A faith that cannot say "God, deal with this man" is a faith that has stopped believing God is just.

There is a second form of the defence that carries particular weight in Nigerian churches. The prayer is not aimed at people at all. Paul says that we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against rulers, authorities and spiritual forces of evil (Ephesians 6:12). When a minister prays a prayer such as "die by fire," on this account, he is commanding the works of darkness, the stubborn problems and the opposing powers, and not a neighbour. Daniel Olukoya, the General Overseer of Mountain of Fire and Miracles Ministries, has said exactly this in a message reported in March 2026. He called the phrase a command for divine judgment against the works of darkness and said it was never meant as a call for physical harm (Sofiyullaha, 2026). That defence deserves to be taken at its word, and I will take it at its word. If the prayer really is addressed to powers and not to persons, then Paul stands behind it.

Last, there is a theological form of the defence that I find the most serious. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing about the psalms in 1940 while the Nazi state tightened round the church, argued that Christians can pray the imprecatory psalms only in Christ, because the vengeance of God that the psalmist begs for has already fallen, and it fell on the cross. On that reading the prayer for vengeance is not abandoned, it is answered, in the one place where God's judgment and his mercy meet.

I think each of these points has real force. I also think they leave a gap that the vigil prayer falls into, and the rest of this essay is about that gap. What none of them supports is the specific prayer that began this essay, in which God is asked to promote the plaintiff so that the plaintiff may deliver the sentence in person.

## What the Angry Psalms Actually Do

Look at what the psalmists do with their anger. They hand the case to God and leave the room. The cry is addressed to the judge, and the sentence is left in his hands. A psalmist who prays for God to break a wicked man's arm does not ask for a promotion so that he can do it himself. He asks God to act, and he is not in the picture when the verdict comes.

Some interpreters add that the harshest curse in Psalm 109, in verses 6 to 19, may be the words of the psalmist's accusers quoted back to God, since the Hebrew has no quotation marks and the psalm switches to the singular without warning. That reading is debated, and I would not rest anything on it. The more secure observation is simpler. In the angry psalms, the anger goes up. It is carried to God and set down there. Zenger's point was that this is what makes them prayers and not plots.

C. S. Lewis, in his *Reflections on the Psalms*, found the cursing psalms hard to stomach and said so honestly, and he thought Christians ought to treat them as showing the ugliness of sin and not as models to copy. You need not agree with all of him to see the sense in the instinct. The curses show us something about what injustice does to people, and they invite us to bring that same honesty to God. They do not authorise a private programme of revenge.

The vigil prayer, by contrast, is addressed to God but aimed at an audience. It asks him to arrange a particular future, in which the people who shamed me will see me elevated and know what they missed. That is a request about a scene, with them in it and me at the centre.

The scenes of the Old Testament will show how different that is from the lives the Bible actually praises.

## A Boy Left Out With the Sheep

When Samuel came to Jesse's house to anoint a king, the family lined up seven sons, each one looking more like a king than the last. Samuel looked at Eliab, the tall one, and thought he had found the man. God's word to him that day is worth keeping close.

> *“For the LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.”*
>
> — 1 Samuel 16:7 (ESV)

Seven sons passed, and Samuel asked whether these were all the young men Jesse had. Jesse's answer is one of the quietest sentences in Scripture: "There remains yet the youngest, but behold, he is keeping the sheep" (1 Samuel 16:11, ESV). A father had held a family parade for a prophet and left his youngest son out in the field. You can read a faint tone of dismissal in it, though a translator should be careful with tone, and the text itself says only that the boy was working while the others were summoned. David was forgotten before he was famous. His own family had written the footnote on him before God wrote the title.

What people rarely notice is what David did next. The narrative shows him anointed in front of his brothers, and then it shows him going on with ordinary work. When he later talked himself into a fight with Goliath, the credentials he offered Saul were a lion and a bear he had dealt with while minding his father's sheep (1 Samuel 17:34 to 37). Nobody had clapped for any of it. The road to the throne was long, and Saul spent a good deal of it trying to kill him. Twice David had Saul at his mercy and walked away (1 Samuel 24 and 26). Nothing in the record suggests he spent those years rehearsing how Eliab would look when he returned in glory.

Picture, as a scene, a young man in Ibadan whose uncle gave away his school fees money, and who watched cousins he had once helped pass him at a wedding without a greeting. Years later he becomes a person of some standing, and the first thing he notices is how often he checks who in the hall has seen him arrive. That checking is the thing to be afraid of. It doesn't need an enemy to be present. It needs only a memory and a room.

## Joseph in the Pit and in the Palace

Joseph is the man everyone reaches for, and the story is hard enough to bear the weight. He was seventeen when his brothers threw him into a pit at Dothan, and Genesis does not spare us the picture of them sitting down to eat with him in it. "The pit was empty; there was no water in it" (Genesis 37:24, ESV). Slavery followed in the house of Potiphar, then a prison term he did not earn because a powerful man's wife lied, then two full years in which a cupbearer he had helped simply forgot him. If any man in the Bible had earned a revenge fantasy, it was Joseph.

When Pharaoh needed his dream explained and Joseph was at last brought up from the cell, he did not say, *watch me*. He said, "It is not in me; God will give Pharaoh a favorable answer" (Genesis 41:16, ESV). A man with every reason to make the moment about himself made it about someone else.

The elevation that followed was not a monument. The famine ran for seven years, and the grain stored in the good years fed Egypt and the families around it. Genesis 41:29 to 31 is plain that the lean years were seven. And the end of the story, when his brothers were frightened and entirely in his power, is the strangest part.

> *“Do not fear, for am I in the place of God? As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today.”*
>
> — Genesis 50:19 and 20 (ESV)

Am I in the place of God? Joseph never claims the evil was small, and he never tells his brothers it was nothing. He names it. *You meant evil against me.* What he refuses is the judge's chair. Joseph was raised so that people could live, and the brothers' humiliation was a by product that he declined to enjoy. Notice, too, that he did not forgive in a single stroke. In Genesis 42 to 45 he tests his brothers, hides his identity, and watches to see whether they would sell another favoured brother. Forgiveness in his case was slow, cautious, and finally tearful. A person who wants to forgive and still feels the sting is in better company than most sermons admit.

## A King Who Let Shimei Curse

David's later life tests the pattern more sharply. When he fled Jerusalem from his son Absalom, a man of Saul's clan named Shimei followed him along the hillside, throwing stones and dirt and shouting that David was a man of blood. Abishai, David's nephew, offered to take off his head. David refused. The reason he gave is remarkable (2 Samuel 16:5 to 13): perhaps the Lord had told him to curse, and perhaps, he said, the Lord would look on the wrong done to him and repay him with good for the cursing of that day. David did not deny that he was being wronged, and he did not deny that Shimei was wrong. He left the verdict to God.

I should be honest that this story does not end tidily. When David came back as victor, Shimei begged for mercy and David swore he would not die (2 Samuel 19:18 to 23). Yet on his deathbed, in 1 Kings 2:8 and 9, David told Solomon not to hold Shimei guiltless, and Solomon later confined him and executed him when he broke the terms. David's mercy was real and also partial. In 2 Samuel 21, after a three year famine, he handed seven of Saul's descendants over to the Gibeonites, who hanged them, while sparing Mephibosheth because of his oath to Jonathan. David was a political animal in a violent age. He makes a poor hero for a tidy story about revenge refined into mercy, which is part of why the New Testament doesn't leave the matter with him.

## The Table Laid in Front of Enemies

Here is the verse that most vigils reach for when the prayer point gets going, and it deserves a slower reading than it usually gets.

> *“You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; you anoint my head with oil; my cup overflows.”*
>
> — Psalm 23:5 (ESV)

The popular reading runs like this. God will seat me at a banquet, my enemies will be made to stand at the wall and watch me eat, and they will be sorry. It is a satisfying picture, and the verse does not draw it. The enemies are present, and they are not removed. They are not described. They don't weep, they don't apologise, and nobody confesses to having been wrong about you. All the attention of the verse falls on the host, the table, the oil and the cup. The psalm is about being fed and kept safe while the people who wanted you gone are still in the room.

Another scene in David's life shows what that table looked like from the other side. In 2 Samuel 17 David himself is the man in flight, hungry and worn out, with Absalom's rebellion behind him. Three men meet him at Mahanaim with beds, basins, wheat, barley, lentils, honey and cheese, because, as the text says, the people are hungry and weary and thirsty in the wilderness. One of the three is Machir, son of Ammiel, from Lo Debar.

That name should sound familiar. Lo Debar is where, some chapters earlier, Jonathan's crippled son Mephibosheth was found living in Machir's house. When David became king, with the house of Saul no longer a danger to anyone, he asked a question that nobody in his court would have expected.

> *“Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may show him kindness for Jonathan's sake?”*
>
> — 2 Samuel 9:1 (ESV)

The king did not ask whom he might settle accounts with. And Mephibosheth, a man who had every reason to expect a knife, was given a seat at the king's table. A rival house had a way of disappearing when a new dynasty settled in, and David's advisers would have understood. He chose otherwise, and the household that took in the lame prince in his hiding place later fed the king when the king was the hungry one. Whatever else you make of that, it is hard to read it as a story about winning an argument.

## Esther and the Man on the Horse

If anyone has a text for the vigil prayer, it is Esther, and I do not want to avoid it. The book is the nearest the Bible comes to a story of public reversal, and it enjoys the reversal. Haman, the king's second in command and the enemy of the Jews, plans a gallows for Mordecai and ends up leading Mordecai through the city square on the king's horse, "proclaiming before him, 'Thus shall it be done to the man whom the king delights to honor'" (Esther 6:11, ESV). The man who wanted his enemy hanged is made to carry his enemy's glory, in public, with a crowd watching. The gallows is then used on Haman himself (Esther 7:10). If you want to say that God sometimes does flip the room, Esther is your witness.

Three things, though, belong in the account. First, Haman's offence was a plan to destroy a whole people on a single day, so the reversal belongs to a defence against genocide and not to a quarrel over who sat where at a wedding. When the Jews fought back on the thirteenth of Adar, the text takes care to say three times that they did not lay hands on the plunder (Esther 9:10, 15 and 16). Even with the sword in their hands they refused to profit. Second, Esther did not stage the reversal. She fasted, and risked her life by going to the king unsummoned, and the moment that humbled Haman came through an insomniac king reading the chronicles. Third, look at what the elevation was for. Mordecai became second in rank to the king, and the book closes by saying he was great among the Jews and sought the welfare of his people and spoke peace to all of them (Esther 10:3). The promotion fed and protected people. It did not crown a grievance.

So the strongest text in favour of public reversal makes the same point as Joseph's story. God does sometimes turn the tables. The tables that matter are the ones set for the vulnerable.

## Sarah's Laughter and What It Does Not Prove

Sarah deserves a place here, with a warning that her story is easy to over read. At ninety, after decades of barrenness, the detour through Hagar, and the humiliation of twice being passed off as Abraham's sister to a foreign king (Genesis 12 and 20), she bore Isaac and said something that has been preached often.

> *“God has made laughter for me; everyone who hears will laugh over me.”*
>
> — Genesis 21:6 (ESV)

Many sermons have lingered on the small word. Everyone who hears will laugh *with* me, we say, not *at* me, and then we praise Sarah for refusing to turn her joy into a verdict on the people who doubted her. The difficulty is that the Hebrew preposition is ambiguous. The ESV renders it "over me," and other translations, such as the NIV and the NRSV, say "with me." The same verse also puns on Isaac's name, which means *he laughs*. So I would not build a doctrine on one syllable.

What Sarah's story does support is smaller and still worth having. After all those years she did not use the birth of her son as a weapon. Her next words (Genesis 21:7) are an astonished question about who would ever have told Abraham that Sarah would nurse children. She was amazed, and nothing in the text shows her using the moment to score. A person whose joy no longer depends on who is watching and how impressed they are finds, I think, that the joy gets bigger and fills the room.

## What Jesus and Paul Ask of Us

What the New Testament says is simple enough, and it isn't gentle about the part that costs us. Proverbs had already told us not to say *I will repay evil*, and to wait for the LORD. Paul takes the ancient line and gives it a purpose.

> *“Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave it to the wrath of God, for it is written, ‘Vengeance is mine, I will repay, says the Lord.’”*
>
> — Romans 12:19 (ESV)

Paul doesn't say the vengeance will never come. He says it isn't ours to carry, and a few lines later he finishes the thought with a sentence every wronged person should keep somewhere visible: “Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good” (Romans 12:21, ESV). Jesus pushes in the same direction.

> *“But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,”*
>
> — Matthew 5:44 (ESV)

And at the end, from the cross, he does it: “Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do” (Luke 23:34, ESV). Stephen follows him in Acts 7:60, praying for the men throwing the stones.

The strongest case from earlier in this essay is not dismissed by that. It is completed. Romans 12 does not tell the wronged to stop wanting justice. It tells them where to send the wanting. Bonhoeffer was right that the vengeance of God has a place where it has already been spent, and Paul's own reason for leaving room for God's wrath is that God will not leave sin unanswered. The believer who says "vengeance is yours" has not dropped the claim for justice. She has handed it to the one who can carry it, and in doing so she has become free to love the person on the list.

That is a hard word for anyone with an honest grievance, and I don't want to soften it. Nothing in it says the injustice was imaginary, that the wicked will never face God, or that you must feel warm toward a person who hasn't repented. What changes is the prayer. Say the angry thing to God in full, in the words of the psalms if you need them, with the name attached. Then leave the sentencing where Joseph left it. The judge isn't you.

There is a practical cost to the other kind of prayer, too. Anyone who spends the waiting years watching the door for enemies to arrive at their feet isn't doing the work in front of them. The obsession becomes its own small prison. A position built on the need to prove something to somebody is a performance, which means it needs an audience, and audiences get bored. One day the crowd that was supposed to be so impressed stops looking, and the person on the platform finds that the whole thing was a mirror.

## A Different Prayer for the Next Vigil

There is a verse the vigil leaders like to reach for at the end of a night, and it too needs its context. "Remember not the former things, nor consider the things of old. Behold, I am doing a new thing; now it springs forth, do you not perceive it? I will make a way in the wilderness and rivers in the desert" (Isaiah 43:18 and 19, ESV). Many sermons hear in it a command to forget your personal wounds. Isaiah is speaking to exiles about the God who brought Israel out of Egypt and will now bring them home from Babylon, so the verse is firstly about looking forward to a new act of rescue. It does not tell a hurt person to wipe their memory. It tells a community that God's best work is ahead of it, and that is a fair word to bring to anyone who has spent years watching the faces of the people who put them in the wilderness.

So ask for the elevation, if you want it, and ask for the right reason. Joseph's promotion fed a region. Mordecai's protected a people. David's reached a crippled man in a hiding place. None of them was raised so that someone else could be made smaller, and the prayer worth praying at the next vigil asks for the same shape of future. *Raise me so that they can be fed.*

When the prayer point comes round again and the room begins to stamp, you will not need to stop anyone. Take the name from your own list, the one that really hurts, and say it to God as plainly as the exiles did by the river. Then ask him what a table would look like if you were the one who got to lay it. You may find the answer surprises you, and I suspect it will take longer than one vigil to learn.
