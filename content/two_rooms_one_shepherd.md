# Two Rooms, One Shepherd: Can a Believer Lose Salvation?

***

## Two Rooms and One Question

Picture a Sunday morning in Calabar. The service is unhurried. A choir sings, an elder reads, and the minister preaches on a God who does not drop what he has picked up. Afterwards, over rice and stew in the churchyard, a woman who buried her husband last year says, with the plain calm of someone reporting the weather, "I am not worried. He has me."

Now picture a Friday night vigil on the edge of Ibadan. It is past midnight. A young man kneels on a bare floor, and he is not praying for a visa or a job. He is praying for his own endurance. He has watched friends who once sang louder than anyone drift away, and he does not intend to become the next story told in a lowered voice. Nobody in that hall is careless about grace. They simply believe that a person who has been rescued can still walk off, and they would very much like not to.

Both rooms love Jesus. Both open the same Bible. Both have buried the faithful and prayed for the wandering. A visitor who passed from the first room to the second in a single weekend could be forgiven for asking whether the two congregations follow the same gospel.

They do. The harder question is why they sound so different when they speak about the end of the road.

## What Both Rooms Believe Before They Disagree

Start where the argument has not yet started. Salvation, in its plainest sense, is rescue: God lifting a person out of sin and its consequences and bringing them into a restored relationship with himself, now and for ever. Grace is the name for how it happens: favour that nobody earned. Paul puts it in a sentence that both rooms could recite in their sleep.

> *"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."*
>
> — Ephesians 2:8 to 9 (ESV)

Neither the woman in Calabar nor the young man in Ibadan thinks she or he earned a place in God's family. The quarrel begins one step later, and it is a narrow quarrel with a long history. Once God has saved someone, can that person lose what was given?

Sincere believers have answered in two ways, and the answers carry names. The first is often called eternal security, or Calvinism, after John Calvin, a leader of the Reformation in the sixteenth century. The second is often called conditional security, or Arminianism, after Jacobus Arminius, a Dutch teacher who died in 1609. Names are handy. They are also a little like uniforms: they tell you which team a person stands with, and almost nothing about what that person is like at home.

## The Room Where the Hand Holds

Give the first room its best case, because it is a strong one. A defender of eternal security would say that salvation begins with God, so it cannot end with us. If the rescue was his work from first to last, then it is not hostage to our weakest Tuesday. The believer is not a rope held up by their own grip; the believer is a child carried by a father who has never once misplaced his keys, let alone his children.

The passages they love are among the most beautiful in the New Testament. Paul, having listed every threat he could think of, closes the list with a flourish.

> *"For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
>
> — Romans 8:38 to 39 (ESV)

And Jesus himself, speaking of his own sheep, gives a promise with two hands in it.

> *"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand."*
>
> — John 10:27 to 29 (ESV)

Notice what the slogan "once saved, always saved" does to that teaching, and what it does not. The slogan is short enough to print on a mug, which is perhaps why it travels better than it explains. Those who hold this view usually prefer a longer name, the perseverance of the saints, because it carries two ideas at once: believers are kept by God, and therefore believers keep going. A person who coasts into careless living has, on this reading, offered evidence against their own claim to have been saved. Security here is never a licence. It is the ground on which obedience stands.

This tradition is not distant from Nigerian soil. The Presbyterian Church of Nigeria, a church of the Reformed tradition, dates its beginning to 10 April 1846, when Scottish missionaries led by Hope Masterton Waddell arrived in Calabar at the invitation of the local ruler. The woman in the churchyard, whether or not she has ever heard the name of Calvin, belongs to a family that has spoken this comfort over Calabar for almost two centuries.

The concern that others raise is real and should be stated honestly. Some worry that this teaching, in careless hands, can make people casual about holy living, and that it sits uneasily beside the Bible's many warnings about falling away. Hold that thought. It is about to walk into the second room.

## The Room Where the Voice Warns

Now give the second room its best case, because it is also a strong one. A defender of conditional security would point out that the New Testament is full of letters written to believers who are told to continue, to watch, to hold fast, and not to harden their hearts. A writer who warns a person about a cliff edge is not insulting the person. He is loving them in the only language a cliff understands.

The sharpest of those warnings sits in the letter to the Hebrews.

> *"For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt."*
>
> — Hebrews 6:4 to 6 (ESV)

Read slowly, that passage is not describing a bystander. It describes people who tasted, shared and were enlightened. If that language can describe someone who never truly belonged, the second room asks, then what words would the writer have used for someone who did?

This view takes its name from Arminius, and his followers carried it forward. In 1610 they presented a Remonstrance in five articles to the authorities of the Netherlands, and the dispute that followed ended at the Synod of Dort, which met from November 1618 to May 1619. The synod rejected their position and framed five canons in reply. John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, later championed the Arminian emphasis in the eighteenth century, with a particular stress on holy living and on the seriousness of the warnings.

That emphasis reached Nigeria early. The Methodist missionary Thomas Birch Freeman landed at Badagry on 24 September 1842, with William de Graft and his wife, and became the first Methodist missionary to arrive there. Freeman was the son of an African father and an English mother, which is a useful reminder that this was never a story of Europe handing something down to Africa and nothing more. Many Holiness and Pentecostal churches since then have kept a similar accent on watchfulness, even if their members have never once heard the word Arminianism. Not all have; some Pentecostal bodies teach eternal security with equal conviction. Anyone who has attended a vigil in this country knows the sound of a congregation that takes warnings seriously.

The concern that others raise here is also real. This teaching, in anxious hands, can leave a believer checking their own pulse every morning to see whether they are still in. Critics fear that it moves the weight of salvation from the giver to the receiver. Those who hold the view answer that staying saved is still by grace, received through faith, and never a wage for effort. The woman in Calabar and the young man in Ibadan would both say amen to that last sentence.

## The Verse Both Rooms Quietly Borrow

Here is where an article of this kind is expected to name a winner. Hold the verdict for a moment, because there is a verse that both rooms reach for, and each room thinks it belongs to them.

> *"They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. But they went out, that it might become plain that they all are not of us."*
>
> — 1 John 2:19 (ESV)

The first room hears a reassurance: those who walk away never truly belonged, so the genuine are safe. The second room hears an observation of real departures from a real fellowship, and asks what exactly "of us" is supposed to mean. Honest readers on both sides know this passage does not end the debate; it only moves the debate one inch to the left.

And that is the moment the floor shifts. We have all been trained, by the internet if by nothing else, to treat the Bible as two lawyers' briefs in the same case. One lawyer cites the promises, the other cites the warnings, and the jury of onlookers is invited to vote. Yet the biblical authors do not write like opposing counsel. They place promise and warning on the same page, sometimes in the same breath, and they seem entirely untroubled by the arrangement.

Look again at the writer of Hebrews. Having delivered the severest warning in the letter, he turns almost at once to the people he is writing to and says that, though he speaks this way, he is sure of better things in their case, things that belong to salvation. The warning was never meant to be the last word. It is a fence on a hillside, built for the climbers, and nobody builds a fence on a hillside to tell the climbers they are doomed.

Look again, too, at the words of Jesus in John 10. In a single sentence the sheep hear, the sheep follow, and the sheep are held. Following and being held sit side by side in the same verse. The two rooms do differ, and they differ on a serious point: which of those verbs carries the weight, and what happens to a sheep that stops following. That is a real question and good people answer it differently. It is worth noticing, though, that neither room is arguing about whether the shepherd is able, or whether the sheep are loved. Both rooms are arguing about how a loved flock is kept.

## What We All Agree On

It is no small thing that two traditions so far apart can still say four things together.

Salvation is a gift of grace, not a prize anyone earns. Jesus is fully able to save. We are called to keep trusting him and to walk closely with him. And a person who loves Jesus and wants to follow him has no reason to live in fear.

Nothing in that list requires anyone to pretend the disagreement away. A Presbyterian elder in Calabar and a Methodist steward in Badagry can still disagree about Hebrews 6 over a plate of rice and part as brothers. That has been the pattern for four centuries, and the church has survived it rather better than the internet has.

## To the Afraid and to the Careless

To the reader who lies awake wondering whether you are still in: the dread of drifting is rarely the sound of drifting. People who are leaving Christ do not usually spend their midnights terrified of it. Take that fear to the Shepherd, and take it to a pastor who knows you by name. Read Romans 8 aloud, slowly, as though someone were reading it to you at a bedside, because someone is.

To the reader who has grown comfortable, who hears "once saved, always saved" as a permission slip: the warnings are addressed to you as well, and no tradition in this debate teaches that Christ may be treated lightly. A grace that cost the Son of God his life does not ask for a casual reply.

Whichever room you were raised in, the invitation is the same, and it was never an invitation to win an argument. Read these passages for yourself. Ask the Holy Spirit to open them. Bring your questions to your pastor or teacher instead of carrying them alone. And come to the one whose hand is spoken of in John 10, the one who calls his own by name and walks ahead of them. He has not asked you to understand the whole of how he keeps his people. He has only asked you to hear his voice, and to follow.
