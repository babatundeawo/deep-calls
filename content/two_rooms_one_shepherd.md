## Two Rooms and One Question

Picture a Sunday morning in Calabar. The service is unhurried. A choir sings, an elder reads, and the minister preaches on a God who doesn't drop what he has picked up. Afterwards, over rice and stew in the churchyard, a woman who buried her husband last year says, with the plain calm of someone reporting the weather, "I'm not worried. He has me."

Now picture a Friday night vigil on the edge of Ibadan. It is past midnight. A young man kneels on a bare floor, and he isn't praying for a visa or a job. He is praying for his own endurance. He has watched friends who once sang louder than anyone drift away, and he has no wish to become the next story told in a lowered voice. Nobody in that hall is careless about grace. They simply believe that a person who has been rescued can still walk off, and they would very much like not to.

Both rooms love Jesus. Both open the same Bible. Both have buried the faithful and prayed for the wandering. A visitor who went from the first room to the second in a single weekend could be forgiven for asking whether the two congregations follow the same gospel.

They do. The harder question is why they sound so different when they talk about the end of the road.

## What Both Rooms Believe First

Start before the argument starts. Salvation, in its plainest sense, is rescue: God lifting a person out of sin and its consequences and bringing them into friendship with himself, now and for ever. Grace is the name for how it happens, favour that nobody earned. Paul puts it in a sentence both rooms could recite in their sleep.

> *"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."*
>
> — Ephesians 2:8 to 9 (ESV)

Neither the woman in Calabar nor the young man in Ibadan thinks they earned a place in God's family. The quarrel begins one step later, and it is a narrow quarrel with a long history. Once God has saved someone, can that person lose what was given? And if the answer is no, the warnings in the Bible need explaining.

Sincere believers have answered in several ways, and the answers carry names. The Reformed answer, after John Calvin, the sixteenth century reformer, is called the perseverance of the saints. The Arminian answer takes its name from Jacobus Arminius, a Dutch teacher who died in 1609. The Catholic, Orthodox and Lutheran churches each have an answer older or sturdier than either label, and the Pentecostal answer is the one most Nigerian readers grew up beside. Names are handy. They are also a little like football shirts. They tell you which team a person supports and almost nothing about what he is like at home.

So this essay does something unfashionable. It lets each tradition speak in words its own people would accept, then takes the hard passages one at a time, then asks what a frightened or a careless person should actually do on Monday morning.

## The Reformed Position in Full

Give the first room its best case, because it is a strong one, and because it is often caricatured. A Reformed believer would say that salvation begins with God, so it can't end with us. If the rescue was his work from first to last, it isn't hostage to our weakest Tuesday. The believer is no rope held up by its own grip. The believer is a child carried by a father who has never misplaced his keys, let alone his children.

The technical name needs spelling out. Perseverance of the saints means that those whom God has genuinely called and renewed will certainly continue in faith to the end, and will certainly be saved. "Saints" here carries its New Testament sense of all God's people, not a shortlist of the extra holy. The doctrine is the fifth of the five points that English speaking Reformed people summarise with the acrostic TULIP: total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, and perseverance of the saints. The acrostic is a modern teaching aid and Calvin never saw it. The five points themselves trace back to the Synod of Dort, which met from November 1618 to May 1619 in the Netherlands and answered the five articles of the Remonstrants, the followers of Arminius. The synod's reply, the Canons of Dort, has its own fifth head of doctrine on this very subject.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, written by the Westminster Assembly in the 1640s and the doctrinal standard of Presbyterian churches the world over, gives it a whole chapter, the seventeenth, titled Of the Perseverance of the Saints. Its first section says that those whom God has accepted in Christ, effectually called and sanctified by his Spirit, "can neither totally nor finally fall away from the state of grace." Read the adverbs slowly. The Confession is not saying a Christian cannot fall at all. Its third section says plainly that believers can fall into grievous sins, remain in them for a time, grieve the Spirit, lose some of their comforts, harden their hearts, wound their consciences and bring judgments on themselves. What it denies is a total and final fall. The second section says why: perseverance does not depend on the believer's own free will. It rests on the unchangeable decree of election, on the merit and intercession of Jesus Christ, on the abiding of the Spirit and of God's seed within the believer, and on the nature of the covenant of grace.

The Canons of Dort say the same thing in their own way, and with more tenderness than their reputation allows. They are frank that believers still carry sin. Because of the remnants of sin and the temptations of the world and Satan, the converted could never stand by their own strength. But "God is faithful, mercifully strengthening them in the grace once conferred on them" and preserving them to the end (Fifth Main Point, Article 3). Even when they fall grievously, God "does not take his Holy Spirit from his own completely" (Article 6). The ground is never the believer's grip. "His plan cannot be changed, his promise cannot fail" (Article 8). And the Canons add something that critics tend to skip. Assurance is possible. Believers "can and do become assured in accordance with the measure of their faith" (Article 9), and that assurance, far from making them proud, "is rather the true root of humility, of childlike respect, of genuine godliness" (Article 12). Article 11 also admits that believers struggle with doubts in this life, and that God revives their assurance. The Canons were not written to give the careless a pillow. They were written to give the trembling a floor.

The passages this tradition loves are among the most beautiful in the New Testament. Paul, having listed every threat he could think of, closes the list with a flourish.

> *"For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
>
> — Romans 8:38 to 39 (ESV)

And Jesus, speaking of his own sheep, gives a promise with two hands in it.

> *"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand."*
>
> — John 10:27 to 29 (ESV)

The slogan "once saved, always saved" is short enough to print on a mug, which is perhaps why it travels better than it explains. Reformed teachers usually dislike it, and they would be the first to tell you so. Their own phrase carries two ideas at once: God keeps his people, and therefore his people keep going. A person who coasts into careless living has, on this reading, given evidence against their own claim to have been saved. Security here is never a licence. It is the floor that obedience stands on.

This tradition isn't distant from Nigerian soil. The Presbyterian Church of Nigeria traces its beginning to 10 April 1846, when Scottish missionaries led by Hope Masterton Waddell arrived in Calabar at the invitation of the local ruler. In 2026 that church is 180 years old, and the Church of Scotland has marked the anniversary. The woman in the churchyard, whether or not she has ever heard the name of Calvin, belongs to a family that has spoken this comfort over Calabar for nearly two centuries.

The worry others raise is fair and should be stated honestly. Some fear that this teaching, in careless hands, makes people casual about holy living, and that it sits uneasily beside the Bible's many warnings about falling away. Hold that thought. It is about to walk into the second room.

## The Arminian Position and Its Roots

Now give the second room its best case, because it is also a strong one. An Arminian would point out that the New Testament is full of letters written to believers who are told to continue, to watch, to hold fast and not to harden their hearts. A writer who warns a person about a cliff edge isn't insulting him. He is loving him in the only language a cliff understands.

The history needs getting right, because Arminius himself was more cautious than his later reputation. Jacobus Arminius, a Dutch pastor and professor at Leiden, died in 1609. A year later his followers presented a Remonstrance of five articles to the authorities of Holland, which is why they are called the Remonstrants. Its fifth article is careful to the point of hesitation. It affirms that believers have the help of Christ's Spirit in every temptation, and then says that "it has not yet been proved from the Scriptures that grace, once given, can never be lost." In other words, the founders asked for further study. Their followers did not wait long. Within a few years the Remonstrant party was teaching plainly that a genuine believer could fall totally and finally from grace. The Synod of Dort rejected the five articles and answered with the Canons described above.

The great champion of the view in the English speaking world was John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, in the eighteenth century. In his treatise on perseverance he argued that a true believer, one holy, believing and in covenant with God, can fall away completely and be lost for ever. He appealed to Ezekiel 18, to Hebrews 6 and 10, to 2 Peter 2, to Romans 11 and to John 15. He answered the promise passages by saying that they carry an implied condition, continued faith. And he ended the treatise with a warning drawn from Paul: "Therefore let him that standeth take heed lest he fall." Yet Wesley was no friend of anxiety. He taught that believers can know their sins are forgiven by the witness of the Spirit. His complaint was never against assurance. It was against the idea that assurance could outlive faith.

Put in the form an Arminian would accept, the claim has three parts. God's grace is prior to everything, and nobody comes to faith without it. Faith is a continuing trust and not a single moment. And a person who has genuinely believed can, by persistent and deliberate unbelief, abandon Christ, and God does not force anyone to stay. Most Arminians would add at once that a wanderer can be restored by repentance, and that the fear of drifting is itself a sign that the Spirit is still at work.

The sharpest warning in the New Testament, on this view, sits in the letter to the Hebrews.

> *"For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt."*
>
> — Hebrews 6:4 to 6 (ESV)

Read slowly, that passage isn't describing a bystander. It describes people who were enlightened, who tasted, who shared. If that language can describe someone who never truly belonged, the second room asks, what words would the writer have used for someone who did?

That emphasis reached Nigeria early. The Methodist missionary Thomas Birch Freeman arrived at Badagry on 24 September 1842, with William de Graft and his wife, and was the first Methodist missionary to reach the town. His father was an African freeman named Thomas, and his mother was an Englishwoman, which is a fair reminder that this was never a story of Europe handing something to Africa and nothing more.

The worry others raise here is real too. This teaching, in anxious hands, can leave a believer checking their pulse every morning to see whether they're still in. Critics fear it moves the weight of salvation from the giver to the receiver. Those who hold the view answer that staying saved is still by grace, received through faith, and never a wage for effort. The woman in Calabar and the young man in Ibadan would both say amen to that last sentence.

## How the Older Churches Answer

Most online arguments about this question are conducted as though only two parties existed. Three much older families of churches have their own answers, and each deserves its own words.

The Catholic Church teaches that faith is a free gift, and that it can be lost. The Catechism, in paragraph 162, says so directly: "We can lose this priceless gift," and it cites Paul's warning to Timothy about those who have suffered shipwreck in regard to their faith. In the Catholic account, grace is lost by mortal sin and restored through repentance and the sacrament of penance. The Council of Trent, in its decree on justification in 1547, drew two lines at once. It condemned anyone who says a justified person can sin no more or cannot lose grace (Canon 23). And it condemned anyone who claims, without a special revelation, absolute and infallible certainty of the great gift of perseverance to the end (Canon 16). So the Catholic Church rejects both the Reformed doctrine that the elect cannot finally fall and the idea that a believer can be certain of final perseverance by ordinary means. It teaches instead a firm hope, resting on God's promises, together with real vigilance. Reformed Christians read Trent as taking assurance away from ordinary believers, and Catholics reply that they are guarding against presumption. Neither side is silly.

The Orthodox Church speaks of salvation as a path rather than a single event, and of the relation between God and the believer as synergy, a word that means working together. Orthodox writers insist that grace is God's free gift and that good works can never earn it, but they hold that grace invites and never compels, so that a person can, through his own fault and neglect, turn away and be lost. One Orthodox article puts the teaching with a saying of John Chrysostom, that grace saves those who desire it and not those who turn away from it. The Orthodox, like the Catholics, tend to be uneasy about calling a person saved in the past tense. They speak of being saved, of the path still being walked, and of the mercy of God at the end of it.

The Lutheran churches sit in an interesting middle. Lutherans have always insisted that salvation is by grace alone through faith alone, and Article XII of the Augsburg Confession of 1530 condemns the Anabaptists "who deny that those once justified can lose the Holy Ghost." So Lutherans teach that a person can fall from faith. A Lutheran statement on the Formula of Concord says it "does teach that people can lose salvation by falling away from the Christian faith," and records Luther's own sober remark that in temptation "people fall away in droves." Yet the same Confession comforts those who fall: it holds that there is remission of sins "whenever they are converted," and it condemns the Novatians, an ancient sect that refused to restore those who had fallen after baptism. Lutherans also hold that God chooses his elect in Christ and keeps them to the end, and they decline to explain why some believe and others fall, saying only that the Scriptures are silent on this and that the believer should look to Christ in the Word and sacraments and not into the hidden counsels of God. Lutherans, in short, keep both the warning and the comfort, and refuse to resolve them by logic.

## Where Pentecostals Stand

Most Nigerian readers will have met this question in a Pentecostal hall, so it is worth saying plainly what that tradition holds. The Assemblies of God, one of the largest Pentecostal bodies in the world, adopted a position paper on the assurance of salvation in 2017. It declines the unconditional security view and teaches that a believer who has accepted Christ can be lost if he persistently rejects Scripture and the Spirit's conviction. In the same paper, assurance is a gift of the Holy Spirit, given at new birth, and a believer who trusts Christ and confesses sin has no need to live in doubt. Pentecostals would say, in their own words, that they hold both a warning and a comfort, and that the Spirit who warns is the same Spirit who steadies.

That is close to the Arminian shape, though Pentecostals would not thank you for filing them under a Dutchman's name. Their stress falls on the present work of the Holy Spirit, on holiness as the visible evidence of new birth, and on the daily seriousness of the walk. Not every Pentecostal body answers the question the same way, and I can't speak for every church in Nigeria; a writer who tried would be guessing. What anyone who has attended a vigil here can say is that the sound of a congregation taking warnings seriously and praying for endurance is the sound of this tradition at work.

## Reading the Warning Passages in Context

Every tradition above has to deal with the same short list of hard texts. Take them one at a time, because a verse quoted without its neighbours can be made to say almost anything.

Hebrews 6:4 to 6 is the text already quoted. Notice what the writer does next. He turns to a picture from farming: land that drinks the rain and produces a useful crop receives blessing, while land that bears thorns is worthless and near to being cursed. Then, in verse 9, he says that although he speaks in this way, he is sure of better things in their case, things that belong to salvation. Reformed readers take the warning as describing people who share in the church's privileges and even in the Spirit's gifts without ever being truly born again, as the thorny ground shows by its fruit. Arminians take the people described as genuine believers, and the warning as a real barrier. A third reading, held by some on both sides, treats the warning as a hypothetical meant to frighten the readers back from a danger that God, in fact, prevents them from reaching, by means of the warning itself. All three readings agree that the passage is meant to rouse the readers and not to doom them.

Hebrews 10:26 to 31 is of the same family. It begins "For if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth," and goes on to say that no sacrifice for sins remains, only a fearful expectation of judgment, and that anyone who tramples the Son of God underfoot, counts the blood of the covenant as unholy and insults the Spirit of grace deserves a worse punishment than death under Moses' law. The writer says in verse 29 that the offender was sanctified by the blood of the covenant, and that is the Arminian's strongest card, since it seems to describe a person who really was set apart. The Reformed reply that sanctified can mean set apart by outward covenant membership, as Israel was, and point to the writer's own reassurance in verse 39, that his readers are not people who shrink back and are destroyed but people who have faith and keep their souls. Notice that both readings accept that apostasy, a deliberate and settled abandonment of Christ, is a real and terrible thing. They argue about who is capable of it.

John 15:1 to 6 is the vine and the branches. Jesus says, "I am the vine; you are the branches," and warns that anyone who does not abide in him "is thrown away like a branch and withers," and is gathered and burned. The Arminian hears a true branch in Christ that is cut off. The Reformed hear a branch that was joined to the vine in appearance, that is, a professing disciple like Judas, never bearing the life of the vine. The text itself says only that fruitless branches are removed and that fruit is the evidence of abiding. Both sides agree that Jesus commands abiding and that abiding is not optional.

Romans 11:17 to 22 is addressed to Gentile Christians, who are told they are wild olive branches grafted into Israel's tree. "Note then the kindness and the severity of God," says Paul, and he warns that they too will be cut off if they do not continue in his kindness. The Arminian reads a real warning to real believers. The Reformed point out that the chapter is mainly about the corporate standing of Gentiles and Jews as peoples in God's plan, and that Paul closes the whole discussion in the same chapter with a doxology about the depth of God's wisdom, because the plan will not fail.

First Corinthians 9:27 has Paul speaking of himself: "But I discipline my body and keep it under control, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified." The Greek word translated disqualified can mean a failed test of an athlete. Arminians see Paul admitting that even an apostle could be lost. Reformed readers see an athlete's urgency about his work and his reward, or about being set aside from useful ministry, without any doubt about his place in Christ, since the same man wrote Romans 8. Whichever is right, the text asks for something both rooms could do: keep the body in order and the heart watchful.

Galatians 5:4 reads, "You are severed from Christ, you who would be justified by the law; you have fallen away from grace." Paul is not describing a person who has committed a great sin. He is describing people who have gone back to trying to be justified by law, by adding circumcision to Christ. Here grace is the way of salvation, and to fall from grace is to step off that way on to another. Arminians take the people to be believers who have really left grace; the Reformed take Paul to be warning the Galatians about where their theology leads, while expecting better of them, as he says at 5:10. Either way the sin named is not a moral lapse. It is trusting in something other than Christ.

Second Peter 2:20 to 22 speaks of people who escaped the defilements of the world through knowing the Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ, and are entangled in them again, so that their last state is worse than the first, and Peter closes with the proverbs of the dog returning to its vomit and the washed sow to the mud. The Arminian reads real escape. The Reformed note that the chapter describes false teachers, and that the proverbs, taken at face value, speak of animals whose nature was never changed. They washed, but they were never made into something else.

You will notice a pattern. In each case, the text is clear and the question is who is being described. That should make both rooms slow to say that the other is ignoring the Bible. They are reading the same sentences and disagreeing about the people in them.

## Reading the Promise Passages in Context

Now the other side of the page. The promise passages have their own context, and the Arminian has a fair question for each.

John 10:27 to 29 sits within Jesus' discourse about the Good Shepherd. His sheep hear his voice and follow him, and no one can snatch them from his hand or the Father's. The Arminian wonders whether the verse says that no outside power can snatch a sheep, but leaves open whether a sheep might walk out of its own accord. It is a fair question, and the Reformed answer comes from the verse itself: the sheep that Jesus describes are defined by hearing and following, and are given eternal life now. A life described as eternal that can be lost does not fit the word very well, and the promise is that they will never perish.

Romans 8:28 to 39 is Paul's great argument that everything works for the good of those who love God. He traces a chain from foreknowledge through predestination and calling and justification to glorification, with no link dropped out on the way. Those who are justified are glorified, and the list of enemies that cannot separate us ends with "anything else in all creation." The Arminian replies that the "we" Paul is writing to are those in Christ, and that the list of threats is of outside forces, so that it is silent about my own decision to leave. The Reformed answer that "anything else in all creation" must include the believer himself, since he is part of creation, and that the preceding chain makes sense only if God finishes what he starts.

Philippians 1:6 is a promise addressed to a church: "And I am sure of this, that he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ." The Arminian notes that this is Paul's confidence about a particular church he knew and loved, and is not a universal law. The Reformed notice that the confidence is placed in the one who began the work, and that it is God's work and not theirs.

Ephesians 1:13 to 14 says that believers were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the guarantee of our inheritance until we acquire possession of it. The word translated guarantee is the language of a deposit, the first payment that binds the buyer to the whole. A seal in the ancient world marked ownership and security. Reformed readers hear a pledge that God has put his own name to. Arminians agree the Spirit is a real seal and a real pledge, and say the question is whether the one sealed can, by unbelief, refuse the inheritance that the seal guarantees.

## Promise and Warning on the Same Page

Here is where a piece like this is expected to name a winner. Hold the verdict a moment, because there is a verse that both rooms reach for, and each room thinks it belongs to them.

> *"They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. But they went out, that it might become plain that they all are not of us."*
>
> — 1 John 2:19 (ESV)

The first room hears a reassurance. Those who walk away never truly belonged, so the genuine are safe. The second room hears real departures from a real fellowship, and asks what exactly "of us" is meant to cover, since John says nothing about whether they were once born again. Honest readers on both sides know this verse doesn't end the debate. It only moves the debate along a little.

And this is where the floor shifts. We have all been trained, by the internet if by nothing else, to treat the Bible as two lawyers' briefs in one case. One lawyer cites the promises, the other cites the warnings, and the onlookers are invited to vote. Yet the biblical writers don't behave like opposing counsel. They set promise and warning on the same page, sometimes in the same breath, and they seem untroubled by the arrangement.

Look again at Hebrews. Having delivered the severest warning in the letter, the writer turns almost at once to the people he's addressing and says that he is sure of better things in their case (Hebrews 6:9). The warning was never meant to be the last word. It is a fence on a hillside, built for the climbers, and nobody builds a fence on a hillside to tell the climbers they are doomed. Paul does the same. In 1 Corinthians 9:27 he disciplines his body, and in Romans 8 he is sure nothing can separate him from God's love. He doesn't appear to think those two sentences quarrel.

Look again, too, at John 10. In a single sentence the sheep hear, the sheep follow, and the sheep are held. Following and being held sit side by side. The rooms do differ, and on a serious point. They differ over which of those verbs carries the weight, and what happens to a sheep that stops following. Good people answer that differently, and I won't pretend the answer is obvious. I find the Reformed claim hardest to resist when I read Romans 8 and Philippians 1:6, which describe a God who finishes what he starts. I find the Arminian claim hardest to resist when I read Hebrews 10, which doesn't sound like a hypothetical to me. Honest people feel both pulls, and the Bible seems to expect them to.

But notice what nobody in either room is arguing about. Nobody doubts that the Shepherd is able, or that the sheep are loved. They are arguing about how a loved flock is kept. And the best answer both rooms can give together is that the warning is one of the means by which God keeps his people. The Canons of Dort teach exactly this. In Article 14 God "preserves, continues, and completes his work by the hearing and reading of the gospel," and that includes its warnings as well as its promises. A mother's shout across the road is not evidence that she doubts her child's value. It is how the child stays on the pavement. The Reformed call the warnings means of perseverance, and the Arminian calls them real conditions. The practical difference at the roadside is smaller than the theological difference at the lectern.

## Hard Cases and Honest Questions

A careful reader will have questions the textbooks skip, so here are the ones I would expect from a cousin who has been listening quietly.

What about someone who was in church for ten years, prayed, served, and then walked away and now wants nothing to do with God? The honest answer is that nobody in this world sees the heart. The Reformed say that such a person either was never truly renewed or will be brought back, because God does not lose his own. The Arminian says the person was truly saved and has truly fallen, and may still be restored. Both agree that the door is open for return, and that the story of the son who left home and came back in Luke 15 is told by Jesus in order to be believed. If you are praying for such a person, the two rooms give you exactly the same instructions: keep praying, keep the door unlocked, and don't preach their funeral.

Judas is the next name that comes up. Both sides point to him. Jesus says in his prayer in John 17 that he kept those the Father gave him, and that none was lost except the son of destruction, so that Scripture might be fulfilled. The Reformed say Judas shows that someone can walk with Jesus for three years without ever being his. Wesley said the passage is about the twelve apostles, and that Judas's loss shows what apostles themselves could do. It is the same man, and the same verse, and two readings.

There is a worry that assurance is a kind of presumption. Catholics and some others worry it is. The Reformed, in the Canons and the Westminster Confession, answer that assurance is not the same as certainty about the future by a private vision. It rests on God's promises in the Word, the testimony of the Spirit, and the fruit of a changed life. Assurance of that kind produces humility, not swagger, as Article 12 of Dort says. Lutherans, Wesleyans and Pentecostals can say much the same about present assurance, while differing on its permanence.

Does it matter which view you hold? It matters for the integrity of your reading of the Bible, and it matters in how you counsel others. It matters far less for how you live on Monday. A Reformed Christian who has understood Dort and an Arminian Christian who has understood Wesley will both rise early to pray, both flee sin, both stay close to the church, and both keep the Shepherd's voice in their ears. The only really dangerous positions are the two that nobody in either room teaches: the person who says that because he is saved he may live as he likes, and the person who says that because he might be lost he must earn his way. Both are condemned by every confession quoted above.

The doubter who isn't sure they were ever saved at all will find, in the last section, what the best pastors in every tradition would say.

## What Every Tradition Here Affirms

Several traditions this far apart can still say four things together.

Salvation is a gift of grace, and no prize is earned. Jesus is fully able to save. We are called to keep trusting him and to walk close to him. And a person who loves Jesus and wants to follow him has no reason to live in dread.

None of that asks anyone to pretend the disagreement away. A Presbyterian elder in Calabar and a Methodist steward in Badagry can disagree about Hebrews 6 over a plate of rice and part as brothers. The church has managed that for four centuries, and it has survived it rather better than the internet has.

## For the Anxious and for the Comfortable

If you lie awake wondering whether you are still in, the dread of drifting is rarely the sound of drifting. People who are leaving Christ don't usually spend their midnights terrified of it. Every tradition above would say this to you in its own words. Take that fear to the Shepherd, and take it to a pastor who knows your name. Read Romans 8 aloud, slowly, as though someone were reading it to you at a bedside, because someone is. Then do the plain things that every confession commends. Pray, even badly. Stay with other believers. Open the Bible, even when it feels dry. Confess sin quickly and receive the forgiveness that is offered. The question to ask yourself is not whether you can prove your election, but whether you are trusting Christ today. If you are, you are where every room agrees you should be.

And if you've grown comfortable, if "once saved, always saved" has become a permission slip in your pocket, the warnings are addressed to you as well. No tradition in this debate teaches that Christ may be treated lightly. A grace that cost the Son of God his life doesn't ask for a casual reply. If the Reformed are right, a life of indifference should make you ask whether the new birth ever happened. If the Arminians are right, the same life is walking toward the edge. On the question of whether to wake up, the two rooms speak with one voice.

Whichever room you were raised in, the invitation is the same, and it was never an invitation to win an argument. Open these passages for yourself, with the Bible on your knee and your pastor a phone call away. Ask the Holy Spirit to open them. Then listen for the one John 10 describes, who calls his own by name and walks ahead of them. He hasn't asked you to understand the whole of how he keeps his people. He has asked you to hear his voice, and to follow.

* **Ephesians 1:13 to 14 (ESV).** Referred to, not quoted: the seal and the guarantee of the Spirit, used in the promise passages.
* **Romans 8:28 to 39 (ESV).** Central text for the Reformed case, quoted at verses 38 and 39: nothing in creation separates believers from God's love.
* **John 10:27 to 29 (ESV).** The sheep who hear, follow and are held; used in the Reformed section, in the promise passages and at the turn, and the source of the epigraph at verse 28.
* **Hebrews 6:4 to 9 (ESV).** The principal warning passage for the Arminian case, quoted at verses 4 to 6; verse 9 is referred to as the writer's confidence of better things.
* **Hebrews 10:26 to 31, 39 (ESV).** Quoted in part at verse 26; the second warning passage, with the writer's reassurance at verse 39.
* **John 15:1 to 6 (ESV).** The vine and the branches; verses 5 and 6 quoted in part.
* **Romans 11:17 to 22 (ESV).** The olive tree and the warning to Gentile believers; verse 22 quoted in part.
* **1 Corinthians 9:27 (ESV).** Paul's discipline and the fear of being disqualified.
* **Galatians 5:4 (ESV).** Falling from grace as returning to law; quoted in full.
* **2 Peter 2:20 to 22 (ESV).** Referred to, not quoted at length: those who escape the world's defilements and return to them.
* **Philippians 1:6 (ESV).** God who begins the good work will complete it; quoted in full.
* **John 17:12 (ESV).** Referred to, not quoted: none lost except the son of destruction.
* **Luke 15:11 to 32 (ESV).** Referred to, not quoted: the son who left and returned.
* **1 John 2:19 (ESV).** The verse both rooms claim; the hinge of the turn.
* **1 Timothy 1:18 to 19 (ESV).** Referred to, not quoted: shipwreck of faith, cited in the Catechism paragraph.
