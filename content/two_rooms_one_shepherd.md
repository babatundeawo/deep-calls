## Two Rooms and One Question

Picture a Sunday morning in Calabar. The service is unhurried. A choir sings, an elder reads, and the minister preaches on a God who doesn't drop what he has picked up. Afterwards, over rice and stew in the churchyard, a woman who buried her husband last year says, with the plain calm of someone reporting the weather, "I'm not worried. He has me."

Now picture a Friday night vigil on the edge of Ibadan. It is past midnight. A young man kneels on a bare floor, and he isn't praying for a visa or a job. He is praying for his own endurance. He has watched friends who once sang louder than anyone drift away, and he has no wish to become the next story told in a lowered voice. Nobody in that hall is careless about grace. They simply believe that a person who has been rescued can still walk off, and they would very much like not to.

Both rooms love Jesus. Both open the same Bible. Both have buried the faithful and prayed for the wandering. A visitor who went from the first room to the second in a single weekend could be forgiven for asking whether the two congregations follow the same gospel.

They do. The harder question is why they sound so different when they talk about the end of the road.

## What Both Rooms Believe First

Start before the argument starts. Salvation, in its plainest sense, is rescue: God lifting a person out of sin and its consequences and bringing them into friendship with himself, now and for ever. Grace is the name for how it happens, favour that nobody earned. Paul puts it in a sentence both rooms could recite in their sleep.

> *"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."*
>
> — Ephesians 2:8 to 9 (ESV)

Neither the woman in Calabar nor the young man in Ibadan thinks they earned a place in God's family. The quarrel begins one step later, and it is a narrow quarrel with a long history. Once God has saved someone, can that person lose what was given?

Sincere believers have answered in different ways, and the answers carry names. The Reformed answer, after John Calvin, the sixteenth century reformer, is usually called the perseverance of the saints. The Arminian answer takes its name from Jacobus Arminius, a Dutch teacher who died in 1609. A third voice, the Pentecostal one, deserves a hearing too, because it is the one most Nigerian readers will have grown up beside. Names are handy. They are also a little like football shirts. They tell you which team a person supports and almost nothing about what he is like at home.

## The Room Where the Hand Holds

Give the first room its best case, because it is a strong one. A Reformed believer would say that salvation begins with God, so it can't end with us. If the rescue was his work from first to last, it isn't hostage to our weakest Tuesday. The believer is no rope held up by its own grip. The believer is a child carried by a father who has never misplaced his keys, let alone his children.

The passages they love are among the most beautiful in the New Testament. Paul, having listed every threat he could think of, closes the list with a flourish.

> *"For I am sure that neither death nor life, nor angels nor rulers, nor things present nor things to come, nor powers, nor height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
>
> — Romans 8:38 to 39 (ESV)

And Jesus, speaking of his own sheep, gives a promise with two hands in it.

> *"My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand. My Father, who has given them to me, is greater than all, and no one is able to snatch them out of the Father's hand."*
>
> — John 10:27 to 29 (ESV)

The slogan "once saved, always saved" is short enough to print on a mug, which is perhaps why it travels better than it explains. Reformed teachers usually dislike it, and they would be the first to tell you so. Their own phrase, perseverance, carries two ideas at once: God keeps his people, and therefore his people keep going. They don't claim that a Christian can't stumble. The Canons of Dort, the Reformed answer written in 1618 and 1619, admit that believers can fall into grievous sin and suffer real doubt, while holding that God does not let them fall away completely. A person who coasts into careless living has, on this reading, given evidence against their own claim to have been saved. Security here is never a licence. It is the floor that obedience stands on.

This tradition isn't distant from Nigerian soil. The Presbyterian Church of Nigeria traces its beginning to 10 April 1846, when Scottish missionaries led by Hope Masterton Waddell arrived in Calabar at the invitation of the local ruler. In 2026 that church is 180 years old, and the Church of Scotland has marked the anniversary. The woman in the churchyard, whether or not she has ever heard the name of Calvin, belongs to a family that has spoken this comfort over Calabar for nearly two centuries.

The worry others raise is fair and should be stated honestly. Some fear that this teaching, in careless hands, makes people casual about holy living, and that it sits uneasily beside the Bible's many warnings about falling away. Hold that thought. It is about to walk into the second room.

## The Room Where the Voice Warns

Now give the second room its best case, because it is also a strong one. An Arminian would point out that the New Testament is full of letters written to believers who are told to continue, to watch, to hold fast and not to harden their hearts. A writer who warns a person about a cliff edge isn't insulting him. He is loving him in the only language a cliff understands.

The sharpest of those warnings sits in the letter to the Hebrews.

> *"For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt."*
>
> — Hebrews 6:4 to 6 (ESV)

Read slowly, that passage isn't describing a bystander. It describes people who were enlightened, who tasted, who shared. If that language can describe someone who never truly belonged, the second room asks, what words would the writer have used for someone who did?

Arminius's followers carried the view forward after his death. In 1610 they presented a Remonstrance of five articles to the authorities of Holland. On perseverance it was careful. It affirmed that God's grace is enough to keep believers through every trial, and then said the Scriptures had not yet been shown to settle whether such grace could never be lost. The Synod of Dort, meeting in 1618 and 1619, rejected their position and answered with five canons of its own. John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, later pressed the Arminian emphasis much harder in the eighteenth century, with a stress on holy living and on the seriousness of the warnings.

That emphasis reached Nigeria early. The Methodist missionary Thomas Birch Freeman arrived at Badagry on 24 September 1842, with William de Graft and his wife, and was the first Methodist missionary to reach the town. His father was an African freeman named Thomas, and his mother was an Englishwoman, which is a fair reminder that this was never a story of Europe handing something to Africa and nothing more.

The worry others raise here is real too. This teaching, in anxious hands, can leave a believer checking their pulse every morning to see whether they're still in. Critics fear it moves the weight of salvation from the giver to the receiver. Those who hold the view answer that staying saved is still by grace, received through faith, and never a wage for effort. The woman in Calabar and the young man in Ibadan would both say amen to that last sentence.

## Where Pentecostals Stand

Most Nigerian readers will have met this question in a Pentecostal hall, so it is worth saying plainly what that tradition holds. The Assemblies of God, one of the largest Pentecostal bodies in the world, adopted a position paper on the assurance of salvation in 2017. It declines the unconditional security view and teaches that a believer who has accepted Christ can be lost if he persistently rejects Scripture and the Spirit's conviction. In the same paper, assurance is a gift of the Holy Spirit, given at new birth, and a believer who trusts Christ and confesses sin has no need to live in doubt. Pentecostals would say, in their own words, that they hold both a warning and a comfort, and that the Spirit who warns is the same Spirit who steadies.

That is close to the Arminian shape, though Pentecostals would not thank you for filing them under a Dutchman's name. I can't speak for every Pentecostal church in Nigeria, and a book that tried to would be guessing. What anyone who has attended a vigil here can say is that the sound of a congregation taking warnings seriously and praying for endurance is the sound of this tradition at work.

## The Verse Both Rooms Quietly Borrow

Here is where a piece like this is expected to name a winner. Hold the verdict a moment, because there is a verse that both rooms reach for, and each room thinks it belongs to them.

> *"They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us. But they went out, that it might become plain that they all are not of us."*
>
> — 1 John 2:19 (ESV)

The first room hears a reassurance. Those who walk away never truly belonged, so the genuine are safe. The second room hears real departures from a real fellowship, and asks what exactly "of us" is meant to cover. Honest readers on both sides know this verse doesn't end the debate. It only moves the debate along a little.

And this is where the floor shifts. We have all been trained, by the internet if by nothing else, to treat the Bible as two lawyers' briefs in one case. One lawyer cites the promises, the other cites the warnings, and the onlookers are invited to vote. Yet the biblical writers don't behave like opposing counsel. They set promise and warning on the same page, sometimes in the same breath, and they seem untroubled by the arrangement.

Look again at Hebrews. Having delivered the severest warning in the letter, the writer turns almost at once to the people he's addressing and says that, though he speaks this way, he is sure of better things in their case, things that belong to salvation (Hebrews 6:9). The warning was never meant to be the last word. It is a fence on a hillside, built for the climbers, and nobody builds a fence on a hillside to tell the climbers they are doomed.

Look again, too, at John 10. In a single sentence the sheep hear, the sheep follow, and the sheep are held. Following and being held sit side by side. The rooms do differ, and on a serious point. They differ over which of those verbs carries the weight, and what happens to a sheep that stops following. Good people answer that differently, and I won't pretend the answer is obvious. But notice what nobody in either room is arguing about. Nobody doubts that the Shepherd is able, or that the sheep are loved. They are arguing about how a loved flock is kept.

## What Every Tradition Here Affirms

Two traditions this far apart, and a third beside them, can still say four things together.

Salvation is a gift of grace, and no prize is earned. Jesus is fully able to save. We are called to keep trusting him and to walk close to him. And a person who loves Jesus and wants to follow him has no reason to live in dread.

None of that asks anyone to pretend the disagreement away. A Presbyterian elder in Calabar and a Methodist steward in Badagry can disagree about Hebrews 6 over a plate of rice and part as brothers. The church has managed that for four centuries, and it has survived it rather better than the internet has.

## For the Anxious and for the Comfortable

If you lie awake wondering whether you are still in, the dread of drifting is rarely the sound of drifting. People who are leaving Christ don't usually spend their midnights terrified of it. Take that fear to the Shepherd, and take it to a pastor who knows your name. Read Romans 8 aloud, slowly, as though someone were reading it to you at a bedside, because someone is.

And if you've grown comfortable, if "once saved, always saved" has become a permission slip in your pocket, the warnings are addressed to you as well. No tradition in this debate teaches that Christ may be treated lightly. A grace that cost the Son of God his life doesn't ask for a casual reply.

Whichever room you were raised in, the invitation is the same, and it was never an invitation to win an argument. Open these passages for yourself, with the Bible on your knee and your pastor a phone call away. Ask the Holy Spirit to open them. Then listen for the one John 10 describes, who calls his own by name and walks ahead of them. He hasn't asked you to understand the whole of how he keeps his people. He has asked you to hear his voice, and to follow.
