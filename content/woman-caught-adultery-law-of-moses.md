## A Case Brought to the Wrong Court

The scribes and Pharisees did not carry a woman into the Temple courts that morning. They carried a case, and she happened to be the exhibit.

John tells us plainly what they were doing. They stood her in the middle of the crowd and said, "Teacher, this woman has been caught in the act of adultery" (John 8:4, ESV). Then they quoted Moses, and asked Jesus what He would say. John does not leave us guessing about their motive either.

> *"This they said to test him, that they might have some charge to bring against him."*
> — John 8:6 (ESV)

So the woman was bait. Anyone who has watched a family quarrel turn into a trial at a burial knows how this goes. A real wrong has been done, everyone present is quietly sure of it, and yet the shouting has very little to do with the wrong. It has to do with who will be made to look small by the end of the afternoon.

The question they planted was a theological one. If Jesus refused to condemn her, was He breaking the very law He claimed to fulfil? I think the answer is no, and the reasons take us through some unexpected rooms: a manuscript library, the book of Deuteronomy, a Roman statute, a Katsina courtroom and, finally, a hill outside Jerusalem. But we have to begin with something many preachers skip, because an honest reader will meet it sooner or later, and it is better to meet it here.

## Where the Story Sits in the Manuscripts

The story of the woman caught in adultery is not found in the earliest copies of John's Gospel. The ESV, which I am quoting throughout, says so in a note on the passage, which tells the reader that the earliest manuscripts do not include it (Crossway, n.d.). That's no rumour from a hostile website. The people who spend their lives reading these manuscripts agree about it, and Christians should be the first to say so.

The facts run like this. The two oldest papyrus copies of John, Papyrus 66 and Papyrus 75, leave the passage out. So do the great fourth century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and a long list of later Greek manuscripts besides. The early translations tell the same story: the Peshitta in Syriac, the Sahidic and Bohairic versions in Coptic, the Gothic version, and most Armenian copies do not have it. Two famous manuscripts, Alexandrinus and Ephraemi, have damaged pages at this point, and Bruce Metzger argued that the missing leaves were too small to have held the story, so they probably omitted it as well (Bible Researcher, n.d.). The earliest Greek manuscript that does contain it is Codex Bezae, copied around the year 400.

Then there is the odd thing that happens in the copies that do have it. The story will not stay in one place. Most manuscripts put it between John 7:52 and 8:12, where our Bibles do. A cluster known as Family 13 puts it after Luke 21:38, in the last week of Jesus' ministry, where the setting of Temple teaching seems to fit. Other manuscripts place it after John 7:36, or after John 7:44, or at the very end of John after 21:25 (Bible Researcher, n.d.; Crossway, n.d.). A Romanian Orthodox scholar, Mihai Ciurea, counts at least twelve locations for it, and he thinks the lectionaries explain a good deal of the wandering, because the passage was left out of the Pentecost reading in the standard Greek lectionary and so had to find a home elsewhere (Ciurea, 2025). A text that was nailed down in the original manuscript does not usually wander about like a student looking for a seat. Some scribes also marked it with asterisks or little daggers called obeli, which was a way of telling the reader that doubts existed (Bible Researcher, n.d.).

If the story is not John's, where did it come from? Here the evidence is actually richer than most people suppose, and a good deal of it is African. Eusebius, the church historian, reports that Papias, writing in the early second century, "put forth another history concerning a woman accused of many sins before the Lord" (as quoted in Bible Researcher, n.d.). No one can be sure it is the same story. A later report ties it to the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and that book has not survived (Ciurea, 2025). A Syriac church order from the third or early fourth century, the *Didascalia Apostolorum*, tells bishops to be gentle with sinners, and it points to a sinful woman set before Jesus by the elders (Ciurea, 2025). And in Egypt, in the fourth century, Didymus the Blind, the great teacher of Alexandria, wrote a commentary on Ecclesiastes that was only discovered in 1941, and in it he describes a woman condemned for sin, the Saviour's challenge that whoever was without sin should throw the first stone, and nobody daring to do it (Criddle, n.d.; Ciurea, 2025). Bart Ehrman, no friend of easy answers, studied this and concluded that the form we read today is a combination of two early versions, one like Didymus's and one that lies behind Papias and the *Didascalia* (Ehrman, 1988).

Among the Latin Fathers the story was well known and loved. Ambrose treated it as Scripture. Jerome included it in the Vulgate, and his own Latin Bible then carried it across the West for a thousand years. And Augustine, the African bishop of Hippo, who knew exactly that some copies lacked it, offered an explanation that I have never been able to forget.

> *"Certain persons of little faith, or rather enemies of the true faith, fearing, I suppose, lest their wives should be given impunity in sinning, removed from their manuscripts the Lord's act of forgiveness toward the adulteress, as if he who had said, Sin no more, had granted permission to sin."*
> — Augustine, *On Adulterous Marriages* 2.6 to 7

Whether Augustine was right about the motive is another matter, and I will come back to it. The point for now is that in about 420 an African bishop already knew that copies differed, and already had an argument about why.

## The Strongest Case Against the Passage

I want to give the sceptic's argument at full strength, because it is a good one. It goes like this. The external evidence is lopsided. The earliest Greek copies in all the main streams of the tradition, from Egypt and the East, omit the story. No Greek church writer comments on it until the twelfth century, when Euthymius Zigabenus says that the accurate copies do not have it (Metzger, as summarised in Bible Researcher, n.d.; Ciurea, 2025). When it does appear in Greek manuscripts, it shifts about, and the Greek copies that contain it show an unusual number of variant readings, as if it had been copied from a loose oral tradition and not a fixed written text. Its vocabulary and style also sit awkwardly in John, and Metzger lists a good many words and constructions that are not characteristic of the Fourth Gospel. Metzger judged the evidence against John as its author to be "overwhelming" (as quoted in Bible Researcher, n.d.). Jennifer Knust and Tommy Wasserman, who have given years to this single passage, argue that it probably entered a Greek manuscript of John in the West, perhaps as early as the early third century (Knust & Wasserman, 2020).

Some readers want to push the objection further and say that the church is hiding something. Let me answer that directly. The church hasn't hidden anything. The scribes who copied the story did something I find quietly moving, which was to mark it so that every future reader could see the line. And modern Bibles, including the ESV and the NIV, print it in brackets with a note. If there was a conspiracy, it was a very honest one.

The case on the other side has serious defenders, and they should be heard in their own words. They say that the story was in John from the beginning and was dropped, and that Augustine's explanation fits the pressure of a church that worried about moral laxity in the third and fourth centuries. They say that the vast majority of Greek manuscripts after the fifth century contain it, and that the Byzantine text, which dominated the Greek East for a thousand years, has it in the standard place. Maurice Robinson, who has argued for its originality, is one of several contributors to a recent volume where scholars on both sides of the question set out their cases (Koine Greek, 2016). Chris Keith has made a different and interesting proposal, which is that the story was written to answer the question hanging over John 7:15, where people ask how Jesus can have learning when He was never taught, and so it may be a very early and very deliberate addition. I take no side in that quarrel as a scholar, because I am not one. What I can do is say what difference each answer would make.

Here is where I land. If the story is not John's, it is still very old, and it is still the church's story. Papias, the *Didascalia*, Didymus and Augustine did not all invent it independently. Metzger, who judged the evidence against Johannine authorship overwhelming, still held that the account has "all the earmarks of historical veracity" (as quoted in Bible Researcher, n.d.), and thought it likely an oral tradition which was later written into the Gospel. And nothing I need to say about grace depends on this one scene. Paul writes, "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV), and John records Jesus saying elsewhere that God did not send His Son into the world to condemn it (John 3:17). Those texts stand in every manuscript. The story of the woman illustrates that teaching and does not hold it up. If you decide it does not belong in John, you will still have the gospel. You will have lost a very good story, and that is all.

Notice one more thing about the evidence, because it is a gift to African readers. Egypt's dry sands gave us the two oldest papyri of John, and those papyri leave the story out. Egypt's most famous blind scholar also knew some form of it. The bishop who tells us why it was cut from some copies was an African, and so was the first great Latin writer, Tertullian, who never mentions it in his arguments about adultery (as noted in Bible Researcher, n.d.). The story's history is partly a record of how the African church read and copied its Scriptures. We are in the evidence, not merely looking at it from outside.

So I will read what follows as a scene the church has long believed captures the Lord truthfully, with the scholars' caution printed on the label.

## What Moses Said About Adultery

The accusers were right about the sentence. The law of Moses took adultery very seriously. It sits in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:14), and the book of Leviticus is not vague about the penalty.

> *"If a man commits adultery with the wife of his neighbor, both the adulterer and the adulteress shall surely be put to death."*
> — Leviticus 20:10 (ESV)

Deuteronomy says the same, and says it more fully.

> *"If a man is found lying with the wife of another man, both of them shall die, the man who lay with the woman, and the woman."*
> — Deuteronomy 22:22 (ESV)

Both of them. The man and the woman. Which makes one question impossible to avoid, however politely we try.

The law goes on to handle cases with care. Deuteronomy 22:23 and 24 deals with a betrothed girl found with a man inside a city, and it puts both parties to death at the city gate, the woman because she did not cry out where help was near, and the man because he violated his neighbour's wife. Verses 25 to 27 then address a case in the open country where the girl is overpowered, and the law does the opposite. Only the man dies, and the woman is treated as a victim who did nothing wrong. So the law distinguished consent from violence, and place from place, and it did so in a society where such distinctions were not obvious. Leviticus 18:20 forbids sleeping with a neighbour's wife, and Proverbs 6:32 to 35 describes the adulterer as a man who destroys himself and cannot buy off a husband's fury. There is also a strange and unusual procedure in Numbers 5:11 to 31 for a husband who suspects his wife but has no witnesses. It is a ritual oath before the priest, with the outcome left to God. It concerns a wife only, and a sceptic is right to notice that. But it protects her in a way that is easily missed, because it sends the matter to God and not to a mob, and no one is allowed to carry out a sentence on suspicion.

A fair critic will push back here, and the objection deserves its full strength. The Hebrew Bible, he says, lives inside a patriarchal world. Adultery in that world meant a married woman sleeping with a man, and a husband who slept with an unmarried woman was not guilty of adultery in the strict sense. So the law protected a husband's rights, and the woman was property. I don't think that criticism is silly. The definition of adultery in the Old Testament does centre on the married or betrothed woman. But three things are worth saying. First, when the sentence was given, it fell on both people. Second, the prophets turned that same standard against kings and husbands, with Nathan's confrontation of David as the plainest example. And third, Jesus Himself in the Sermon on the Mount extends the standard to a man's heart, saying that looking at a woman to lust after her is already adultery (Matthew 5:27 to 28). He closed the loophole that the critic complains about.

The Mishnah, the Jewish law collection written down around the year 200, shows how later teachers read all this. Mishnah Sanhedrin 7:1 lists four kinds of capital punishment, and the Gemara explains who falls under each: a betrothed daughter of an ordinary Israelite is stoned, while a married one is strangled, and a priest's daughter who commits adultery is burned (Jewish Virtual Library, n.d.). I raise it only to say that by the time of Jesus the Jewish courts had worked out a careful system, and that none of it allowed a sentence to be handed down in a courtyard by a shouting crowd. The Mishnah is later than the New Testament, and historians are cautious about reading it back into the first century, but the instinct that capital cases need a court is plainly present in the law of Moses itself.

## The Man Who Was Never Brought

Adultery takes two people. John's own report, in the accusers' mouths, is that she was caught "in the act." Whoever she was with was, on that account, caught in the act as well. Yet the only person dragged into the Temple courts was the woman.

I think this is the first crack in their case, and Jesus doesn't even have to point to it. Whatever else the scribes were doing, they weren't applying Moses. They were applying Moses to the one defendant who suited them, and quietly losing the other. Anyone who has seen how scandal works in a church or a compound knows the pattern. The man has a reputation to protect, or a friend among the elders, or simply the advantage of not being the one anyone expected to cry. It's remarkable how often the evidence goes missing when it would implicate someone useful.

Nigerian scholars have noticed the pattern too, and I don't want to pretend they agree with each other. John Ottuh, a Nigerian biblical scholar writing in 2019, read Deuteronomy 22:22 beside John 8:1 to 11 through the eyes of his own Urhobo community, and argued that both texts reflect a world where women hold lower status and where the weight of a charge falls unevenly on them (Ottuh, 2019). C. U. Manus and J. C. Ukaga, in 2017, read the story alongside the abuse of women in northeastern Nigeria, including the kidnapping of the Chibok girls, and argued that the Jesus of this scene gives a theological basis for the rights of women (Ukaga & Manus, 2017). You may or may not accept every part of their reading. But they are right that a Nigerian reader cannot hear the question "where was the man?" as a puzzle from the past.

There is a second thing the accusers skipped, and it matters even more. Moses did not allow a death sentence on a crowd's say so. The same book is firm about proof.

> *"On the evidence of two witnesses or of three witnesses the one who is to die shall be put to death."*
> — Deuteronomy 17:6 (ESV)

And in the next verse it says who acts first.

> *"The hand of the witnesses shall be first against him to put him to death, and afterward the hand of all the people."*
> — Deuteronomy 17:7 (ESV)

The witnesses had to be the first to lift a stone. That is a heavy thing to ask of anyone. It meant that a person who accuses with the intent that someone should die must be ready to carry out the sentence with their own hands, and so must be a person who can bear to be looked at. The law also gave a rule for false witnesses, who were to suffer the penalty they had planned for their target (Deuteronomy 19:15 to 21). Moses had built a fence around the courtroom, and the men in John 8 had climbed over it before the day's business began.

## Moses on One Side and Rome on the Other

The usual reading of the trap is this. If Jesus said, "Stone her," He could be reported to the Romans for ordering an execution that only the governor had the right to order. If He said, "Let her go," He could be called a man who set Himself above Moses. Either answer would hand the crowd what they wanted.

I should say that John does not spell out the two horns. He gives us only the word "test" and the phrase "some charge." The reading comes from the setting, and it is a reasonable one, but it deserves more care than preachers usually give it. In John 18:31 the Jewish leaders tell Pilate that it is not lawful for them to put anyone to death. Yet in Acts 7 a mob stones Stephen, and Josephus, the Jewish historian, tells us that in AD 62 the high priest Ananus took advantage of a gap between two Roman governors, when "Festus was now dead, and Albinus was but upon the road," to assemble the Sanhedrin, accuse James the brother of Jesus as a breaker of the law, and deliver him "to be stoned" (Josephus, n.d., *Antiquities* 20.200, Whiston translation). So historians argue about how tightly Rome held the right of execution, and whether it applied to every case, including religious offences like this one. The trap does not need the law to be clear. It needs only that the answer could be turned against Him in either direction.

Rome had its own view of adultery, and it is worth knowing. Under the Lex Julia of 18 BC, Augustus made adultery an offence to be tried before a court, with penalties that included the loss of half of a man's property and, for the wife, the loss of part of her dowry and banishment to an island. A father who caught a man with his daughter could kill him on the spot, and a husband had a narrower right, but the law surrounded even that with conditions and time limits (Adams, n.d.). In other words, neither Moses nor Rome knew of the arrangement the crowd was proposing, which was a verdict by acclamation in an open courtyard. Both legal systems, whatever else we say about them, asked for a court and for evidence.

Jesus declines the legal quarrel they have set up. He bends down, and writes on the ground with His finger. John does not tell us what.

## What He Wrote in the Dust

This detail gets more attention than any other in the story, and the interpretations are many. Readers across the centuries have suggested that He wrote the sins of the accusers, or the words of the law they were quoting, or simply nothing at all, doodling as a way of refusing to be hurried (BibleHub, n.d.). Mihai Ciurea, surveying the proposals, thinks they are all conjecture and prefers to ask what the act of writing does, twice, in John 8:6 and 8:8, suggesting a withdrawal from the dispute or a gesture toward human mortality and the earth (Ciurea, 2025). I think that is wise. But two old suggestions deserve to be taken seriously, because they connect the scene to Scripture and not to guesswork.

The first is the prophet Jeremiah. Jeremiah 17:13 says, in a verse that every scribe in that courtyard would have known, that those who turn away from the Lord, the fountain of living water, shall be written in the earth, in the dust. Some readers have thought that Jesus, writing in the dust as the accusers walked away, was enacting that verse, and so naming what was happening to the men who had forsaken God while standing in front of Him (BibleHub, n.d.). The second is Sinai. The tablets of the law were, according to Exodus, "written with the finger of God" (Exodus 31:18, ESV), and here is a man writing with His finger in the Temple. Augustine, preaching on this chapter, set the two writings side by side. The law, he says, was written with the finger of God, and written on stone for the sake of hard hearts. And then of the Lord in the courtyard: "The Lord now wrote on the ground, because He was seeking fruit" (Augustine, Tractate 33.5, in Gibb's translation). He added that the accusers had "not yet understood Him as the Lawgiver."

Pause on that last remark, because it is what I most want you to see. On this reading the Law they were using against Jesus was His own. The finger that carved Sinai's stone is the finger now moving in the dirt. It is a lovely thing to notice, and I notice it too. If you ask me whether John meant it, I have to say he does not tell us, and I would be claiming more than I can support if I told you that the writing is a hidden proof of anything. Better to say what the text does say. He did not answer at once. He would not be hurried. A crowd that wants an answer in five seconds has usually decided already what it will do with it.

## The Trinity Argument and How Far It Goes

There is an argument that circulates in Muslim and Christian conversation, and you have probably met a version of it from a colleague, a relative or a video that went round on someone's WhatsApp status. It says that Jesus never taught the Trinity. The doctrine was assembled later, by church councils, out of political convenience and Greek philosophy. A careful reading of the Gospels, the argument goes, shows a Jesus who pointed away from Himself toward the Father, who never claimed equality with God and who never spoke of a Spirit as a third divine person.

Let me state that position as a thoughtful Muslim would. He honours Jesus as the Messiah, born of the virgin Mary, a mighty prophet and a worker of miracles. What he denies is that God is three. The Quran, which he believes to be God's last word, tells the People of the Book not to say "three." For him the oneness of God is the first fact of faith and the one thing no creed may touch, and he sees the Trinity as a late addition to a pure message that Jesus Himself would not recognise. I think that is a serious view, held by serious and often kind people, and it deserves a serious answer and not a slogan.

Some Christian readers, and I include the thought behind this essay in its first form, have found an answer packed inside the scene of John 8. It runs like this. Jesus writes with His finger, and the finger of God wrote the tablets, so the Lawgiver is standing in the dust. In Jeremiah 17:13 those who forsake the Lord are written in the earth, and the Lord there is called the fountain of living waters. A few days before, at the Feast of Tabernacles in the same Temple, Jesus had cried out that anyone who was thirsty should come to Him and drink, and that rivers of living water would flow from the believer's heart, and John explains that He meant the Spirit (John 7:37 to 39). So in one silent gesture, the argument says, Father, Son and Spirit are all present: the Lawgiver whose finger writes, the Lord of Jeremiah who is the source of living water, and the Spirit who is that water. The Son kneels in the dirt exercising divine authority over the law He authored. The Jewish scholars who walked away had every tool to read it, and they chose not to.

Here is how I weigh that argument, since being fair to Christians matters as much as being fair to Muslims. It has real strengths. The echo of Exodus is very natural. The connection between the finger of God and the Spirit of God is also supported elsewhere, for in Luke 11:20 Jesus says He casts out demons by "the finger of God," where Matthew 12:28 reports the same saying as "the Spirit of God." That is a genuine link in the Gospels, and it makes the Spirit's presence in the image less strange. And John 7:37 to 39 stands one page earlier. The weaknesses are just as real. John never says what Jesus wrote. The reading depends on a chain of echoes that no first century listener is reported to have heard, and a sceptic can fairly say that the argument proves too much, since almost any gesture can be tied to some verse. And then there is the matter of the manuscripts. A passage whose place in John is disputed is a shaky foundation for a doctrine as large as the Trinity.

So I treat it as a resonance and not a proof. The doctrine does not need it. The case that Jesus claimed to be more than a prophet is made in places where nobody has to guess. In the very same chapter, a few verses later, He says, "I am the light of the world" (John 8:12). In the same chapter again, He says to His opponents, "Before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58), and they pick up stones to kill Him, which is a detail that fits oddly with the claim that He never said anything divine. Thomas calls Him "My Lord and my God" in John 20:28, and Jesus does not correct him. In Matthew 28:19 the disciples are told to baptise in the one name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. The Trinity isn't a diagram that Jesus drew, and the Muslim critic is right to say that the word does not appear in the Gospels. It is the church's attempt, in the creeds of Nicaea in 325 and Constantinople in 381, to say what the Gospels already assume when they put Father, Son and Spirit side by side and refuse to put the Son among the creatures. The scene in the Temple courts can be read in harmony with that, and I find it lovely to do so. But the weight rests on the whole witness of John, not on a finger in the dust.

## The Only Person Who Qualified

When they pressed Him, He stood and gave His answer.

> *"Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her."*
> — John 8:7 (ESV)

People take this sentence two wrong ways. Some use it to say that nobody may ever judge anything, as if Jesus had handed out a permanent excuse. Others think He was inventing a softer ethic to replace the harsh God of the Old Testament. Both readings miss that He is speaking in the grammar of the law itself. Deuteronomy 17 had already said that the accusers must act first, and Jesus is simply asking whether they qualify. He was not inventing a new standard. He was citing the old one, slowly, to men who had come in with it like a club.

And they could not qualify. John says that "they went away one by one, beginning with the older ones, and Jesus was left alone" (John 8:9, ESV). The oldest went first, as you would expect. Longer lives have longer ledgers. Nobody threw a stone, and nobody needed Jesus to explain why.

This is where the story turns. The crowd has gone, and everyone who had a claim to speak has left because they were disqualified. But one person in that courtyard was not disqualified. He had no secret. He had not set the trap, or hidden the other party. Earlier in the same chapter He asks His opponents, "Which one of you convicts me of sin?" (John 8:46), and the text records no answer, because there was none to give. He was the one human being present who had the right, under the very law being cited, to take up the stone.

He could have thrown it. And He did not.

Augustine, who knew this chapter well and preached on it, put it better than I can. After the accusers left, he said, "The two were left alone, the wretched woman and Mercy" (Augustine, Tractate 33.5). A fifth century bishop in Hippo and a Nigerian reader in the twenty first century can find the same thing in that line. She was left with the only Person who could have condemned her justly, and He was the one who did not.

Then He asked where her accusers were. She said none remained.

> *"Neither do I condemn you; go, and from now on sin no more."*
> — John 8:11 (ESV)

Look at what He didn't say. He didn't say she had done nothing, or that adultery was a small matter, or that Moses was out of date. The command to sin no more only makes sense if there was something to leave behind. Yet He refused the sentence, and He gave her a future.

## Whether Anyone May Judge After This

An honest reader will want to ask whether this story, taken seriously, abolishes judgement altogether. Can it mean that no court may ever execute and no church may ever rebuke? I don't think it can bear that weight, and the reasons come from Scripture itself.

In John 7:24, a chapter earlier, Jesus tells the crowd to stop judging by appearances and to judge with right judgement. Paul tells the church in Corinth to deal with a man living in open immorality (1 Corinthians 5), and tells the Romans that the governing authority does not bear the sword in vain (Romans 13:4). Jesus Himself, when a man asked Him to settle an inheritance quarrel, replied that He had not been made a judge over them (Luke 12:13 to 14). The pattern is that He refuses to be drawn into a rigged process, and yet He never says that wrong does not exist. What He rejects in John 8 is self appointed prosecution by unqualified people, who have chosen the target and arranged the evidence. That leaves a great many questions standing. Whether the state should ever execute is one of them, and Christians who love the Bible have answered it differently, some drawing on Romans 13 and some on the whole movement of the gospel toward mercy. This story cannot settle it, and I would be uncomfortable if you thought it did.

## Where the Sentence Went

An honest listener asks the next question straight away. If the law said death, and the one person qualified to carry out the sentence refused to, did justice simply go missing? Did God look the other way?

The New Testament answers that it did not. Paul says that all have sinned and fall short of God's glory, and are justified by His grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God put forward as a propitiation by His blood, so that He might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus (Romans 3:23 to 26). The word propitiation means a sacrifice that turns away deserved wrath, and the argument of that passage is that God did not pretend sin was small. He bore it. Paul says elsewhere that the wages of sin is death (Romans 6:23), and he says that Christ became a curse for us, since the law says that the one hanged on a tree is cursed (Galatians 3:13, quoting Deuteronomy 21:23). Isaiah had said seven centuries before that morning that the Servant would be pierced for our transgressions and crushed for our iniquities (Isaiah 53:5). The sentence was not cancelled. It was carried. The penalty that the law asked for in that courtyard, and that every sin in every life asks for, was taken by the One who had the right to give it. Outside the city walls, the stone found its mark. It landed on Him.

The Muslim friend I imagined earlier will object here, and he will say it with real force. He will ask why a merciful God would need anyone to die, and whether the Almighty cannot simply forgive, as a king forgives a servant. He will remind me that the Quran teaches that no soul bears the burden of another. I would say three things in answer. The first is that human forgiveness is never free of cost, however it looks from outside. When your neighbour forgives a debt, the debt does not vanish. He takes the loss himself. The second is that the Bible's claim is stronger than forgiveness by decree, because it says God is just and the justifier at the same time, and these two things could not be kept together at all if justice were merely set aside. And the third is that on this account, the burden was not forced onto an unwilling third party. The Son who bore it is the Lord who was offended. God did not punish an innocent stranger. He stepped into His own sentence.

You do not have to lean on any Western theory about courtrooms to see it. Any Nigerian family knows that a real debt has to be settled by somebody, and that a wrong cannot be dismissed just because the offender feels sorry. The cross is where God settled it, in His own body, on a Friday afternoon. It was not a seed sown into a pastor's account, or a transaction that rewards the one who can pay the most. It was a settlement made at the point where holiness and love meet.

So I don't think the woman was simply let off. She was covered. The distance between those two words is the distance between a mercy that costs nothing and the mercy of Calvary.

## Women Still Standing in the Middle

It would be easy to file all this under ancient history, but Nigerians have watched a version of that courtyard in our own time.

Consider a case that the whole country watched. In March 2002 a Sharia court in Katsina State sentenced a woman named Amina Lawal to death by stoning after she gave birth outside marriage. The man she named as the father, Yahaya Mohammed, denied it and was acquitted. The sentence was postponed until her daughter was weaned, and an early appeal was rejected. On 25 September 2003 an appeals court in Katsina, with four of its five judges concurring, overturned the sentence, pointing to procedural errors in the original trial (PBS NewsHour, 2003). She left the courthouse with her little girl and said she wanted to go home, get married and live a normal life.

I don't tell this to score a point against our Muslim neighbours. Their own judges threw the sentence out on the strength of their own law's demand for proof, which is to their credit. I tell it because the shape matches John 8. A woman stands alone in the dock. The man is somewhere else. And the only thing that stopped the stones was a stubborn insistence that accusation is not evidence and that a verdict needs a lawful court.

Most of us will never see a stoning. Many of us will see something that works the same way, only quieter. Picture a church (this is a scene I am imagining, not a report). A young woman is found to be pregnant outside marriage. On Sunday a leader stands before the congregation and announces her name and what she has done. The young man responsible, who attends the same church and sings in the same choir, is not mentioned. Everyone in the building understands the arrangement. Church discipline, as Jesus describes it, starts somewhere else.

> *"If your brother sins against you, go and tell him his fault, between you and him alone."*
> — Matthew 18:15 (ESV)

Between you and him alone. The first step in the Lord's own method is a private conversation, with the aim of winning someone back, not of winning an argument. A rebuke designed to be seen is too often a stone with a pulpit behind it.

The same thing happens on our phones. A screenshot goes into a family group, then into three more, and by evening a stranger in another city has an opinion about a person he has never met. The woman in John 8 faced a crowd of a few dozen. Today's crowd arrives faster and does not forget. The Lord's challenge to men holding their stones is just as good a question for a thumb hovering over the forward button.

## The Command That Follows the Mercy

"Go, and from now on sin no more" is easy to swallow whole, in the drama of everything before it. It should not be. It is not a polite suggestion tacked on at the end. It is the story's insistence on being taken seriously. Grace does not lower the standard. Grace makes possible what the standard asks and what the flesh, left to itself, cannot deliver. The woman was not sent away with a note that said the rules no longer applied to her. She was sent into a new life, or at least invited into one, which is as far as grace can take a person before the will must answer.

Nigerian Christians talk a great deal about deliverance. Vigils, prayers that shake the night, the breaking of chains and the cancelling of curses until the speaker is hoarse and the congregation is tired. There is a real biblical reality beneath all of that. Scripture is not shy about the demonic, and anyone who has watched the darker corners of religious life in this country knows the supernatural is neither a Western import nor an African leftover. It is described in the Bible with complete seriousness and dealt with by the same Jesus who crouched in the dust of that courtyard.

But there is a kind of deliverance that arrives without a programme or a handkerchief or a bottle of oil. It comes with an encounter. A verdict. A word spoken quietly into a life that had braced itself for a stone. Then comes a walk home that is ordinary, daily and demanding, and made possible by the Spirit whom Jesus said would convict the world of sin and of righteousness and of judgement (John 16:8). If you want to know whether a deliverance was real, look at what the person does the following Tuesday.

## Taking Your Hand Off the Stone

If you have been the woman in this story, with your name in people's mouths and a sin that is real and not imaginary, the story was kept for you. It never says your sin was small. It says the One who is able to condemn you has chosen to carry the sentence Himself, and that His word to you is "go, and from now on sin no more." To be told to go is a kindness. It means there is a life to go back to.

And if you are the one holding a stone, think of the last time you passed a story about someone's failure to a friend. What did you hope it would do? Then think of the person you know who is carrying a sin quietly, and ask whether a private word, offered this week and meant for their good, is in order. Jesus began with the crowd and ended with the woman. Most of us are better at the first. I would like to see our churches known for the second, for people who stay behind after the others have gone.
