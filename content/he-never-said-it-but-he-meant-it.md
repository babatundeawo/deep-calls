## The Question That Has Been Waiting Since Jerusalem

Let us begin with a challenge that gets passed around online like a cold that nobody wants to claim they started. It shows up in comment sections, in university dormitory debates, in WhatsApp groups where someone's cousin has just discovered a new religion and is feeling bold about it. The challenge sounds airtight. It sounds, in fact, devastating: *"Show me where Jesus ever said, 'I am God, worship me.' Just find me those exact words. Anywhere. I'll wait."*

And there is a pause. And the Christian, sometimes, fumbles. Not because the answer does not exist, but because the question was designed to make you forget that you already know it.

This is what logicians call a loaded question and what Nigerians call a man who asks you why you didn't greet him, while standing behind a door. The demand for a specific phrase assumes that a divine identity can only be communicated through a specific formula — that unless Jesus filled out the correct declaration form and submitted it in triplicate to the Department of Theological Claims, his divinity remains unregistered. It is a clever trap. It is also, once you see it clearly, not very clever at all.

Here is what I want to do with you. I want to walk through what Jesus actually said and did — not the phrase he never uttered, but the mountain of evidence he left behind — and by the time we are done, I want you to see that the demand for those exact words is not a serious theological argument. It is a magic trick. And the first rule of magic is that it only works when you are not paying attention.

---

## The Trap Hidden Inside the Question

Before we get to Jesus, let us apply the same logic somewhere else, just to watch it collapse.

Where in the Quran does Muhammad say, verbatim, "I am a prophet, obey me"? Not that he *claimed* prophethood. Not that the text *implies* it repeatedly. Those exact words. Find them. Similarly, where does Jesus say, "I am merely a wise teacher and nothing more"? Or "I am the word of God made flesh" — because that phrase, so beloved in Christian theology, is not something Jesus says about himself either. It appears in John's prologue, attributed to John the Evangelist. Does that make it untrue?

The point is this: the way a person communicates their identity is almost never through a single declarative sentence. You know a man's character not because he once stood up and announced it at a town meeting, but because of the pattern of everything he said and did over years — the choices he made under pressure, the things he refused to deny, the moments when he could have corrected a misunderstanding and chose instead to let it stand.

Jesus did not leave us without evidence. He left us with so much evidence that the problem is not shortage — it is that people have trained themselves to look away from it.

## Before Abraham Was, the Earth Was Already Asking Questions

Consider what Jesus said to a hostile crowd in the temple courts of Jerusalem. The exchange begins with a dispute about who his father was and escalates, as temple disputes tend to do, into something that could have gotten him killed on the spot. Which, in fact, it nearly did.

> *"Very truly I tell you," Jesus answered, "before Abraham was born, I am!"*
> — John 8:58 (NIV)

Read that sentence again and sit with what is unusual about it. He does not say, "Before Abraham was born, I *was*." He says, "I *am*." Present tense. Eternal present. The grammar is deliberate, and any Jew in that crowd would have known exactly what it referenced.

Go back to Exodus 3, where Moses stands barefoot before a burning bush that refuses to stop burning, and asks the voice inside it what he should tell Pharaoh when Pharaoh asks who sent him. The answer comes back:

> *"God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM. This is what you are to say to the Israelites: I AM has sent me to you.'"*
> — Exodus 3:14 (NIV)

This is not a coincidence. Jesus was not reaching for poetic language about personal continuity. He was reaching for the name. The divine name. The name that the scribes and Pharisees refused to write in full, that the priests pronounced only once a year in the innermost court of the temple, that a Jewish reader encountering it on a page would mark with reverence. And Jesus used it casually, publicly, as a description of himself.

The crowd knew exactly what he meant. That is why they picked up stones. You do not stone a man for making an abstract philosophical claim about temporal existence. You stone a man for blasphemy. And blasphemy, in the Jewish legal tradition, meant claiming to be God.

He never said "I am God, worship me."

He said something louder.

## The Man Who Forgave What Was Not His to Forgive

There is a scene in Mark's Gospel that is easy to miss if you read it too quickly, but impossible to forget once you have really heard it. A paralysed man is carried by four friends to a house where Jesus is teaching. The house is so packed that the friends, determined not to be stopped, climb to the roof and dig through it — tiles and plaster and all — and lower their man down on a mat into the middle of the room.

Jesus looks at the man on the mat. And then he says something that stops the room cold:

> *"Son, your sins are forgiven."*
> — Mark 2:5 (NIV)

The religious teachers sitting in the crowd immediately understand the problem. They do not say it aloud. But the text records their thoughts:

> *"Why does this fellow talk like that? He's blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?"*
> — Mark 2:7 (NIV)

They are absolutely right. Within the Jewish theological framework, sin is fundamentally an offence against God. When David, after his catastrophic failure with Bathsheba, writes the great psalm of repentance, he says to God: "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4). David had wronged Uriah. He had wronged Bathsheba. He had wronged his own household. But he understood that sin, in its deepest nature, is a vertical offense — a rupture in the relationship between creature and Creator.

This means that only God can truly forgive sin. A bank can forgive a debt. A victim can forgive an assault. But no human being has the standing to forgive the cosmic debt that sin accumulates before the throne of God. No human being, that is, except the one who *is* God.

Jesus knew what the teachers were thinking. He turned to them directly:

> *"Which is easier: to say to this paralysed man, 'Your sins are forgiven,' or to say, 'Get up, take your mat and walk'? But I want you to know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins."*
> — Mark 2:9-10 (NIV)

Then he healed the man.

He never said "I am God, worship me."

He proved it instead.

## The Passage Everyone Quotes and Almost Nobody Finishes Reading

John 14:6 is the verse that gets embroidered on church banners and printed on bumper stickers. It is the verse that pastors reach for when they want something quotable. And I understand why:

> *"I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me."*
> — John 14:6 (NIV)

But most people who quote it stop reading before the next few verses, which is unfortunate, because the next few verses are where it gets theologically explosive. Philip, one of the disciples, hears this claim and responds with what sounds like a reasonable request: "Lord, show us the Father and that will be enough for us."

It sounds humble. Show us God. That's all we need.

Jesus' response is not gentle:

> *"Don't you know me, Philip, even after I have been among you such a long time? Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father."*
> — John 14:9 (NIV)

Anyone who has seen me has seen the Father.

There is no way to read that sentence as anything other than a claim to divine identity. Jesus is not saying he has a special connection to God, or that his teachings reflect divine wisdom, or that he is particularly close to the Father in the way that prophets are close to God. He is saying that to look at him is to look at God. That his face and the Father's face are, in some profound ontological sense, the same face.

This is the same claim that the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE would later wrestle with in technical theological language — the *homoousios*, the declaration that the Son is of the same substance as the Father. The bishops at Nicaea were not inventing something new. They were formalising what the texts already said and what the church had always believed. Jesus had been making the claim for three centuries before they found the Greek vocabulary to articulate it precisely.

## When the Demons Testified and the Disciples Followed

Here is something that should give the sceptic pause: the beings who were most consistently clear about Jesus' divine identity were the ones who had no motive to flatter him.

When Jesus arrived in the region of the Gerasenes and encountered the man who lived among the tombs — the man so possessed that chains could not hold him, who cut himself with stones and screamed through the night — the first voice to speak was not the man's. The text records:

> *"What do you want with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God? In God's name don't torture me!"*
> — Mark 5:7 (NIV)

Son of the Most High God. Not "a great teacher." Not "an impressive rabbi." Not "a prophet with unusual gifts." The spiritual beings on the other side of the veil — the ones who had been present at creation, who knew what God looked like from the inside, who had no theological incentive to exaggerate — called him by his correct title, unprompted.

Compare this to what happened at his baptism, when the endorsement came from above rather than below:

> *"And a voice from heaven said, 'This is my Son, whom I love; with him I am well pleased.'"*
> — Matthew 3:17 (NIV)

From the lowest register of the spiritual order to the highest, the testimony was consistent. The ones below trembled. The one above declared. And in between, the disciples watched and, slowly, understood.

Thomas's confession at the end of John's Gospel is perhaps the most dramatic arrival at that understanding. Thomas, the one who refused to believe without physical evidence — Thomas, who would have been perfectly at home in any modern philosophy department — when he finally encountered the risen Christ, said eight words that constitute one of the most theologically loaded sentences in the entire New Testament:

> *"My Lord and my God!"*
> — John 20:28 (NIV)

Jesus did not correct him. He did not say, "Thomas, please, I'm just a prophet." He accepted the worship. And Jesus, who had spent his entire ministry correcting misunderstandings, would not have accepted worship from a disciple if worship were not his due.

## The Defence That Destroys Itself

There is an argument that says: "These testimonies don't count. Thomas was confused. The Gospel writers were biased. The demons might have been lying." This argument, if followed to its logical conclusion, would prevent us from knowing anything about any historical figure. We know what Julius Caesar said because people who were there wrote it down. We know what Muhammad taught because his companions recorded it. The reliability of ancient testimony does not increase because the subject is secular. The witnesses to Jesus' ministry — including the hostile witnesses, including the religious authorities who wanted him dead and would have gladly corrected any exaggerated claims about his identity — left us a record. And that record points, from every direction, toward the same conclusion.

There is a saying from the Yoruba tradition: the water that is far away does not frighten the one who knows the depth of the well. The objectors to Jesus' divinity are standing at the well and insisting it is shallow because they cannot see the bottom. The depth has been documented. The testimonies are in the record. The only question is whether you will look.

## He Stood Before the High Priest and Refused to Deny It

There is one more scene, and I want to end here because it is, to me, the most decisive. Not because Jesus finally says what his critics have been demanding. He does not. But because what he says instead is, if anything, far more devastating — and the men standing in that room knew it immediately.

Jesus is on trial for his life. The high priest, Caiaphas, has been trying to get a charge that will hold. Witnesses contradict each other. The case is falling apart. So Caiaphas does something that, under Jewish law, compelled an honest answer. He put Jesus under oath:

> *"The high priest said to him, 'I charge you under oath by the living God: Tell us if you are the Messiah, the Son of God.'"*
> — Matthew 26:63 (NIV)

This was the moment. If Jesus were merely a good teacher who had been misunderstood, this was his chance to correct the record. One sentence of denial would have saved his life. "No, I am not the Son of God. I am a prophet. I have been misquoted." The trial would have collapsed. The crucifixion would not have happened.

He said:

> *"You have said so. But I say to all of you: from now on you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of the Mighty One and coming on the clouds of heaven."*
> — Matthew 26:64 (NIV)

Now stop. Because if you read that response as a vague spiritual metaphor, you have missed what every man in that courtroom heard. Jesus was not composing poetry. He was citing scripture — specifically, and deliberately, the most electrifying messianic vision in all of Jewish prophetic literature.

Six centuries earlier, the prophet Daniel had a dream. In that dream, he watched the Ancient of Days — God himself, seated on a throne of fire — receive before him a figure described as one "like a son of man," coming with the clouds of heaven. And to this figure, the Ancient of Days gave something that belongs to God alone:

> *"And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom one that shall not be destroyed."*
> — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

All peoples. All nations. All languages. Serving him. With an everlasting dominion. Daniel was not describing a prophet. He was not describing a political liberator. He was describing a figure who receives universal worship and eternal sovereignty attributes that, in Jewish theology, belong exclusively to God. And the route to that throne runs through the clouds of heaven, which in the Hebrew prophetic tradition are not atmospheric phenomena. They are the chariot of the divine presence. God travels on clouds. When Caiaphas's court heard Jesus say "coming on the clouds of heaven," they were not picturing a weather event. They were hearing a man claim the throne of Daniel's vision.

This is why, in Mark's parallel account of the same trial, Jesus does not even wait to be asked:

> *"And Jesus said, 'I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven.'"*
> — Mark 14:62 (ESV)

"I am." There it is again — the divine name, spoken in the hall of the high priest, with the arresting officers still warm from Gethsemane. He says "I am," and then immediately fuses it with Daniel's vision of the son of man who inherits universal dominion. He is not making two separate claims. He is making one enormous claim from two directions at once: the divine name from Exodus, and the heavenly throne from Daniel. Identity and authority, braided together, delivered under oath, in front of the men who had the power to execute him.

Caiaphas tore his robes. He declared it blasphemy. The council condemned Jesus to death.

Not because Jesus refused to claim divinity. But because he claimed it so precisely drawing on the most sacred imagery in his tradition, at the most consequential moment of his life that there was no room left for the court to pretend they had misunderstood him.

He never said "I am God, worship me." He quoted Daniel 7 under oath at his own murder trial.

Which, if you think about it, is considerably harder to walk back.

Three days later, the tomb was empty.

The case does not get stronger than that. But it also does not need to. The evidence was already overwhelming before the resurrection. The resurrection simply removed any remaining reason for doubt.

## What You Are Actually Being Asked

When someone poses the "show me where Jesus said I am God" challenge, what they are really asking underneath the surface demand is a question about whether Jesus can be trusted. Whether the record can be trusted. Whether the church that has confessed his lordship for two thousand years, across every continent and culture including this one, is the product of a magnificent misunderstanding or a genuine encounter with the living God.

I grew up in a country where the supernatural is not a fringe conversation. Where the question is not whether spiritual forces are real, but which ones you should align yourself with. Where people have sought power from sources that ultimately destroy them, and where those same people have found in Christ a power that is different in kind — not coercive, not transactional, not subject to cancellation when the herbalist dies. A power that raises the dead because death does not have a final answer over the one who created life.

The claim of Jesus' divinity is not a Western theological abstraction that arrived in Nigeria on a colonial ship. It is the claim of the text itself, made in a first-century Jewish context that had no patience for theological exaggeration, confirmed by hostile witnesses, sealed by a resurrection that his enemies could not explain away, and carried across two millennia by people who staked their lives on it.

Not because Jesus said "I am God, worship me" — those exact words, in that exact sequence.

But because everything he said and did and claimed and refused to deny was, for anyone paying attention, saying exactly that.

The question was never what he said. The question is what you will do with it.
