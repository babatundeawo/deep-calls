## The Challenge in the Group Chat

Sooner or later someone in a Nigerian WhatsApp group posts it, usually late, usually with a confidence that has not yet met an answer. *Show me one place where Jesus said, "I am God, worship me." Just one. I'll wait.*

The group goes quiet. Nobody wants to type three paragraphs at midnight and be left on read. So the challenge sits there, looking like a checkmate.

It travels well, too. It turns up in lecture halls, on street corners, in the comments under debate videos, and it has an honourable pedigree. Many Muslim readers meet the idea in the Qur'an itself, where God asks Jesus whether he told people to "Take me and my mother as deities besides Allah", and Jesus answers, "It was not for me to say that to which I have no right" (Qur'an 5:116, Sahih International). So the person asking is often not playing a trick. He is repeating a conviction his whole tradition has taught him, and he deserves a straight answer, not a wounded look.

Here is the straight answer, and I will give it first so nobody has to wait. No, those words are not in the Gospels. You will not find "I am God, worship me" in Matthew, Mark, Luke or John, and a Christian who claims otherwise has not read them.

But the question quietly assumes something that isn't true, and it is worth finding out what. It assumes that if a man is God, or claims to be, he will say so in one tidy formula, as though a divine identity were a form to be filled in and stamped by the right office. People don't announce who they are like that. This essay walks through what Jesus said, what he accepted and what he did, and asks whether the missing sentence matters as much as the challenge supposes.

***

## What the Sceptic Gets Right

The strongest version of the challenge isn't the one in the group chat, so let me give that one first.

Look at the three Gospels that scholars call the Synoptics, Matthew, Mark and Luke. Jesus there is oddly reserved about himself. When a man runs up and calls him good, Jesus answers with a question.

> *"Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone."*
> — Mark 10:18 (ESV)

And when he talks about the end of the age, he says something that sits uncomfortably next to the claim that he is God.

> *"But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father."*
> — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

Then there is John. John's Jesus talks very differently, in long, high speeches about himself, and John is generally thought to be the last of the four Gospels to be written. This is where the historian Bart Ehrman presses hardest. As his Christian critics summarise him, Ehrman holds that the Jesus of history saw himself as an apocalyptic prophet announcing the coming Kingdom of God, and that the divine Jesus of John's Gospel is unhistorical. He asks, in the words Kruger quotes, "If Jesus really went around calling himself God, wouldn't the other Gospels at least mention the fact?" He argues that a major push towards seeing Jesus in divine terms was the belief that God had raised him from the dead, and, according to Evans, that Paul understood Jesus as something like an exalted angel (Ehrman, 2014; Evans, 2014; Kruger, 2014).

Ehrman's reading amounts to a story in which devotion grew upwards, step by step, from a prophet to a figure with a place beside God. That is a serious position held by a man who knows the manuscripts, and nobody should wave it away with a verse. It also has a fair point at its centre. Evans, who disagrees with Ehrman, says plainly that he doesn't rely on John in his own argument because the Gospel's genre and its historical relationship to the others are disputed (Evans, 2014). So if my whole case rested on John alone, I'd be nervous. It doesn't, and I'll say why. But the Christian who answers all this by shouting John 10:30 and going home hasn't met the argument.

***

## The Muslim Objection at Its Strongest

Since the challenge so often comes from Muslim friends, it should be put in the form they would accept. It runs in five steps.

God is one, and Jesus was among his greatest messengers. The Qur'an says so in words many Christians never read: "The Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, was but a messenger of Allah", and it adds, "do not say, 'Three'" (Qur'an 4:171, Sahih International). Jesus himself, in the verse quoted above, is made to deny that he ever told anyone to worship him. The Christian titles for him, Son of God and Lord, are then understood as honorific, much as Israel's kings were called sons of God, and the later church, under Greek influence, turned an honour into a doctrine. And the Gospels themselves keep the Father above him. He prays, he submits, and he says that the Father is "greater" than he (John 14:28). A Jesus who prays to God, the argument concludes, cannot be God.

The argument is far from stupid. People hold it with real devotion, and it asks questions Christians must answer, not dodge. The Qur'an also says something which deserves honest handling, which is that it denies that Jesus was crucified, and a Christian cannot pretend that point is minor. What this essay can do is test the five steps against the earliest evidence we have, and that evidence is Christian, Jewish and Roman, all of it written within a lifetime or so of Jesus. I will return to the specific steps as we go.

***

## Why Exact Words Are the Wrong Test

Start with a small thing about how people announce who they are. Find me the sentence where your mother says, "I am your mother." She doesn't say it. She behaves like one for decades, and the whole household would be baffled if you doubted it. Find me the sentence where a magistrate announces, "I have the authority to send you to prison." He says "Be seated" in a certain voice, and everybody sits. Authority and identity are mostly shown. A man who needs to announce them has often lost them.

The demand is also lopsided. Where in the Gospels does Jesus say "I am merely a wise teacher and nothing more"? He never does, and nobody takes that silence as proof that he thought otherwise. Where does he say "I am the Word of God made flesh"? That sentence, so beloved in Christian theology, is the evangelist's about Jesus, in the prologue of John, and not Jesus' about himself. Nobody concludes it is false because of that. A test that rejects every truth not spoken in the preferred formula will reject far too much, and I suspect the person setting it would not accept it applied to his own scripture.

There is also a more particular reason the Gospels don't hand us the sentence, and it comes from the Christian side. The philosopher William Lane Craig argues that for Jesus to say flatly "I am God" would have been "tantamount to claiming to be the Father, which would have been false and misleading", since the Father is the one who sent him (Craig, n.d.). Christian doctrine has never said that Jesus is the Father. A Jesus who prays to the Father and also shares the Father's identity has to say it in ways that keep both true. That is what we find.

It also helps with the two texts above. When Jesus asks the man why he calls him good, he asks. He doesn't say "I am not good." Whatever he is probing, the sentence is a question, and the man was throwing the word around like a compliment. And "nor the Son" in Mark 13 is what Christians have always expected from a Son who took on human limits. Craig notes that the classical view of Christ gives him a real human mind, limited in knowledge, so a Jesus who doesn't know the date is no surprise to the doctrine (Craig, n.d.).

None of this settles the matter. It only shows that the missing sentence is a weak test. The better question is what Jesus did say and do, and how the people around him reacted.

## What Happened When He Said Before Abraham Was

Take John first, since the sceptic has put it on the table. In the temple courts a dispute about Abraham ends with this.

> *"Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am."*
> — John 8:58 (ESV)

The sentence is built oddly. Abraham "was", which describes a man who came into being. Jesus says "I am", which describes someone who simply is. Most readers also hear the voice from the burning bush, where Moses is told what to say when he is asked who sent him.

> *"Say this to the people of Israel: 'I am has sent me to you.'"*
> — Exodus 3:14 (ESV)

I should be honest that scholars argue about how much weight two Greek words can carry on their own. Some think John is plainly echoing God's naming of himself. Others think the sentence is shaping a claim about existence before Abraham without quoting the bush. I wouldn't stake the case on the grammar.

I'd stake it on what happened next.

> *"So they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple."*
> — John 8:59 (ESV)

People don't reach for stones over an interesting thought about the past. Two chapters on, John lets the crowd say out loud what they thought they heard. Jesus says, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30), and they answer:

> *"It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God."*
> — John 10:33 (ESV)

Hold that sentence up against the challenge. The sceptic says Jesus never made himself God. John's hostile hearers say he did, and they knew what the words meant to them. They weren't Christians reading backwards. They were the people who wanted him gone. John says the same in plainer narration earlier in the Gospel, when he reports that Jesus was "calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God" (John 5:18), and he records Jesus adding that all should "honor the Son, just as they honor the Father" (John 5:23). Honour of that kind, in Jewish hands, belongs to one Person only.

The best known of these sayings comes at the Last Supper, and it is the one on which this page rests. Philip says, "Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us." Jesus answers him with some surprise.

> *"Whoever has seen me has seen the Father."*
> — John 14:9 (ESV)

Notice that he is not claiming a special closeness of the kind prophets enjoyed. Moses was told he could not see God's face and live. Jesus tells his friend that to look at him is to look at the Father. The same chapter holds the line that Muslim readers often quote against it, "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28), and I will take that up below, since the early church read the two sayings side by side from the beginning.

Ehrman will answer that this is John, and John is late. Fair enough. Evans would agree that it's disputed ground. So go to Mark, the earliest Gospel, and see whether the same thing is there without the long speeches.

## The Man Let Down Through the Roof

A crowd has packed a house in Capernaum so tightly that four friends cannot get a paralysed man through the door. They open the roof and lower him down in front of Jesus. Everyone is expecting a healing. Jesus says something else.

> *"Son, your sins are forgiven."*
> — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

Some scribes are sitting there, and their reaction is the evidence.

> *"Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?"*
> — Mark 2:7 (ESV)

They have the theology right. David, after his sin with Bathsheba and the murder of Uriah, prayed to God, "Against you, you only, have I sinned" (Psalm 51:4). He had wronged a husband and a wife and a household, and he still understood that the deepest offence was against the Lord. A bank can forgive a debt and a victim can forgive an assault. Nobody has the standing to wipe out the debt owed to God, except God.

A careful reader can push back here. "Your sins are forgiven" could be what scholars call a divine passive, a polite way of saying that God has forgiven you, announced by a prophet. A man can say such a thing without claiming to be God. I accept that this reading is available.

But look at how the story goes on. Jesus doesn't clarify that he is only passing along news from God. He doesn't say, "You have misheard me." He says that the Son of Man "has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10), and he backs it by telling the man to get up, which the man does. The authority is claimed as his own. The scribes' objection is exactly right on their terms, since sins against God are God's to forgive, and Jesus answers by doing the thing they said only God could do.

That is how the Gospels tend to work. They give you no slogan. They give you a man who keeps doing what, by everyone's own theology, belongs to God, and who never backs away when the audience says so. He lords it over the Sabbath, which God instituted. He says "you have heard that it was said" and then "but I say to you" over the law of Moses. He tells a storm to be still, and the disciples ask each other who he is. The Gospels are built around that question, and they don't answer it with a slogan either.

***

## Titles He Took and Titles He Let Stand

Jesus' favourite way of speaking of himself was "the Son of Man", and it is a better clue than it looks. In everyday Aramaic it could simply mean a person. But in Daniel 7 it names a figure to whom God gives everlasting rule, and Jesus uses it in sentences about authority, suffering and judgement. It is deliberately a puzzle, and the puzzle has an answer that we will reach at his trial.

Other titles he accepted. When Peter says, "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God", Jesus does not correct him. He calls him blessed, and says that the Father in heaven has revealed it (Matthew 16:16 to 17). That is no small matter, because the Gospels show Jesus rebuking his friends quickly when they get him wrong.

The title Son of God is where the Muslim objection presses. It is true that "son of God" is used in the Old Testament of Israel and of her kings, and Christians should say so. But a king was called God's son by adoption and function. In Matthew 11:27 Jesus says, "All things have been handed over to me by my Father, and no one knows the Son except the Father." A claim to unique mutual knowledge, with everything handed over, is not the language used of a king. The same writer ends the Gospel with the risen Jesus saying that "all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me", and sending his followers to baptise "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:18 to 19). One name, and three Persons within it.

Even beings who had no reason to flatter him spoke up. A man living among the tombs of the Gerasenes cried out to Jesus, "What have you to do with me, Jesus, Son of the Most High God?" (Mark 5:7). You can read that as a possessed man's distress or as the voice of what possessed him, but either way the title is given by someone who feared him.

None of these is a proof by itself, and I don't offer them as such. They are the texture of a life lived in a way that kept raising the question.

***

## Worship He Accepted

Now to the half of the challenge that is most interesting. The sceptic asks for "worship me". Jesus never says it. What do we find when someone does worship him?

First, the rule. Jesus himself quotes it to the devil: "You shall worship the Lord your God and him only shall you serve" (Matthew 4:10). It was the most basic rule in Israel. When people try to worship the wrong object, the Bible shows the right response. In Acts, when a Roman officer fell at his feet, Peter lifted him up and said, "Stand up; I too am a man" (Acts 10:26). In Revelation an angel refuses the same honour from John. Worship belongs to God, and every faithful messenger knows it.

Now watch what Jesus does. After he walks on the water, "those in the boat worshiped him, saying, 'Truly you are the Son of God'" (Matthew 14:33). He does not stop them. At the end of Matthew, the disciples meet the risen Jesus on a mountain in Galilee, and "when they saw him they worshiped him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17). He does not say, "Please, I'm only a prophet." And Thomas, the one who would not believe without touching the wounds, falls before him with the confession that has become the most compact in the New Testament.

> *"My Lord and my God!"*
> — John 20:28 (ESV)

Jesus answers him, and the answer matters as much as the confession. He does not tell Thomas that he has gone too far. He says, "Have you believed because you have seen me? Blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed" (John 20:29). A prophet who had been misheard would have corrected the man at once, for that is what the faithful do. Jesus accepted what he had spent his whole ministry teaching was due to God alone.

So here is where the challenge goes wrong. Jesus never said "worship me", and he never refused to be worshipped either. The sceptic wants an order. The Gospels show an acceptance, and for a Jew in that world the acceptance is much more telling. Anyone can issue a command. Only someone who knows what the rule is, and who stands where the rule says only God may stand, can receive worship without a flicker of correction.

***

## What He Said When It Cost Him Everything

Now to the scene where the sceptic's best card turns over.

The Synoptics, we said, are reserved. Jesus avoids bold public claims about himself, which is true for most of Mark. That makes the one place where he is asked directly, with his life at stake, hit all the harder. He has been arrested at night and brought before the high priest and a council. The witnesses against him can't agree. So the high priest asks him straight out.

> *"Again the high priest asked him, 'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?'"*
> — Mark 14:61 (ESV)

"The Blessed" was a reverent way of not saying God. If Jesus were a prophet who had been misunderstood, here was the moment to say so. One sentence of denial and the case collapses. Mark says he answers.

> *"I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven."*
> — Mark 14:62 (ESV)

He says yes. And then he reaches for a picture that every man in the room would have known, from the book of Daniel, where a figure comes with the clouds of heaven before the Ancient of Days.

> *"And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him;"*
> — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

The one who receives this is no ordinary prophet or general. All peoples and all languages serve him, which is a rule no human king in Israel's story ever held. In the Old Testament it is God who rides the clouds. And Jesus tells the court that they will see this person seated at the right hand of Power, and that he is the one.

The high priest heard it, and he wasn't confused.

> *"Then the high priest tore his robes and said, 'He has uttered blasphemy.'"*
> — Matthew 26:65 (ESV)

There is a fair question about all this, and I won't duck it. Nobody filmed the room. A sceptic can ask how anyone knew what was said at a closed hearing before a hostile council, and historians do press that point. I can't answer it by pretending we have a transcript. The accounts also differ in wording. In Matthew, Jesus says "You have said so" before he speaks of the Son of Man (Matthew 26:64), which some hear as a qualified yes and some as a deflection. Luke puts the questions in two steps and gives a more oblique answer, and I won't quote his wording here because I haven't set it beside the others. What I can say is that all three Synoptic writers have the same question put to him and the same picture of the Son of Man in his reply, and that Matthew and Mark record the charge as blasphemy. Kruger notes that Matthew and the others show the chief priests treating this identity as worthy of that charge (Kruger, 2014). A man who wanted to be remembered as a gentle teacher would hardly have been condemned for it.

So here is the turn. The challenge says Jesus never claimed to be God. The Gospels say that when the one question was put to him that he could have answered with a denial, he didn't deny it, and the men with the most to lose from getting it wrong understood him to mean exactly that.

He never said "I am God, worship me." He said "I am", and then he described himself in the language Daniel used for the one who is given everything. I find that harder to walk back than the sentence the sceptic wanted, because a sentence can be explained away as boasting, and this was said with a death sentence waiting.

## What the Church Did Within a Generation

There is a second line of evidence, and it works from the other side. If Jesus never claimed anything, one would expect devotion to him to grow slowly, the way legends do. It didn't.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians in the middle of the AD 50s, by the usual dating, which is within a generation of the crucifixion. In it he rewrites the great Jewish statement of faith that God is one. He doesn't add a second God. He puts Jesus inside the sentence.

> *"yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist."*
> — 1 Corinthians 8:6 (ESV)

All things come through Jesus. That is the work Jewish writers reserved for the Creator, and it answers the idea that Paul saw Jesus as one angel among others, because no angel made the world. In the same letter, Paul passes on a short statement of faith that he himself received, which says that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day and appeared to Cephas and then to the Twelve (1 Corinthians 15:3 to 5). Paul says "received", and many scholars think he received it in Jerusalem within a few years of the crucifixion, since he reports a visit to Peter three years after his conversion (Galatians 1:18). That puts a creed on the page long before any council.

Another early piece is the hymn in Philippians. Many scholars think it was already in use when Paul quoted it, and it holds in a few lines almost everything the sceptic says is late.

> *"Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."*
> — Philippians 2:9 to 11 (ESV)

Anyone who knew Isaiah would catch what Paul has done. In Isaiah 45:23 it is God who swears that every knee shall bow to him. Paul gives the same honour to Jesus and says that it glorifies the Father. That is the Christian way of holding one God and one Lord together, and it was said within a generation.

Richard Bauckham reads the whole New Testament this way. As his reviewer reports him, the early Christians weren't placing Jesus on a ladder of lesser beings beneath God. They were including him within the unique identity of the God of Israel, the sole Creator and sovereign Ruler, and Bauckham holds that "earliest Christology was already the highest Christology" (Bauckham, 2008; Review of Jesus and the God of Israel, n.d.).

Larry Hurtado, who spent a career looking at what early Christians actually did, came to a similar place from the direction of worship. In the words of a reviewer, he argues for an "amazingly early" date for devotion to Jesus, of unusual intensity, and he holds that those who gave it saw it as "fully within the bounds of Jewish monotheism" (Hurtado, 2003; Gathercole, 2004). Even the earliest Aramaic speaking communities called on Jesus in prayer. Paul preserves one of their cries at the end of the same letter (1 Corinthians 16:22), which the reviewer glosses as "come, Lord". Calling like that on a dead prophet would have been strange. Calling it to the risen Lord was the point.

Outside the church, a Roman governor named Pliny wrote to the emperor in about AD 112 about what he had learned from interrogating Christians in Bithynia, in what is now Turkey. They were accustomed, he says, "to meet on a fixed day before dawn and sing responsively a hymn to Christ as to a god" (Pliny, Letters 10.96). That is a hostile outsider describing what he saw, not a believer.

Here Ehrman and these scholars don't really disagree about the date. They agree that the exaltation of Jesus happened very early. The question is what began it. Ehrman points to the first followers' conviction that God had raised him. Wright asks what Jesus had done to make that conviction mean what it did. As his reviewer puts it, Wright holds that Jesus "not only proclaimed the return of God to Zion, but he embodied it in person" (Wright, 1996; Forster, n.d.). I think that is the stronger reading. A resurrection belief can explain why people were convinced of something. It's harder for it to explain why that something was this, a man given the honours of God, in a community whose whole upbringing warned them against it. On Wright's account the worship didn't come from nothing. It followed a life that had already been making the claim.

## Equal With God and Still Obedient

The Muslim objection has one more step that I promised to take up. Jesus prays. He submits. He says the Father is greater. Surely, the argument goes, God does not pray to God.

The same hymn answers it, and it is why I quoted it. Before the lines about exaltation Paul says that Christ Jesus, "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:6 to 7, ESV). The Christian claim was never that Jesus is the Father, or that a man became a god while remaining as he was. It is that the Son took on a human life, with its obedience and its limits, including a mind that did not know the day of the end. When he says the Father is greater, he is speaking from inside that servant's position, as a Son sent by a Father, and not denying what he said in John 10 and 14. The Father is greater in the order of sending, and equal in being. That is the church's reading, not a patch added later, because the early church put both sayings in the same Gospel and saw no contradiction.

What follows from this for the Qur'anic picture is a plain disagreement, and I would rather state it than blur it. The Qur'an has Jesus say that he told people only to worship God, and a Muslim reads that as the end of the matter. A Christian reads the Gospels and finds a Jesus who, as we saw, accepted worship, said that the Son must be honoured as the Father is, and went to his death rather than deny that he was the one Daniel saw. Those are two different Jesuses, and the question for either of us is which one the earliest records show. I would add that the Qur'an was recited some six centuries after the events, and the sources I have been quoting were in circulation within decades. When a friend says the Gospels were corrupted, the fair reply is to ask when, by whom, and on what manuscript evidence. The claim needs a date and a culprit, and I have not seen one.

## What Nicaea Did and Did Not Do

People sometimes say the church voted Jesus into divinity at Nicaea in 325. The record does not support it, and it is worth saying what the council actually decided.

Hundreds of bishops met there at the emperor's invitation. The quarrel began in Alexandria, in Egypt, between a presbyter named Arius, who argued that there was a time when the Son was not, and his bishop Alexander, who held that the Son had eternally coexisted with the Father and was fully equal to him (Moss, 2025). Both sides said Jesus was divine in some sense. The fight was over what sense. Alexander's camp insisted on a Greek word, *homoousios*, "of the same substance", while Arius's supporters preferred a word meaning "of a similar substance". The vote was overwhelming, and only three refused to sign, Arius and his two closest allies (Moss, 2025).

The word was new. The claim wasn't, since the bishops were defending what the church had been praying for three centuries. I should correct something I've heard preachers say, that Jesus himself was using this technical language. He wasn't. The bishops needed a precise term because Arius could use the old words while meaning something smaller.

The geography is worth noticing. Athanasius, whom later generations credit with the fiercest defence of the Nicene side, was born in Alexandria, or perhaps in the nearby Nile Delta, and he went to Nicaea as a deacon and secretary to Bishop Alexander (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). Arius, the man they opposed, was a presbyter in the same city. The Nigerian Christian who hears that Jesus' divinity is a Western import that came on a colonial ship should know that the argument which settled it was fought out among Africans on the southern shore of the Mediterranean, centuries before any ship sailed to Lagos. Thomas Oden goes much further and argues that classical Christian theology was heavily shaped by Africans. His reviewer notes that the book reads more as an outline and research agenda than a fully documented case, so I'd take the Alexandria point as established and the larger thesis as an invitation to African scholars to prove (Christianity Today, 2008; Oden, 2007).

African Christians have also thought about Jesus in their own terms. The Ghanaian theologian Kwame Bediako argued that Christ could be spoken of in African categories as the "Supreme Ancestor", the one who mediates between God and people more truly than any lineage elder, as Luka (2024) reports him. It is a bold proposal, and a fair critique, noted by Luka, is that an ancestor language risks making Jesus a progenitor and so underplaying his divinity. I mention it because the Nigerian question is rarely whether Jesus is important, since almost everyone grants that. It is whether he is God, and that is exactly the question the Gospels press.

***

## What You Do With the Evidence

If you're the person who posted the challenge, I'd like to say something to you directly. You're right that the sentence is missing. What you're really asking is whether Jesus can be trusted to have meant what the church says he meant, and that's a fair thing to ask. In a country where nearly everyone assumes the spiritual world is real, and the question is which powers to trust, it is the right question to be asking.

Try a different test. Open Mark 14 and read the trial slowly, on your own, with the phone away. Ask what you'd have to believe about a man for him to answer like that to that question on that night. Then read Mark 2 and ask the same about the man on the mat. Then read the end of John and ask why Thomas is not corrected. If Jesus was only a wise teacher, those scenes make him careless with his own life and indifferent to blasphemy. If he was a liar, he picked a strange hill to die on. If he meant what Daniel meant, then the missing sentence doesn't matter very much.

I don't think you'll reach the end of it by argument alone, and I wouldn't trust anyone who said you would. But I'd be glad to hear where you think Mark gets it wrong. Send me the verse, and we can read it together.

* **Matthew 26:64 and 26:65 (ESV).** Matthew's different wording of the answer to Caiaphas, and the charge of blasphemy.
* **Matthew 28:17 to 19 (ESV).** Worship of the risen Jesus, his claim to all authority, and the threefold name.
* **Mark 2:5, 2:7 and 2:10 (ESV).** Jesus' pronouncement of forgiveness, the scribes' objection, and his claim of authority on earth to forgive.
* **Mark 5:7 (ESV).** The man among the tombs addresses Jesus as Son of the Most High God.
* **Mark 10:18 (ESV).** The sceptic's first text, raised honestly and read as a question rather than a denial.
* **Mark 13:32 (ESV).** The sceptic's second text, read as the Son's human limits.
* **Mark 14:61 and 14:62 (ESV).** The high priest's direct question and Jesus' answer, the hinge of the argument.
* **John 5:18 and 5:23 (ESV).** The charge of making himself equal with God, and the demand that the Son be honoured as the Father is.
* **John 8:58 and 8:59 (ESV).** Jesus' "I am" before Abraham and the crowd's reaction with stones.
* **John 10:30 and 10:33 (ESV).** Jesus' claim of oneness with the Father and his hearers' stated reason for stoning him.
* **John 14:9 (ESV).** The epigraph, in which Jesus tells Philip that whoever has seen him has seen the Father.
* **John 14:28 (ESV).** "The Father is greater than I", the Muslim objection's favourite text, read as the Son's position in the order of sending.
* **John 20:28 and 20:29 (ESV).** Thomas's confession and Jesus' acceptance of it.
* **Acts 10:26 (ESV).** Peter's refusal of worship, set against Jesus' acceptance of it.
* **1 Corinthians 8:6 (ESV).** Paul's reworking of the confession that God is one, with Jesus included.
* **1 Corinthians 15:3 to 5 (ESV).** The early statement of faith that Paul received and handed on.
* **1 Corinthians 16:22.** Mentioned only, for the early Aramaic cry to the Lord, glossed from Gathercole (2004); not quoted.
* **Galatians 1:18 (ESV).** Paul's visit to Peter in Jerusalem three years after his conversion, used to date the received creed.
* **Philippians 2:6 to 11 (ESV).** The early hymn on the Son's equality with God, his self emptying and his exaltation.
* **Revelation 22:8 to 9.** Mentioned only, for the angel who refuses John's worship; not quoted.
