## The Challenge in the Group Chat

Sooner or later someone in a Nigerian WhatsApp group posts it, usually late, usually with a confidence that has not yet met an answer. *Show me one place where Jesus said, "I am God, worship me." Just one. I'll wait.*

The group goes quiet. Nobody wants to type three paragraphs at midnight and be left on read. So the challenge sits there, looking like a checkmate.

It travels well, too. It turns up in lecture halls, on street corners, in the comments under debate videos, and it has an honourable pedigree. Many Muslim readers meet the idea in the Qur'an itself, where God asks Jesus whether he told people to "worship me and my mother as gods in derogation of Allah" (Qur'an 5:116, in the Yusuf Ali translation as quoted by Dalcour, 2016), and Jesus answers that he never could have said what he had no right to say. So the person asking is often not playing a trick. He is repeating a conviction his whole tradition has taught him, and he deserves a straight answer, not a wounded look.

Here is the straight answer, and I will give it first so nobody has to wait. No, those words are not in the Gospels. You will not find "I am God, worship me" in Matthew, Mark, Luke or John, and a Christian who claims otherwise has not read them.

But the question quietly assumes something that isn't true, and it's worth finding out what.

***

## What the Sceptic Gets Right

The strongest version of the challenge isn't the one in the group chat, so let me give that one first.

Look at the three Gospels that scholars call the Synoptics, Matthew, Mark and Luke. Jesus there is oddly reserved about himself. When a man runs up and calls him good, Jesus answers with a question.

> *"Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone."*
> — Mark 10:18 (ESV)

And when he talks about the end of the age, he says something that sits uncomfortably next to the claim that he is God.

> *"But concerning that day or that hour, no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father."*
> — Mark 13:32 (ESV)

Then there is John. John's Jesus talks very differently, in long, high speeches about himself, and John is generally thought to be the last of the four Gospels to be written. This is where the historian Bart Ehrman presses hardest. As his Christian critics summarise him, Ehrman holds that the Jesus of history saw himself as an apocalyptic prophet announcing the coming Kingdom of God, and that the divine Jesus of John's Gospel is unhistorical. He asks, in the words Kruger quotes, "If Jesus really went around calling himself God, wouldn't the other Gospels at least mention the fact?" He argues that a major push towards seeing Jesus in divine terms was the belief that God had raised him from the dead, and, according to Evans, that Paul understood Jesus as something like an exalted angel (Ehrman, 2014; Evans, 2014; Kruger, 2014).

Ehrman's reading amounts to a story in which devotion grew upwards, step by step, from a prophet to a figure with a place beside God. That is a serious position held by a man who knows the manuscripts, and nobody should wave it away with a verse. It also has a fair point at its centre. Evans, who disagrees with Ehrman, says plainly that he doesn't rely on John in his own argument because the Gospel's genre and its historical relationship to the others are disputed (Evans, 2014). So if my whole case rested on John alone, I'd be nervous. It doesn't, and I'll say why. But the Christian who answers all this by shouting John 10:30 and going home hasn't met the argument.

***

## Why Exact Words Are the Wrong Test

Start with a small thing about how people announce who they are. Find me the sentence where your mother says, "I am your mother." She doesn't say it. She behaves like one for decades, and the whole household would be baffled if you doubted it. Find me the sentence where a magistrate announces, "I have the authority to send you to prison." He says "Be seated" in a certain voice, and everybody sits. Authority and identity are mostly shown. A man who needs to announce them has often lost them.

There is also a more particular reason the Gospels don't hand us the sentence, and it comes from the Christian side. The philosopher William Lane Craig argues that for Jesus to say flatly "I am God" would have been "tantamount to claiming to be the Father, which would have been false and misleading", since the Father is the one who sent him (Craig, n.d.). Christian doctrine has never said that Jesus is the Father. A Jesus who prays to the Father and also shares the Father's identity has to say it in ways that keep both true. That is what we find.

It also helps with the two texts above. When Jesus asks the man why he calls him good, he asks. He doesn't say "I am not good." Whatever he is probing, the sentence is a question, and the man was throwing the word around like a compliment. And "nor the Son" in Mark 13 is what Christians have always expected from a Son who took on human limits. Craig notes that the classical view of Christ gives him a real human mind, limited in knowledge, so a Jesus who doesn't know the date is no surprise to the doctrine (Craig, n.d.).

None of this settles the matter. It only shows that the missing sentence is a weak test. The better question is what Jesus did say and do, and how the people around him reacted.

***

## What Happened When He Said Before Abraham Was

Take John first, since the sceptic has put it on the table. In the temple courts a dispute about Abraham ends with this.

> *"Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am."*
> — John 8:58 (ESV)

The sentence is built oddly. Abraham "was", which describes a man who came into being. Jesus says "I am", which describes someone who simply is. Most readers also hear the voice from the burning bush, where Moses is told what to say when he is asked who sent him.

> *"Say this to the people of Israel: 'I am has sent me to you.'"*
> — Exodus 3:14 (ESV)

I should be honest that scholars argue about how much weight two Greek words can carry on their own. Some think John is plainly echoing God's naming of himself. Others think the sentence is shaping a claim about existence before Abraham without quoting the bush. I wouldn't stake the case on the grammar.

I'd stake it on what happened next.

> *"So they picked up stones to throw at him, but Jesus hid himself and went out of the temple."*
> — John 8:59 (ESV)

People don't reach for stones over an interesting thought about the past. Two chapters on, John lets the crowd say out loud what they thought they heard. Jesus says, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30), and they answer:

> *"It is not for a good work that we are going to stone you but for blasphemy, because you, being a man, make yourself God."*
> — John 10:33 (ESV)

Hold that sentence up against the challenge. The sceptic says Jesus never made himself God. John's hostile hearers say he did, and they knew what the words meant to them. They weren't Christians reading backwards. They were the people who wanted him gone.

Ehrman will answer that this is John, and John is late. Fair enough. Evans would agree that it's disputed ground. So go to Mark, the earliest Gospel, and see whether the same thing is there without the long speeches.

***

## The Man Let Down Through the Roof

A crowd has packed a house in Capernaum so tightly that four friends cannot get a paralysed man through the door. They open the roof and lower him down in front of Jesus. Everyone is expecting a healing. Jesus says something else.

> *"Son, your sins are forgiven."*
> — Mark 2:5 (ESV)

Some scribes are sitting there, and their reaction is the evidence.

> *"Why does this man speak like that? He is blaspheming! Who can forgive sins but God alone?"*
> — Mark 2:7 (ESV)

A careful reader can push back here. "Your sins are forgiven" could be what scholars call a divine passive, a polite way of saying that God has forgiven you, announced by a prophet. A man can say such a thing without claiming to be God. I accept that this reading is available.

But look at how the story goes on. Jesus doesn't clarify that he is only passing along news from God. He doesn't say, "You have misheard me." He says that the Son of Man "has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10), and he backs it by telling the man to get up, which the man does. The authority is claimed as his own. The scribes' objection is exactly right on their terms, since sins against God are God's to forgive, and Jesus answers by doing the thing they said only God could do.

That is how the Gospels tend to work. They give you no slogan. They give you a man who keeps doing what, by everyone's own theology, belongs to God, and who never backs away when the audience says so.

***

## What He Said When It Cost Him Everything

Now to the scene where the sceptic's best card turns over.

The Synoptics, we said, are reserved. Jesus avoids bold public claims about himself, which is true for most of Mark. That makes the one place where he is asked directly, with his life at stake, hit all the harder. He has been arrested at night and brought before the high priest and a council. The witnesses against him can't agree. So the high priest asks him straight out.

> *"Again the high priest asked him, 'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?'"*
> — Mark 14:61 (ESV)

"The Blessed" was a reverent way of not saying God. If Jesus were a prophet who had been misunderstood, here was the moment to say so. One sentence of denial and the case collapses. Mark says he answers.

> *"I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven."*
> — Mark 14:62 (ESV)

He says yes. And then he reaches for a picture that every man in the room would have known, from the book of Daniel, where a figure comes with the clouds of heaven before the Ancient of Days.

> *"And to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him;"*
> — Daniel 7:14 (ESV)

The one who receives this is no ordinary prophet or general. All peoples and all languages serve him, which is a rule no human king in Israel's story ever held. And Jesus tells the court that they will see this person seated at the right hand of Power, and that he is the one.

The high priest heard it, and he wasn't confused.

> *"Then the high priest tore his robes and said, 'He has uttered blasphemy.'"*
> — Matthew 26:65 (ESV)

There is a fair question about all this, and I won't duck it. Nobody filmed the room. A sceptic can ask how anyone knew what was said at a closed hearing before a hostile council, and historians do press that point. I can't answer it by pretending we have a transcript. The accounts also differ in wording. In Matthew, Jesus says "You have said so" before he speaks of the Son of Man (Matthew 26:64), which some hear as a qualified yes and some as a deflection. Luke puts the questions in two steps and gives a more oblique answer, and I won't quote his wording here because I haven't set it beside the others. What I can say is that all three Synoptic writers have the same question put to him and the same picture of the Son of Man in his reply, and that Matthew and Mark record the charge as blasphemy. Kruger notes that Matthew and the others show the chief priests treating this identity as worthy of that charge (Kruger, 2014). A man who wanted to be remembered as a gentle teacher would hardly have been condemned for it.

So here is the turn. The challenge says Jesus never claimed to be God. The Gospels say that when the one question was put to him that he could have answered with a denial, he didn't deny it, and the men with the most to lose from getting it wrong understood him to mean exactly that.

He never said "I am God, worship me." He said "I am", and then he described himself in the language Daniel used for the one who is given everything. I find that harder to walk back than the sentence the sceptic wanted, because a sentence can be explained away as boasting, and this was said with a death sentence waiting.

***

## What the Church Did Within a Generation

There is a second line of evidence, and it works from the other side. If Jesus never claimed anything, one would expect devotion to him to grow slowly, the way legends do. It didn't.

Paul wrote 1 Corinthians in the middle of the AD 50s, by the usual dating, which is within a generation of the crucifixion. In it he rewrites the great Jewish statement of faith that God is one. He doesn't add a second God. He puts Jesus inside the sentence.

> *"yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist."*
> — 1 Corinthians 8:6 (ESV)

All things come through Jesus. That is the work Jewish writers reserved for the Creator, and it answers the idea that Paul saw Jesus as one angel among others, because no angel made the world. Richard Bauckham reads the whole New Testament this way. As his reviewer reports him, the early Christians weren't placing Jesus on a ladder of lesser beings beneath God. They were including him within the unique identity of the God of Israel, the sole Creator and sovereign Ruler, and Bauckham holds that "earliest Christology was already the highest Christology" (Bauckham, 2008; Review of Jesus and the God of Israel, n.d.).

Larry Hurtado, who spent a career looking at what early Christians actually did, came to a similar place from the direction of worship. In the words of a reviewer, he argues for an "amazingly early" date for devotion to Jesus, of unusual intensity, and he holds that those who gave it saw it as "fully within the bounds of Jewish monotheism" (Hurtado, 2003; Gathercole, 2004). Even the earliest Aramaic speaking communities called on Jesus in prayer. Paul preserves one of their cries at the end of the same letter (1 Corinthians 16:22), which the reviewer glosses as "come, Lord". Calling like that on a dead prophet would have been strange. Calling it to the risen Lord was the point.

Outside the church, a Roman governor named Pliny wrote to the emperor in about AD 112 about what he had learned from interrogating Christians in Bithynia, in what is now Turkey. They were accustomed, he says, "to meet on a fixed day before dawn and sing responsively a hymn to Christ as to a god" (Pliny, Letters 10.96). That is a hostile outsider describing what he saw, not a believer.

Here Ehrman and these scholars don't really disagree about the date. They agree that the exaltation of Jesus happened very early. The question is what began it. Ehrman points to the first followers' conviction that God had raised him. Wright asks what Jesus had done to make that conviction mean what it did. As his reviewer puts it, Wright holds that Jesus "not only proclaimed the return of God to Zion, but he embodied it in person" (Wright, 1996; Forster, n.d.). I think that is the stronger reading. A resurrection belief can explain why people were convinced of something. It's harder for it to explain why that something was this, a man given the honours of God, in a community whose whole upbringing warned them against it. On Wright's account the worship didn't come from nothing. It followed a life that had already been making the claim.

## What Nicaea Did and Did Not Do

People sometimes say the church voted Jesus into divinity at Nicaea in 325. The record does not support it, and it is worth saying what the council actually decided.

Hundreds of bishops met there at the emperor's invitation. The quarrel began in Alexandria, in Egypt, between a presbyter named Arius, who argued that there was a time when the Son was not, and his bishop Alexander, who held that the Son had eternally coexisted with the Father and was fully equal to him (Moss, 2025). Both sides said Jesus was divine in some sense. The fight was over what sense. Alexander's camp insisted on a Greek word, *homoousios*, "of the same substance", while Arius's supporters preferred a word meaning "of a similar substance". The vote was overwhelming, and only three refused to sign, Arius and his two closest allies (Moss, 2025).

The word was new. The claim wasn't, since the bishops were defending what the church had been praying for three centuries. I should correct something I've heard preachers say, that Jesus himself was using this technical language. He wasn't. The bishops needed a precise term because Arius could use the old words while meaning something smaller.

The geography is worth noticing. Athanasius, whom later generations credit with the fiercest defence of the Nicene side, was born in Alexandria, or perhaps in the nearby Nile Delta, and he went to Nicaea as a deacon and secretary to Bishop Alexander (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). Arius, the man they opposed, was a presbyter in the same city. The Nigerian Christian who hears that Jesus' divinity is a Western import that came on a colonial ship should know that the argument which settled it was fought out among Africans on the southern shore of the Mediterranean, centuries before any ship sailed to Lagos. Thomas Oden goes much further and argues that classical Christian theology was heavily shaped by Africans. His reviewer notes that the book reads more as an outline and research agenda than a fully documented case, so I'd take the Alexandria point as established and the larger thesis as an invitation to African scholars to prove (Christianity Today, 2008; Oden, 2007).

***

## What You Do With the Evidence

If you're the person who posted the challenge, I'd like to say something to you directly. You're right that the sentence is missing. What you're really asking is whether Jesus can be trusted to have meant what the church says he meant, and that's a fair thing to ask.

Try a different test. Open Mark 14 and read the trial slowly, on your own, with the phone away. Ask what you'd have to believe about a man for him to answer like that to that question on that night. Then read Mark 2 and ask the same about the man on the mat. If Jesus was only a wise teacher, those two scenes make him careless with his own life and indifferent to blasphemy. If he was a liar, he picked a strange hill to die on. If he meant what Daniel meant, then the missing sentence doesn't matter very much.

I don't think you'll reach the end of it by argument alone, and I wouldn't trust anyone who said you would. But I'd be glad to hear where you think Mark gets it wrong. Send me the verse, and we can read it together.
