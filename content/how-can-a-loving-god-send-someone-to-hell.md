## The Question Behind the Question

The question has a tone to it. You can hear it, even when it is written on a page. *How can a loving God send someone to hell?* It arrives with arms folded. It is not really a request for information. It is a verdict dressed up as an inquiry — the conclusion already drawn, the sentence already handed down, God already in the dock awaiting your judgment on whether His actions meet your standard of decency.

I understand the impulse. I have sat with people who asked this question through tears, not through cynicism — people who had just buried someone they loved without any certainty of that person's faith, people whose grief needed somewhere to go, and the question became a container for it. That is a real thing and it deserves a gentle answer.

But I have also heard the question deployed as a rhetorical grenade lobbed into a conversation to end it, not to advance it. *How can a loving God send someone to hell?* The implication being: He cannot, therefore He does not, therefore hell is a fiction invented by controlling religious institutions to keep frightened people in line. Pack up the altar, send everyone home.

Both uses of the question deserve a serious answer. Not because the question is well formed — it is not — but because the confusion underneath it is real and the stakes are eternal.

So let us begin at the beginning: the question, as most people ask it, contains at least two broken assumptions. And you cannot answer a question correctly when the frame around it is warped.

---

## The First Broken Assumption: That Love Means Tolerance

When someone asks how a *loving* God could possibly consign a person to hell, they are carrying a particular definition of love — one so thoroughly marinated in modern sentiment that most people never stop to examine it. The God of their imagining is genial. Accommodating. Non-confrontational. The kind of deity who would not dream of making anyone uncomfortable at a dinner party, let alone in eternity. A divine yes-man. A cosmic therapist who validates every choice and challenges nothing.

The problem is that this God does not appear anywhere in Scripture. He is a construction assembled from cultural preferences, emotional needs, and a few Bible verses stripped of their surrounding context. He is, in a word, an idol. He just happens to be an idol that many people find more comfortable than the living God.

The Bible does not say that God *has* love, as though love is a trait He picked up somewhere and could theoretically put down again. It says something far more radical:

> *"God is love."*
> — 1 John 4:16b (ESV)

God does not possess love the way you possess a characteristic. God *is* the definition of love. He is its source, its standard, and its fullest expression. Which means that whatever God does is by definition an act of love. The law of non-contradiction, that bedrock of logic that even the most committed atheist philosopher cannot escape, makes this unavoidable: something cannot be both true and untrue at the same time and in the same respect. If God *is* love, He cannot simultaneously be unloving. The categories do not coexist.

Now here is where it gets uncomfortable. When we look at hell and declare that God cannot possibly be loving if He allows it, what we are actually doing is appointing ourselves as the judges of God's love. We are saying: I have a clearer understanding of what love requires than the Being who *is* love does. We have not asked a theological question. We have committed a theological coup. We have dethroned God and installed ourselves in His place — and from that elevated position, we have found Him wanting.

A West African proverb puts this precisely: the child who refuses to hear the history of the corn will end up living inside the corn in the dark, confused, wondering why everything has gone wrong. You cannot understand a thing by refusing to accept what it tells you about itself. You cannot understand God's love by substituting your own definition for His.

The first corrective, then, is this: agree with Scripture. God is love. Therefore, whatever He does — including whatever He permits in relation to hell — is consistent with that love. The moment we accept this, the question changes. It is no longer *how can God do this?* It becomes *how does this fit within a love we may not fully understand yet?* That is a much better question, and it has a much more satisfying answer.

---

## The Second Broken Assumption: That God *Sends* People There

Look at the verb in the question again. *Send.* How can a loving God *send* someone to hell?

When you send something — a letter, a package, a child to the shop for groundnuts — you are the actor. The thing being sent is passive. It has no agency in the matter. The letter does not choose to go to Lagos. The package does not negotiate its destination. To send something is to exercise complete unilateral authority over an object that has no say.

The question, as it is asked, frames the person going to hell as that letter. That package. A passive victim of divine postal service, stamped and dispatched without consent. And if that were true, then yes — the outrage would be justified. A God who arbitrarily selected people, wrote their names on an envelope, and mailed them to eternal suffering would not be love. He would be a tyrant.

But that is not what the Bible describes. Not even remotely.

> *"For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life. For God did not send his Son into the world to condemn the world, but to save the world through him. Whoever believes in him is not condemned, but whoever does not believe stands condemned already because they have not believed in the name of God's one and only Son."*
> — John 3:16-18 (NIV)

Read that last sentence carefully. Whoever does not believe stands condemned *already* — not condemned by a divine act of sending, but condemned by their own response to the truth placed before them. The trajectory toward hell is not initiated by God. It is already in motion. Christ comes not to establish condemnation but to interrupt it — to stand in the path of something that is already happening and offer a way out.

God does not send people to hell. People walk toward it, and God, at extraordinary cost to Himself, builds a road in the opposite direction and lights it with the life of His own Son. The question of how they end up in hell is not a question about God's disposition toward them. It is a question about their response to His provision.

This is not a minor semantic quibble. It is the entire architecture of the gospel. Rephrase the question correctly and it becomes: *If God is love, why do some people go to hell?* That is a question the Bible answers directly, and the answer is both humbling and clarifying.

## The Passage Everyone Quotes and Almost Nobody Finishes Reading

Romans 1 is frequently referenced in theological discussions about God's judgment, usually in fragments. Someone will mention that "the wrath of God is revealed" and then the conversation moves on before the text has had a chance to make its full argument. Let us not do that here.

> *"The wrath of God is being revealed from heaven against all the godlessness and wickedness of people, who suppress the truth by their wickedness, since what may be known about God is plain to them, because God has made it plain to them. For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities — his eternal power and divine nature — have been clearly seen, being understood from what has been made, so that people are without excuse."*
> — Romans 1:18-20 (NIV)

Three things in this passage deserve full attention, because together they dismantle the idea that hell is something God does *to* people.

The first is the phrase "suppress the truth." Paul does not say that people have not seen the truth. He says they have seen it and are actively working to hold it down — the Greek word *katechō* carries the image of restraining something that is trying to surface. The truth about God is pressing upward through the created order, through conscience, through the structure of human longing itself, and there are people who are deliberately sitting on it. This is not ignorance. This is refusal.

Atheist philosopher Thomas Nagel, in a moment of remarkable honesty, wrote in his book *The Last Word*: "It isn't just that I don't believe in God and, naturally, hope that I'm right in my belief. It's that I hope there is no God! I don't want there to be a God; I don't want the universe to be like that." What Nagel describes — and he is describing it about himself — is not intellectual conclusion. It is volitional preference. He does not want God to exist. The belief follows the want, not the other way around. Paul would recognise this immediately. The suppression of truth by wickedness has been a feature of the human condition since the garden.

The second thing to notice is that God takes the initiative to make Himself known. "God has made it plain to them." He has not hidden in theological technicalities accessible only to seminary graduates. He has written Himself into the fabric of the created world — into the precision of stellar mechanics, into the irreducible complexity of the living cell, into the moral intuition that rises involuntarily in even the most hardened human heart when it witnesses injustice. Every people group in human history has carried some version of the knowledge that there is a Creator and that they owe Him something. This is not cultural coincidence. It is, as Genesis 1:27 tells us, what it means to be made in the image of God. The knowledge is structural. It comes with the design.

The third — and this is the one that should stop us cold — is the conclusion: "so that people are without excuse." Without excuse before whom? Before the very God who says He has made Himself known to them. The judgment is not handed down by a distant, indifferent magistrate who never sent a summons. It is rendered by One who inscribed the summons into creation itself, who sent His Son as a living epistle, and who still waits with what the Bible calls *makrothumia*, a long-suffering patience that refuses to hurry toward the end for people to turn.

If anyone goes to hell, it is not because they never had access to the truth. It is because they had access and refused it.

---

## The God Who Is Also Just

There is one more dimension of God's character that the question "how can a loving God send someone to hell?" conveniently omits, and its omission is not accidental. Love is not the only thing God is.

God is also just. Perfectly, completely, non-negotiably just. And justice, by its nature, requires that wrongs be answered for. It requires adequate payment for crimes committed.

Now, here is where the Nigerian context is important, because Africans tend to understand justice in a way that some Western frameworks have lost. In our tradition, a wrong done to someone is not an abstraction. It is a rupture in relationship, in community, in the fabric of things as they should be. It cannot simply be ignored. African communal wisdom has always understood this: what a person has done cannot simply be declared undone. There are consequences. There is accountability. A world without these things is not a world of mercy. It is a world of moral chaos, where the distinction between the butcher and the saint becomes meaningless.

The Christian gospel does not argue against justice. It argues that God's justice was fully satisfied at the cross — that the eternal Son of God absorbed the full, unmitigated penalty for human sin so that no human being need face it alone.

> *"He forgave us all our sins, having cancelled the charge of our legal indebtedness, which stood against us and condemned us; he has taken it away, nailing it to the cross."*
> — Colossians 2:13b-14 (NIV)

The debt existed. Paul does not pretend otherwise. "The charge of our legal indebtedness" — this language is deliberate. There was something owed. Justice demanded it. What the cross accomplished was not the cancellation of justice but the full satisfaction of it. Christ paid what we owed, completely, so that we could receive what we had not earned, freely.

But here is the thing about a payment made on your behalf: you can refuse it. If someone settles your debt and you walk past the creditor's office every day insisting that you owe nothing and need no one's help, the payment made for you does not automatically transfer to your account. You have to receive it. You have to accept that you were in debt, that you could not pay it yourself, and that someone else has paid it. That acceptance — that surrender — is what the Bible calls faith. And Romans 6:23 is plain about what happens in its absence:

> *"For the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
> — Romans 6:23 (NIV)

The wage is earned. The gift is received. Hell is the place where people collect what their choices earned, having refused the gift that would have cancelled the debt. God does not send them there. He sent His Son to prevent them from going there. The tragedy of hell — and it is a tragedy, not a triumph — is that people arrive having been offered every alternative.

---

## Love Without Coercion Is Still Love

The final word in this argument belongs to freedom. God gave us the freedom to choose how we respond to Him — not as a design flaw, not as a theological afterthought, but as the irreducible condition of genuine love. A coerced love is not love. It is compliance. It is performance. It is the smile of a hostage.

If God forced every human being to love Him, He would have an infinitely large congregation of prisoners singing His praise with their arms behind their backs. That is not worship. That is not relationship. That is not the thing that the entire sweep of Scripture from Eden's garden to the New Jerusalem describes as the goal of creation: a free and genuine love between Creator and creature, chosen freely on both sides.

So God honours our freedom. He holds it in higher regard than many of us do ourselves. And because He honours it, He will not override our choices — not even our choice to reject Him. Not even our choice, persisted in until the last breath, to suppress the truth we know and walk toward the darkness we prefer.

C.S. Lewis had already said it better than I can: "There are only two kinds of people in the end: those who say to God, 'Thy will be done,' and those to whom God says, in the end, 'Thy will be done.'" The dreadful freedom of the second group is that God respects their decision. He does not chase them into hell to drag them out. He does not force salvation on those who have made clear, in the grammar of their whole lives, that they want no part of it.

This is not a comfortable truth. It was never meant to be. It should land on us with the weight it carries because every human being we know is somewhere on that continuum, moving toward one of two destinations, and the gospel is the only thing that changes the trajectory.

The question is not how a loving God can send someone to hell. The question — the only question that matters, the question that the whole argument has been building toward — is this: what will you do with the love He has already sent?

---

*He sent His Son. He sent the Spirit. He sent the Word. He sent the witnesses. He sent the dreams and the doubts and the dawning moments of clarity at three in the morning when the excuses run thin and the silence gets too honest to ignore.*

*He has sent everything except the bill and even that He sent His Son to pay.*

*The only thing left is your answer.*

## Biblical References

- **1 John 4:16** — Foundation for the definition of God as love itself, not merely a being who possesses love as an attribute.
- **John 3:16-18** — Establishes that God's purpose in Christ was salvation, not condemnation, and that condemnation is the default state interrupted by belief, not a punishment dispatched from above.
- **Romans 1:18-20** — The scriptural core of the argument: God has made Himself known to all people through creation; the suppression of this knowledge is volitional, not circumstantial; and therefore no one stands before God without excuse.
- **Colossians 2:13-14** — The cross as the full satisfaction of the debt of sin, cancelling the legal charge against us.
- **Romans 6:23** — The distinction between what is earned (death) and what is freely given (eternal life), which defines the difference between hell and salvation.
- **Genesis 1:27** — The imago Dei as the theological basis for the universal human knowledge of God made in His image, every person carries some structural awareness of their Creator.

## External Sources

- **C.S. Lewis, *The Great Divorce*** — Quoted directly on the nature of hell as the end state of free human choices made against God, not a divine imposition.
- **Thomas Nagel, *The Last Word* (1997)** — Quoted on the volitional nature of atheism: the desire that God not exist, which Paul's language of "suppressing the truth" anticipates by two millennia.
