## Two People Asking the Same Question

Picture a compound in Ibadan on the evening after a burial. The plastic chairs are being stacked, the generator has finally been switched off, and a young woman is sitting near the gate with her cousin. The man they buried had not been inside a church for twenty years, and nobody knows what, if anything, he said at the end. She asks it very quietly. "So how could God send a person like that to hell?" This is a scene, not a memory, but anyone who has attended a Nigerian burial will recognise the hour and the voice.

Now picture a second person. A lecture hall, or a WhatsApp group that has gone on too long. The same sentence arrives with its arms folded. Nothing is being asked. The conclusion has simply been delivered as a question. A loving God would not do this, so there is no such God, or at least no such hell, and the whole religious machine has been frightening people into pews for centuries.

The words are identical and the two people are not. One needs a hand on the shoulder and the other needs an argument, and a lazy answer fails both. So here is the objection at its strongest, in words the second person would happily sign. If God is good, he must be better than I am. I would never lock my own child out of the house for ever, however badly she behaved. If God will do what I would never do, then either he is less loving than I am, or hell is not what you say it is.

That deserves a real reply, and a real reply takes time. It also means admitting, early, that believers who love the Bible do not all agree about what hell is. I will lay out the main positions in the form their own holders would accept, because the question is asked of all of us and nobody gets to answer it with a slogan.

## What the Word Love Has to Carry

Most people who ask the question mean something very particular by love. They mean approval. A loving God, on that picture, is a pleasant presence who never makes anyone uncomfortable, and who would no more judge a man than a good host would throw out a guest. Genial, accommodating, a divine yes man. He would never dream of spoiling anyone's evening, let alone their eternity.

That God is very comfortable, and he does not appear in the Bible. He appears in the heads of people who have kept a few verses and thrown away the rest. I think it fair to call him an idol, a god cut to the measure of our feelings, and the old trouble with idols is that they never say anything we did not already want to hear.

Ordinary life does not work like that, and we know it. A father who watches his son wreck his life and says nothing, because he does not want to spoil the mood, is not being loving. Ask anyone who has queued at a bank for a pension that never arrived whether the man who took the money should face nothing at all. They will not tell you that a kind judge forgives the thief and sends everyone home. They will tell you that a judge who looks away from the theft has betrayed the pensioners. We want justice precisely because we love the people who were hurt.

So love and judgement are not strangers. But I don't want to get away that cheaply, because the Bible says something stronger than that God is loving.

> *"God is love, and whoever abides in love abides in God, and God abides in him."*
> — 1 John 4:16 (ESV)

Love is not a trait God picked up somewhere and might put down again. It is what he is, and everything he does flows from it. The objector is usually moving too fast at this point. He says that if God is love, hell is impossible. A careful reader says that if God is love, then hell must somehow be consistent with it, and asks what hell would have to be for that to hold. That is a better question than the one we began with, though it does carry a risk. When we declare that a loving God could not permit this, we have appointed ourselves judge of what love requires. It may be that we are right. It is worth noticing that we have taken the chair.

And the Bible is clear that God himself does not enjoy the subject. Through the prophet Ezekiel he says it in the first person.

> *"Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked, declares the Lord GOD, and not rather that he should turn from his way and live?"*
> — Ezekiel 18:23 (ESV)

Paul says the same of God's desire in a letter to Timothy, that he "desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:3 to 4, ESV). Whatever hell is, it is not the outcome God is working toward. Any account of it that makes him eager is wrong before it begins.

## Who Does the Sending

Look again at the verb in the question. When you send a child to the shop for groundnuts, you are the one acting and the child is the one acted upon. A letter has no say in whether it reaches Lagos. If hell works like that, with God choosing names and posting people to it, the outrage is justified, and I would share it.

John's Gospel describes something else.

> *"For God did not send his Son into the world to condemn the world, but in order that the world might be saved through him. Whoever believes in him is not condemned, but whoever does not believe is condemned already, because he has not believed in the name of the only Son of God."*
> — John 3:17 to 18 (ESV)

The word that matters is "already". The picture is of people already moving in a direction, and of someone stepping into the road to stop them. God does not post anyone to hell. He builds a road in the other direction, at a cost the Bible keeps calling his own Son, and lights it. That isn't a semantic quibble. If the question is rephrased as why some people go to hell although God is love, the Bible has a direct answer, and the answer is about what people do with what they are given.

Jesus says this about his own city, and he says it with something that sounds a lot like grief.

> *"O Jerusalem, Jerusalem, the city that kills the prophets and stones those who are sent to it! How often would I have gathered your children together as a hen gathers her brood under her wings, and you were not willing!"*
> — Matthew 23:37 (ESV)

"You were not willing." Elsewhere, to people who studied the Scriptures with great care, he says, "you refuse to come to me that you may have life" (John 5:40, ESV). The picture is a door held open and a refusal to walk through it. Nobody could read those lines and think the Lord is a magistrate who enjoys sentencing.

Paul gives the same diagnosis from the other side in Romans 1, and he is more unsettling. People, he says, suppress the truth.

> *"For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who by their unrighteousness suppress the truth."*
> — Romans 1:18 (ESV)

The Greek verb behind "suppress" can mean to hold something down, the way you hold a lid on a pot that wants to boil over. Paul adds that what can be known about God "is plain to them, because God has shown it to them", and ends the thought with "so they are without excuse" (Romans 1:19, 20, ESV). He does not say that nobody sees. He says that some people see and sit on it.

Then he describes what wrath looks like, and the verb is the surprise. Three times in the chapter, the text says that God "gave them up" (Romans 1:24, 26, 28, ESV). He hands people over to what they have been insisting on. That is a strange kind of judgement, because it looks so much like a refusal to force the issue.

Is it fair to say that of everyone who does not believe? Of course not. Plenty of people have left the faith because of what a church did to them, or because nobody gave them an honest answer in the one year they were asking. But some unbelief is a decision before it is a conclusion. The atheist philosopher Thomas Nagel, of New York University, said so about himself in *The Last Word*. "I want atheism to be true", he wrote, and he went on, "It isn't just that I don't believe in God and, naturally, hope that I'm right in my belief. It's that I hope there is no God! I don't want there to be a God; I don't want the universe to be like that." He also called it a "cosmic authority problem", and said he suspected he was not alone. That is a man telling the truth about his motives, and I respect him for it. It also shows that wanting God out of the room can be a real reason, not just the result of an argument.

## The Words the Bible Actually Uses

Before we get to the positions, a plain look at the vocabulary, because the English word "hell" is doing more work than the original languages ever gave it.

The Old Testament speaks of Sheol, the place of the dead, where good and bad alike are gathered. The Greek of the New Testament has several words. Hades is the Greek counterpart, the realm of the dead, and in Revelation 20 it is not the final destination at all, because "Death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire" (Revelation 20:14, ESV). Tartarus appears once, in 2 Peter 2:4, of fallen angels held for judgement. And then there is Gehenna, the word behind almost every warning that people remember.

Gehenna is the Greek form of the Hebrew for the Valley of Hinnom, a ravine south of Jerusalem. In the days of Judah's worst kings, children were burned there in sacrifice to foreign gods, and Jeremiah announced that it would become a "Valley of Slaughter" (Jeremiah 7:31 to 32). It occurs twelve times in the New Testament, and eleven of those are on the lips of Jesus; the twelfth is in James 3:6. You will sometimes hear that in Jesus' day the valley was the city's burning rubbish heap. The story is repeated everywhere, but I could find no first century source for it, so I would not build anything on it. What the valley certainly carried was the memory of judgement on a people who had sacrificed their children, and Jesus' listeners knew what that meant.

So a good deal of the popular picture, the red devil with a pitchfork, comes from Dante and Milton and medieval art, and not from any verse. That does not make the verses gentle. Look at what Jesus actually says, since nobody in the Bible speaks of this more often. He warns of "the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matthew 25:41, ESV). He says to fear the One "who can destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28, ESV). In Mark 9 he speaks of hell "where their worm does not die and the fire is not quenched" (Mark 9:48, ESV), borrowing the last lines of Isaiah. In Luke 16 he tells of a rich man in torment in Hades, with a great chasm fixed between him and the poor man he had ignored (Luke 16:26).

Two words deserve a closer look, because the debate turns on them. The first is *aionios*, usually translated "eternal" and related to the Greek word for an age. Conditionalists and universalists note that it can mean "of the age to come", belonging to the new era, without necessarily meaning endless. Defenders of the traditional view reply that the same word is used of life and of punishment in a single sentence, and that it is used of God himself in Romans 16:26. The second is *kolasis*, translated "punishment" in Matthew 25:46. Some scholars point out that the Greeks sometimes used it of correction, as against *timoria*, which meant punishment as vengeance. Others reply that the New Testament does not keep the distinction so tidily. I mention both without settling them, because anyone who says the vocabulary is simple has not looked at it.

## The Traditional View at Its Strongest

Now to the positions themselves. The first, and the oldest and most widely held, is that hell is endless conscious punishment. Its strongest text is a single sentence of Jesus.

> *"And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life."*
> — Matthew 25:46 (ESV)

The same Greek word, "eternal", is used for both halves. If the life lasts for ever, the argument goes, so does the punishment. The supporters add Revelation 20:10, where the devil, the beast and the false prophet "will be tormented day and night forever and ever", and 2 Thessalonians 1:9, which speaks of "the punishment of eternal destruction, away from the presence of the Lord". The image of the worm that does not die points the same way. Augustine defended this position in *The City of God*, against Christians of his own day who hoped that the punishment of the wicked might one day end. A 2000 report from the Evangelical Alliance in Britain calls it "the classic, mainstream evangelical position".

Its holders don't say it with relish. They say it because they cannot read Jesus any other way, and because they believe that sin is the rejection of a Being of infinite worth, so that its weight is not measured only by the minutes it took to commit. The strongest form of the argument adds something that gets less attention. It is that people in hell are creatures who still refuse God, and so the offence has not stopped. The punishment is not out of proportion to a finite life, because the refusal is not finished.

Its difficulty is the one the young woman at the gate feels in her stomach. How can endless suffering be the right answer to a finite life? The traditional view has a reply, which I find serious but not painless, and I will come back to it.

## Why Some Believers Hold Conditional Immortality

The second view is conditional immortality, sometimes called annihilationism, though its supporters dislike that word because it sounds as if God were swatting something. They hold that human beings are not naturally immortal, that eternal life is a gift in Christ, and that those who finally refuse him are destroyed. Their case rests on how often Scripture speaks of destruction, death and perishing for the lost. Jesus says to fear the one who can "destroy both soul and body in hell" (Matthew 10:28, ESV). Paul speaks of "eternal destruction" (2 Thessalonians 1:9, ESV). Revelation calls the lake of fire "the second death" (Revelation 20:14, ESV), and conditionalists ask a plain question. Would a second death be endless life in torment?

The American author Edward Fudge made the case at book length in *The Fire That Consumes*, first published in 1982 with a foreword by F. F. Bruce. John Stott, the Anglican leader and one of the most respected evangelical voices of the last century, said in a 1988 dialogue with David Edwards that "emotionally, I find the concept intolerable", and that he held the alternative "tentatively". The same Evangelical Alliance report that defends the traditional view calls conditionalism "a significant minority evangelical view". Its holders are not dodging Scripture. They are reading it hard, and I would be uneasy with anyone who wrote them out of the family. The traditional reply is that texts such as Matthew 25:46 and Revelation 20:10 describe conscious experience that does not end, and that "destruction" in the Bible often means ruin of a life rather than ceasing to exist. That reply is not frivolous either. This is an argument inside the household, between people who accept the same Bible and the same Christ.

## The Hope That All Will Be Saved

The third is universalism, the belief that in the end all will be saved. It comes in two forms, and they are not the same. One says that every religion leads to God, so that nobody can really be lost. Christians of every stripe reject that, and the Nigerian theologian Byang Kato, writing in the 1970s, rejected it with particular force when it appeared in the form of the claim that African traditional religions were already a road to salvation. He believed in the real knowledge of God in African hearts, and held that traditional religions "highlight the cry of the human heart, but the solution lies elsewhere". Kato, born in Kwoi in 1936 and dead by drowning in Mombasa at thirty nine, spoke of a continent "groping in complete darkness without Christ". You may find his language hard. It was not hostile. It was the language of a man who thought the stakes were eternal.

The second form is the one a thoughtful Christian actually holds, which is that through Christ, in the end, God will bring everyone home. Origen held a version of this, and Gregory of Nyssa after him. The philosopher and theologian David Bentley Hart argued it with great force in *That All Shall Be Saved* in 2019. Its best texts speak of God's wish for everyone, and of the reach of what Christ has done.

> *"The Lord is not slow to fulfill his promise as some count slowness, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish, but that all should reach repentance."*
> — 2 Peter 3:9 (ESV)

Add Romans 5:18, where Paul sets the "one act of righteousness" against the "one trespass", and the claim in Colossians 1:20 that God is pleased to reconcile "all things" to himself, and you can see why serious readers are drawn this way. If God wishes it and God is able, the argument runs, why would anyone be lost? A mother who loves her child does not stop hoping, whatever the child has done.

Augustine had an answer, and it was blunt. In *The City of God* he says that the Church has rightly condemned Origen for teaching that even the devil and his angels would be released after long suffering. The condemnation itself is a more tangled story than people usually say. A synod under the Emperor Justinian around the middle of the sixth century condemned Origen's teaching on a final restoration, and the fifteen anathemas long attributed to the Council of Constantinople in 553 are now generally thought not to have been part of that council's formal acts. And Gregory of Nyssa, though honoured as a saint in the East, was never given the same treatment, and Orthodox writers differ over how far he meant his words. So the history is not a simple verdict. It is an argument that the Church conducted at length and mostly decided against.

My own difficulty is simpler. I cannot make it fit the warnings of Jesus himself, who spoke of judgement in some of the plainest words in the Bible, and I cannot make his urgency fit a story in which everyone gets there in the end. I do think universalism has one thing right that many of us forget, which is that God wants all to be saved and will not stop wanting it. We may hope for as many as possible. We may not announce that none will be lost.

## What Purgatory Claims and What It Does Not

The fourth teaching is often mistaken for a fourth kind of hell, and it is not one. Catholic doctrine on purgatory concerns believers who die in God's grace and friendship but still imperfectly purified. It holds that they are assured of salvation and go through a final cleansing before heaven. The Catechism is careful about this, and keeps the two apart. It teaches that "We cannot be united with God unless we freely choose to love him" (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1033), and it describes hell as a state of definitive exclusion that a person brings on himself by dying in unrepented mortal sin. It states plainly that the Church "affirms the existence of hell and its eternity" (1035). It also says that "God predestines no one to go to hell", because a willing turning away and persistence in it are required (1037).

John Paul II, speaking at a general audience on 28 July 1999, said that hell is "not a punishment imposed externally by God but a development of premises already set by people in this life". That sentence is worth putting beside the earlier one from Lewis, because it comes from a pope and not from an Anglican author. Both lines agree about where the weight falls. He added that the Bible's images of hell are "symbolical language" which "must be correctly interpreted", and that they describe the emptiness of life without God. That is a restrained reading, and it is held by many Protestants as well.

Catholics point to texts such as 1 Corinthians 3:15, in which a builder whose work is burned "will suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire". Most Protestants read the passage as being about the testing of a believer's work and not about a place of purification after death, and they add that Romans 8:1 says there is "no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus", and that the writer to the Hebrews speaks of a single offering that has "perfected for all time those who are being sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14). They also note that Catholic support for the doctrine leans on 2 Maccabees, a book Protestants do not count as Scripture. I think the Protestant reading is the better one. But notice what the disagreement is about. It is about how God completes the saved. It is not about whether hell is real.

The Eastern tradition adds something worth hearing. Orthodox writers in the line of Isaac of Nineveh, a Syriac bishop of the seventh century, speak of hell as the experience of God's love by those who have refused to be loved. Isaac is quoted as saying that "those who are punished in Gehenna are scourged by the scourges of love". I could not trace the line to a particular homily, so I give it as the tradition reports it. The idea is that there is one fire, which is God himself, and that it warms those who want him and tortures those who do not. It is an arresting image and I think it is close to Lewis. It doesn't make hell any milder. It makes it more terrible, and it makes it entirely personal.

## Justice and a Debt Already Paid

Across all these views, the Bible's answer begins with the seriousness of the debt. Paul writes it flatly.

> *"For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord."*
> — Romans 6:23 (ESV)

Wages are earned, and a gift is given. A Nigerian reader may find the point easier than the Western secular one does. In our experience a wrong is never private. It is a rupture in a family or a community, and the elders do not simply declare that it never happened. They call a meeting. They ask what is owed and to whom. A village that shrugs at a killing has not become merciful, only unsafe. We know in our bones that the butcher and the saint cannot be treated alike, and that when the courts and the police fail us, as they too often do, we do not conclude that justice is a mistake. We conclude that there is not enough of it.

The Christian claim is that the cross is where the debt was paid by someone else. Paul puts the image in a courtroom.

> *"By canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross."*
> — Colossians 2:14 (ESV)

The record existed. Paul does not pretend that nothing was owed. What the cross accomplished was the full satisfaction of justice, by the one person who could afford it. A payment made for you, though, can still be refused. You can walk past the creditor's office every day insisting you owe nothing, and the account will go on standing as it did. Faith is the plain business of receiving what has been paid. Hell, in this picture, is a tragedy and not a triumph, and the tragedy is that the refusal was real and the alternative was on the table all along.

I should say honestly that this explains the shape of the thing better than it settles every case. It speaks to those who knew about the payment and declined. It says very little about those who never heard of it, and I will come to them.

## Freedom and the Locked Door

The last piece of the argument belongs to freedom. God gave us the freedom to choose how we respond to him, and it was no design flaw. Without it there is no real love. A coerced love is compliance. It is the smile of a hostage. If God forced every human being to love him, he would have a very large congregation of prisoners singing with their arms behind their backs, and that is not what Scripture describes from Eden to the New Jerusalem.

C. S. Lewis built the picture most carefully. In *The Problem of Pain* he wrote, "The doors of Hell are locked on the inside." In *The Great Divorce*, a dream vision, the dead of a grey town take a bus ride to the edge of heaven and are offered the chance to stay. The character of George MacDonald, who guides the narrator, says what you read at the top of this page: "All that are in Hell, choose it." A little later he adds that without that choice there could be no hell. The book is a dream, and Lewis does not offer it as a map of the afterlife. Tim Keller, in *The Reason for God*, draws the same line from the Christian tradition and sets it out as a life turned in on itself, carried on and on. As I read him, his point is that God does not invent a new punishment to drop on that person at the end. He lets the life reach its destination.

I find that picture true to much of what I have seen of human nature, and I think it matches Romans 1, where God hands people over to what they want. It also fits the traditional position better than people expect. The people in Lewis's grey town are not tortured. They are not able to bear what the light asks of them. And it echoes the old defence of the traditional view, that the refusal goes on and so does its consequence.

Honesty requires a warning, though. "Choice" is a big word to use of someone who was damaged as a child, or lied to by a preacher, or who died at nineteen before he had really thought about any of it. Someone who says "he chose it" at a burial has gone beyond what Scripture lets us say. Lewis's account fits those who have set their faces against God. It does not tell us who those people are. The doctrine says that the damned choose it. It does not give anyone a list.

## What I Cannot Tell You About the Dead

I don't know where that man in the compound went. Neither does his cousin, and neither does anyone who stands up at the thanksgiving service and tells the family what they hope to hear. A person's last hours are known to God and almost never to us. The thief beside Jesus asked only to be remembered, and was told, "Today you will be with me in paradise" (Luke 23:43, ESV). I'm not offering that as a rule to build on. I mention it because nobody is in a position to read a stranger's final moments from the outside.

The opposite mistake is just as quick. It is the preacher who announces a verdict on a man he barely knew, as though he sat on the bench. Scripture doesn't give us that seat.

What it does give us is Abraham. Standing before the Lord and hearing that Sodom was to be judged, he did not stay silent. He asked.

> *"Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?"*
> — Genesis 18:25 (ESV)

He asked it, as the chapter goes on, from fifty righteous down to ten, and God did not rebuke him once. Abraham was not accusing God. He was taking for granted that God would turn out to be just, and he wanted to be near while it happened. That is a better posture than the arms folded on one side or the confident verdict on the other. It admits that we are not the Judge, and it still insists that the Judge is good.

There are questions this leaves open. There are those who never heard the gospel, and children, and people whose minds were never their own. Christians have answered these differently. Some say that God judges people by the light they have, as Paul hints in Romans 2, and that Christ's work still saves those he chooses to save. Kato's own conviction was that Christ is the only way and that this is exactly why the gospel must be taken to everyone. I find that both of those things can be held together, a confidence in God's justice for those who never heard and a refusal to treat that as a reason to stop telling. I do not claim that a paragraph settles it. The only thing I would say with confidence is that the Judge of all the earth will be just, and that anyone who thinks they care more about justice than he does should be ready to be surprised.

A word about where we are standing. A survey by the Pew Research Center in 2010, the most recent of its kind I could find, reported that in Nigeria belief in heaven was nearly unanimous, at 97 per cent or more, and that in every sub Saharan country it surveyed six in ten people or more believed in hell. For most Nigerians, then, the question is rarely whether hell exists. It is whether the God who runs it is just, and what it says about him. That is a better question, and Scripture answers it.

## An Invitation for Either Kind of Asker

If you are the young woman at the gate, don't hurry yourself. Ask the question aloud, to someone who will not flinch, and keep asking it. God can take it, and so can a pastor worth his salary. You don't have to produce a verdict on the dead tonight. Grief that has somewhere honest to go is not a failure of faith.

If you are the one with the folded arms, I have a smaller request. Before you decide what sort of God sends people to hell, read the Gospel of John from the first page to the last, in one sitting, with your question in your pocket. Watch what the man who spoke most seriously about judgement actually does with his time, and who he spends it with, and where he ends up. Then come back and tell me whether the God you were accusing looks like the one in the book.
