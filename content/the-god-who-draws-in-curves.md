## A Challenge Posted in 1696

In June 1696 the Swiss mathematician Johann Bernoulli printed a puzzle in the journal *Acta Eruditorum* and invited the best minds in Europe to solve it. Take two points, one higher than the other, and join them with a track. Let a bead slide down the track under gravity alone, with no friction to spoil the sums. What shape of track gets the bead from the top to the bottom in the least time? He gave the problem a Greek name, the brachistochrone, from words meaning shortest time.

Most people who hear it give the answer you would give. A straight line. It is the shortest distance between two points, Euclid says so, and a Lagos driver who has never opened Euclid will tell you the same. Surely the shortest road is the quickest.

It isn't.

Galileo had half seen this already. According to the St Andrews history of the problem, he showed in 1638 that a path bent into two straight pieces can beat the straight line from the same start to the same finish. Then he overreached and decided the very quickest path must be a circular arc. He was wrong. The true answer, the one Bernoulli was fishing for, is the cycloid, the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling wheel.

Bernoulli had planned to give six months, and Leibniz persuaded him to allow longer, so the deadline was stretched to Easter 1697. Isaac Newton heard about the puzzle at four in the afternoon on 29 January 1697, by the usual account, and had finished by four the next morning. He sent his solution in without his name. It was printed anonymously in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society, and Bernoulli is said to have known the author at once, as one knows a lion by its claw. Jakob Bernoulli, Leibniz, Ehrenfried von Tschirnhaus and the Marquis de l'Hôpital also answered, and Johann, who had set the puzzle, reportedly needed about two weeks for his own solution. That made five solutions in all.

I have lingered on the history because it is a good story, but the story is not why it matters. The Bible has been saying something very like this about roads for far longer than mathematics has, and it says it with a good deal more honesty about what the curve costs.

## Why the Straight Line Loses

The cycloid wins because of what happens at the start. It drops steeply at first, so the bead picks up speed early, and then it carries that speed across the flatter stretch that follows. The straight track is shorter, but the bead on it spends too long going slowly. Distance was never the only thing that counted. Speed along the way counts too, and the curve is built to buy speed early and spend it later.

Anyone who has driven from Ibadan to Lagos on a Friday evening knows a version of this. There is always a driver who leaves the expressway for a side road because it looks shorter on the map, and who is not seen again until you are home. The shortest line on the map and the quickest line through the traffic are two different lines.

A metaphor is only a metaphor, and I won't push this one past what it can carry. A bead has no will, no fear and no history, and a person has all three. The curve in Bernoulli's problem is also computed by someone who can see both ends of the track. The believer on a bend in the road usually cannot. What the puzzle gives us is a single, modest lesson, that the road which looks most direct is not always the one that gets us where we are going. The rest of the argument has to come from Scripture, and from the lives of people who actually walked these roads.

## God Chose the Longer Road to Canaan

Look at what happens as Israel leaves Egypt. There was a direct way into Canaan. Archaeologists call it the Way of Horus, an ancient road lined with Egyptian military fortresses that connected Egypt and Canaan (Windle, 2022). Scripture's name for it is the way of the land of the Philistines. And here is what the text says about the choice.

> *"When Pharaoh let the people go, God did not lead them by way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near. For God said, 'Lest the people change their minds when they see war and return to Egypt.' But God led the people around by the way of the wilderness toward the Red Sea."*
> — Exodus 13:17 to 18 (ESV)

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say God overlooked the near road. It says he saw it, called it near, and still turned the people away from it, because he knew what they would do at the first sign of a fight. A people who had spent centuries as slaves were not ready to meet a war on their third day of freedom, and God, who is wise as well as all knowing, preferred a road that would form them to a road that would only shorten them. The destination was never only a piece of land. It was a people, and a people cannot be rushed the way a bead can be released.

Moses himself later reminded Israel how short the short way would have been. Deuteronomy 1:2 says it is eleven days from Horeb to Kadesh Barnea by way of Mount Seir. Preachers often attach the eleven days to the whole trip from Egypt, but the verse measures a stretch from Sinai, and the real distance needs no exaggerating. The road that Israel did take cost a generation. The contrast is already sharp enough.

And the road God chose ran straight into the sea. The crossing of the Red Sea, in the very next chapters, was not an obstacle placed on the route. It was the route. The psalmist remembers it this way.

> *"Your way was through the sea, your path through the great waters; yet your footprints were unseen."*
> — Psalm 77:19 (ESV)

Nobody on the shore could see a path in the water. It was there all the same, and it's the only kind of road some of us will ever be shown.

I should be honest about the limits of the comparison. The wilderness didn't cure Israel. When they reached the edge of Canaan, ten of the twelve scouts brought back a report of fear, and the forty years that followed are tied in the Book of Numbers to that refusal. The long road was not a guarantee that everyone on it would grow. It was a road on which growth was possible, which is a different thing, and the text offers more than one reason for the wilderness years. But the first choice, at the start, was a deliberate one. God picked the curve over the line on purpose.

## Thirteen Years Between the Pit and the Palace

If you had told Joseph at seventeen that his road to the throne room ran through a dry cistern, a slave market, a steward's house and a prison, he would have asked you to leave. Politely, perhaps. He would have pointed at his father's coat.

Genesis tells us he was seventeen when his brothers turned on him, and thirty when he stood before Pharaoh. That is thirteen years. We tell the story in a few minutes and so we feel nothing of it. Thirteen years is the whole of a young man's twenties and the end of his teens, and for a long stretch of it the only visible result of his faithfulness was another locked door. Genesis says over and over that the LORD was with him in Potiphar's house and in the prison, and that is the point worth holding. Being with a man is not the same as removing him from the pit. The text keeps those two apart.

When the road finally arrived somewhere, Joseph read it back to his brothers in one sentence.

> *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today."*
> — Genesis 50:20 (ESV)

The Hebrew behind "meant" is the same verb in both halves of the sentence, *chashav*, to plan or devise. The brothers planned something and God planned something, over the same events, and the second plan was the one that stood. Joseph does not say the brothers were innocent, and he does not say the pit was pleasant. He says the pit was inside a larger design, and that he can see it now only because the road is behind him.

That last clause is worth a second look. Joseph speaks at the end. A man in the cistern had no such sentence. He had a hole, a cold night, and the sound of brothers eating a meal nearby (Genesis 37:25 tells us they sat down to eat). Providence is usually read backwards, and the people who read it best are the ones who have been through the middle of it without a map.

## Ruth Went Out to Glean

Ruth is the quietest example in the Bible, and perhaps the most useful, because nobody in it has a vision. Naomi leaves Bethlehem in a famine and returns widowed and bereaved of both sons. She asks the women of the town not to call her Naomi, but Mara, because the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with her. She does not tidy her grief into a testimony. The writer lets her say it.

Ruth, a Moabite widow with no standing, goes out to pick up leftover grain behind the harvesters, which was the only work open to a woman like her. The narrator tells us, with a deadpan that I find hard to resist, that she happened to come to the part of the field belonging to Boaz. Nothing in the verse says God arranged it. Everything in the book says he did. By the end of the story Boaz has married her, Naomi holds a grandson, and the women of Bethlehem are praising the LORD that she is no longer without a redeemer. The last lines name that grandson's grandson as David, and with David, in the Gospel of Matthew's genealogy, Jesus himself.

Think of what it looked like on the morning Ruth walked to the field. A hungry foreign woman, a bitter mother in law, an empty house. Nobody had a plan that anyone could have drawn on a whiteboard. Providence in Ruth does not announce itself. It works through ordinary acts of loyalty, through a kinsman's obedience to the law, through a young woman who got up and went to work. The straight road, a quick end to the famine, was never on offer. The road that was on offer led, some generations later, to a baby born in Bethlehem again.

## Paul and the Doors That Closed

If you want a plain case of curves in the life of a faithful and Spirit led person, read Acts 16. Paul and his companions set out to preach in the province of Asia, and Luke tells us they were forbidden by the Holy Spirit to do so. They try to enter Bithynia, and the Spirit of Jesus does not allow it. So they walk through Phrygia and Galatia and Mysia, with two doors shut, and come down to the coast at Troas, which is about as far as you can go in that direction without a boat.

Then Paul has a night vision of a man from Macedonia, and the words he hears are, "Come over to Macedonia and help us." They go. In Philippi a businesswoman called Lydia hears the gospel and is baptised with her household, a slave girl is freed from a spirit, a jailer and his family believe after an earthquake, and the church that Paul would later call his joy and crown is planted on European soil.

Notice how the guidance came. Not as a map drawn in advance, but as a series of refusals, and then a single opening. Most of us who pray for direction expect a clear yes. Paul got two noes first. The noes were not a sign that he was out of God's will. They were the way the will was being communicated. If the Spirit had permitted the first plan, Asia would have heard the gospel from Paul at that moment, and Macedonia, as far as the text goes, would have heard it later or from someone else. God's will was not a different plan hidden behind the blocked one. It was the one that came out of the blocking.

Some preachers draw the lesson that we should simply keep moving. I think there is more to it. Paul went on walking in obedience with a door shut in front of him, and the next door opened to a man who was still on the road. A ship in motion can be steered, and a ship tied up at the quay because it wants a clearer chart cannot.

## Seventy Years in Babylon

Most of us have met a famous sentence from the book of Jeremiah on a mug or a graduation card, the one about plans for welfare, a future and a hope. The sentence is true. It is also part of a letter written to people who had just been marched to Babylon, and the verse just before it sets the timetable.

> *"For thus says the Lord: When seventy years are completed for Babylon, I will visit you, and I will fulfill to you my promise and bring you back to this place."*
> — Jeremiah 29:10 (ESV)

Seventy years. Many of the people who first read that letter would not live to see the end of it. God's plan for their welfare ran through a long exile in a foreign empire, and the letter itself told them to settle in and build, which isn't what anyone says to a people expecting rescue by Friday. In the next verse the promise of a future and a hope is made to people who would have to wait for it across a generation. The line from the mug is still there, but it has a long road attached to it.

## Nigerian Roads and Nigerian Delays

Anyone who has lived in Nigeria knows the feeling of being on a road that was supposed to be shorter. A student who gained admission to a federal university in 2022 knows that the academic staff union there stayed on strike from 14 February until it suspended the action on 13 October that year, eight months of a calendar given over to waiting (Channels Television, 2022). A graduate who has served out the national youth service year and is still sending applications knows it. A couple who have prayed for a child, or a family for a visa, or a farmer for rain, know it. And so does the contractor's victim, because let us admit that some bends on a Nigerian road are the work of providence and some are the work of a man who was paid to finish the job and did not.

That distinction matters, and I will return to it. For now I want to put one life beside it. In 1821, a boy of about twelve called Ajayi was seized in a raid in what is now south western Nigeria and sold into the Atlantic slave trade. The ship that was meant to carry him across was boarded by the Royal Navy, and he was taken to Sierra Leone, where he was educated, baptised and given the name Samuel Crowther (Wikipedia, n.d.). In 1864 he was consecrated at Canterbury as the first African bishop of the Church of England. Before he died in 1891 his work had produced a Yoruba Bible, completed in the mid 1880s.

I am not saying that God sent a slave raid. What I am saying is that the Christian history of this country has no straight line in it. The man who gave Yoruba speakers the Scriptures in their mother tongue got to the work by way of a stolen childhood, and nobody who was alive on the day of the raid, least of all the boy, could have drawn that curve in advance. The same can be said of many people who sit in our pews. The testimony nobody planned is often the only testimony there is.

## What Some Churches Promise Instead

There is a stream of Pentecostal teaching in West Africa, and in the diaspora churches that watch it by satellite and stream, that treats the curved road as a malfunction. I should say what it gets right before I say what it gets wrong. Its strongest case goes like this. The Bible is full of sudden reversals. Walls fell at Jericho, the sick were healed in a moment, a widow's jar did not run dry. People in Nigeria are tired of being told that poverty is a form of holiness, and a gospel that tells them God wants them to flourish is a relief to a person who is watching the price of garri go up. The anthropologist Daniel Jordan Smith, writing on the Nigerian prosperity churches, notes that members credit them with practical gains such as business opportunities, school admissions and housing (Smith, 2021b). That should not be waved away.

The scale of the belief is also real. Christianity Today reported in 2007 on a Pew Forum survey from 2006 in which 95 percent of Nigerian Pentecostals agreed that God will grant material prosperity to all believers who have enough faith (Christianity Today, 2007). I have not found a more recent figure that I can verify, so I leave that one as a snapshot of what was already widely believed.

My quarrel is with one inference. If God gives prosperity to people who have enough faith, then the person who is still waiting must not have enough. Delay becomes evidence against the believer. And Joseph's thirteen years, Israel's wilderness and Judah's seventy years all become, by that logic, a record of people who failed to pray hard enough. Smith has described a related puzzle in his own work. These churches voice real anger at government corruption, he writes, while also offering what he calls "divine justification for the pursuit and accumulation of wealth" (Smith, 2021a). A theology that sorts people into the blessed and the insufficiently faithful will do that sorting wherever it finds them, including on the platform.

I am not arguing against miracles. God does shorten roads. What I won't accept is a theology that reads every long road as a punishment, because it teaches people to misread their own lives. It tells the believer in her third year of waiting that something is wrong with her, when the Bible would have told her that something is being done in her.

## What the Great Traditions Say About Providence

The word providence means God's care and government of the world, and Christians have never agreed on exactly how it works, though they agree on more than their arguments suggest. It is worth setting out the main accounts in a form their own holders would accept.

The Reformed tradition speaks most forcefully. The Westminster Confession, chapter 5, begins by saying that God the great Creator of all things "doth uphold, direct, dispose, and govern all creatures, actions, and things," and later says that over the church he takes special care and "disposeth all things to the good thereof." The Heidelberg Catechism, Question 27, puts the same conviction in a pastor's voice. God's providence is his almighty and ever present power by which he upholds heaven and earth and so governs them that "leaf and blade, rain and drought, fruitful and barren years, food and drink, health and sickness, riches and poverty, indeed, all things, come to us not by chance but by his fatherly hand." Reformed teachers hold that this is compatible with real human choice, a position called compatibilism, and with God never being the author of sin. Joseph's brothers chose freely, and they are responsible, and God's purpose ran through the same act. Genesis 50:20 is the standard text.

The Arminian tradition, which in Nigeria includes Methodists and a good number of Pentecostals, affirms providence just as firmly but locates it differently. God foreknows and governs all things, and his grace goes before every human choice, but he has created persons who can really resist him, and he works his purposes through and around those choices. On this view the sin of Joseph's brothers was not in any sense decreed, though God's ability to turn it to good was always secure. Arminians point to the many passages in which God appeals, warns and waits, and they read the long detours of Scripture as the patience of a God who persuades rather than compels.

The Catholic tradition, following Thomas Aquinas, stresses God's government through created causes, so that God acts through the real working of creatures and not in competition with it. The Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 302, says creation was made "in a state of journeying" toward an ultimate perfection yet to be attained. That phrase is worth lingering over, because it makes the curve a feature of the whole created order and not only of individual lives. God guides creation toward its perfection through the working of creatures, and he permits real evil without ever willing it.

A smaller group, the open theists, argue that God knows the future as partly open and responds to it. I think the case against them is strong when it is set beside Genesis 50:20 and Acts 2:23, but they too would insist that God draws good out of bad turns, and they would say that this is why God can be trusted at all.

These accounts disagree about the machinery. They agree on three things that matter at the bend in the road. God is not absent. God is not the author of evil. And God will not waste what he allows. The believer who has all three is standing on the ground of every historic confession, whichever denomination he sits under on Sunday.

## When the Curve Does Not Resolve

Here I have to slow down, because an essay on curved paths can quietly become an essay on happy endings, and the Bible does not allow that.

Joseph's story ends well. So does Ruth's. But Hebrews 11 lists people who were tortured, mocked, imprisoned, stoned and sawn in two, and then says that all of them, having obtained approval through their faith, did not receive what was promised. Their curve did not resolve inside their lifetimes. In Acts 12 Herod kills James with the sword and, in the same chapter, an angel walks Peter out of prison. The text does not explain why the one and not the other. It simply sets them next to each other and leaves us to bear it.

The Psalms are more honest on this point than most sermons. Psalm 88 is a prayer that ends in darkness with no turn to praise. Habakkuk, looking at a failed harvest and empty stalls, resolves in chapter 3 to rejoice in the LORD anyway, and it is clear that he does not know whether the harvest will come. Ecclesiastes 7:13 says, "Consider the work of God: who can make straight what he has made crooked?" The Preacher is not being fatalistic. He is saying that there are bends God has made, and we do not always get to see round them.

So what is the believer to say to the parent who has buried a child, or the woman whose illness did not lift, or the family whose breadwinner was killed on a road or in an attack and whose children are now in school only because an uncle has taken on the fees? I don't think she should say that it was all part of the curve and that she will understand it later. She mightn't, this side of the grave. Romans 8:28 is often quoted at such moments, and the quotation needs its next verse. Paul writes that for those who love God "all things work together for good", and he goes on to say that the good he has in mind is that God's people be conformed to the image of his Son. The promise is not that every route ends in comfort. It is that no route is outside the purpose of making us like Christ, and Christ's own road went through Gethsemane and a cross.

That is as far as honesty lets me go, and it is also where the Christian offer of comfort begins. The God of the curves is not an engineer who has never walked a road. He has walked this one.

## The Cross Was the Route All Along

If you want the ultimate case of God declining the straight road, leave Joseph and Moses and go to Calvary.

The incarnation is already a curve. The one who spoke the world into being came into it as an infant, in a province of the Roman Empire, to a young woman from a town people made jokes about. He could have arrived differently, and didn't. He chose to come small.

And then he told his disciples where it was going.

> *"Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you."*
> — Matthew 16:22 (ESV)

That is Peter, and I don't read it as cowardice. It's logic. Kings do not take their thrones by way of a Roman execution. The cross looked to Peter exactly as the curved track looked to Bernoulli's readers, like the wrong answer. A moment later Jesus told him, in the same passage, that he was thinking the thoughts of men rather than of God.

The resurrection wasn't a recovery from the cross. It was where the cross had been pointed from the beginning. Three days inside the worst thing that ever happened turned out to be the quickest way to the best thing ever offered, because there was no other road by which sin could be answered and death defeated. God did not take a detour through the cross. The cross was the road.

And here is why the cross matters for everything above. It is the one place where we can see both ends of a curve at once. We know that the Friday was not the end of the story, and that the Sunday was not a surprise to the Father. Every other bend in the road we must take on trust. This one we are shown, and it is the pledge that the rest are being drawn by the same hand.

## Living on the Curve You Are On

So you are in a season that does not look like progress. A prayer you have prayed for three years has not produced what you asked. The job that should have come has not come, and neither has the result, or the visa, or the marriage, or the healing. The thing you can see clearly at the end of the road keeps curving away from you.

I won't tell you that nothing is wrong. Some bends in a Nigerian road are the work of providence, and some are the work of a contractor who was paid and did not finish. Not every delay is a design, and a person who treats every closed door as a divine lesson will sometimes sit in a bad situation far too long. Ask for wisdom. Ask a friend who loves you whether you are being formed or simply stuck. If you have chosen badly, repent and move. Providence isn't a licence for inertia, and Paul didn't sit in Troas waiting for a vision. He kept walking.

But if you have asked, and nothing in the road is your own doing, then consider what James says about trials of many kinds, that the testing of faith produces steadfastness and that steadfastness has its full effect so that you may be complete. That is the destination he names, and no straight track reaches it, because the straight track does not run through the places where it is made. Paul, writing to Rome, put it in a sentence I will quote only in part, because I could not honestly stretch it further. For those who love God, he wrote, "all things work together for good" (Romans 8:28, ESV). The good is real, and it is promised to a particular people, and it is promised over all things, including the bends.

The bead on Bernoulli's curve is not lost when it drops steeply. It is gathering something it will need further down.

So here is something to try tonight. Write out the prayer you have been praying, in the words you actually use. Under it, list what this road has already put into you that the short road would not have, the skill you were forced to learn, the friend who stayed, the habit of praying when nothing was moving. Then read the page to someone who knows you. You may still want the straight road, and God doesn't mind you asking for it. He might simply want to show you what the long one has been building while you were looking the other way.
