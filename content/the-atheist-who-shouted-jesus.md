## A bus on the Lagos to Ibadan road

Here is a scene. I have built it from pieces that anyone who has travelled that road will recognise, so please read it as a scene and not as a report.

A commercial bus leaves the park in Lagos on a weekday morning, every seat sold, and has hardly settled into the traffic when a man in the third row stands, grips the seat in front, and begins to pray over the trip. He prays for twenty five minutes. He rebukes the spirit of accidents. He declares angels along the whole length of the road. At some point he seems to address the tyres directly. Nobody asked him to stop, and nobody tries, because it is a Nigerian bus and you don't interrupt a man who is praying.

Now I want to be fair to him. The instinct is sound. The Lagos to Ibadan expressway is about 128 kilometres long, and media reports cited by Africa Check put the toll at 645 deaths over twenty seven months, and 73 deaths from 175 crashes in the first quarter of 2025 alone (Africa Check, 2026). A traveller who does not pray before that road has either not read the news or has decided that fatalism is a spiritual gift. Praying before you set out is plain good sense.

What I would ask about is the kind of praying. Some prayer comes from a person who knows God the way you know someone whose number you actually have, and it can be quiet because it is not performing for anybody. And some prayer is driven by dread, loud and long and never sure it has said enough, because stopping feels dangerous. The letter to the Hebrews describes the people Christ came to rescue in just those terms.

> *"and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by their fear of death."*
>
> — Hebrews 2:15 (NIV)

I can't see inside the man in the third row, and neither can you. He may be the most settled believer on the bus having a loud morning. Hold that thought, because the scene is not really about him.

## A joke about the satellites

Somewhere past the first toll point, a passenger two rows behind says, in the local language and loud enough for the driver to hear, that he would like to do his own ministration now. Then he turns to the driver and tells him to drive carefully and bring everybody safely, since nobody is watching over us except the satellites in space.

The bus laughs. A Muslim cleric two seats from him stops laughing first, and the temperature on the bus climbs a little. I'd have laughed too, I think, before I remembered myself. It was well made, that jab. He had kept the whole shape of a prayer, the address and the petition and the confident close, and swapped God for GPS.

Notice what he did not do. He did not argue that God is impossible, or that the evil on that road disproves him. He made a joke, and a joke is a small, cheap way of saying "I don't share your premise, and I'm not going to be bullied into pretending I do." Keep that in mind. It decides what happens next.

## Why the verses did not land

What happens next is something Nigeria produces better than anywhere I know. Half the bus turns on him. Someone gives him Genesis. Someone gives him John. A woman by the window delivers Psalm 23 with the force of a person who has known it since before she could tie her shoes. The man listens to each verse, shrugs it off, and waits for the next. He is not rude about it. He simply does not accept the premise.

And here I have to say something that will sound disloyal. He's right to refuse the method. His conclusion is wrong, but his refusal of the method is correct. If I quote a book to you as proof, and you have not agreed that the book has any standing, then I have made a claim and not an argument. A Christian who cannot feel that difference will lose the debate before anyone speaks, and not because the Bible is weak. They skipped the step that makes the Bible usable in that conversation.

The people on that bus were sincere. They had the right God. But they were building a case that the one man who needed it couldn't walk into. And anyone who has been pinned against a wall by a stranger with a Bible and a lot of breath knows how it feels from the other end. That is not an evangelist. That is a disturbance with good intentions.

There is a second thing I would say, and it matters more. Nobody on that bus had actually asked for an answer. He had made a joke. Hold that against what Peter wrote, because Peter's verse is the one people reach for at this exact point.

## What Peter meant by a defence

Most of us quote 1 Peter 3:15 as a slogan for being ready in an argument. Read it again with its neighbours and it sounds quite different.

Peter is writing to scattered exiles in Asia Minor who were being slandered, shut out and sometimes persecuted by their own families (Nichols, 2019). He tells them not to be frightened, and then he gives the instruction. The very first clause is not about arguments at all. It is about worship. In your hearts, revere Christ as Lord. The apologist's foundation is that Jesus is actually Lord of him, which is a harder thing than being clever.

The word behind "answer" is *apologia*. It is a courtroom word, the speech a person makes when called to account. Socrates gave one in Athens. Paul gave one to the crowd in Jerusalem in Acts 22 and before Festus in Acts 25, and he described his whole Roman imprisonment in Philippians as a defence of the gospel (Nichols, 2019). So Peter is not asking Christians to start fights. He is asking them to be able to stand up and explain themselves when someone with a real question puts them on the stand.

Notice who triggers it. The answer is for "everyone who asks." Peter doesn't say everyone within earshot. He expects the question to come, often because people have watched how his readers live under pressure and want to know what is holding them up. And what is asked for is a reason for the hope, which is wider than a proof of a theory. Mikel Del Rosario says the verse is about hope, a relationship and not only philosophical ideas (Del Rosario, 2019), and I think he has it right. Finally, the whole thing is to be done "with gentleness and respect." Nichols points out that the word translated respect can also mean reverence, the same word used for how we approach God (Nichols, 2019). So the person across the table from you gets treated with a trace of the awe you bring to your Lord.

Measured against that, a bus full of Christians shouting verses at a man who made a joke was a fine display of conviction and a poor display of 1 Peter 3:15. Nobody had asked for a reason, and nobody was offering one in a voice he could hear without flinching.

One more point before leaving the passage. The scene is invented, but the cost of being the dissenter on a Nigerian bus is not. Mubarak Bala, a Nigerian atheist, was convicted over Facebook posts that were found to blaspheme Islam, saw his sentence cut on appeal, and was released in January 2025 after four years in prison. One report of his release carried the headline that he feared for his life (Gazette, 2025). I raise it not to argue about the law, which is for another day, but because it should slow us down. Anyone in this country who says openly that he does not believe in God is paying a price for saying it. The least we owe such a person is the courtesy of an argument.

## How the apostles argued with strangers

Look at how the apostles actually went about it with people who did not share their book.

At Pentecost, Peter did not open with doctrine. He opened with what his hearers already knew. He told them Jesus of Nazareth was "a man attested to you by God" through works God did among them, which they had seen for themselves (Acts 2:22, ESV). The thing came first and the interpretation came second. He was standing on shared ground before he asked anyone to move, and the Acts account has him ending the day with about three thousand people baptised.

Stephen, before the council that was about to condemn him, walked his judges through their own history, from Abraham through Joseph and Moses to the prophets, and used it to show them a pattern in their own tradition of refusing the messenger God sent (Acts 7). He argued from their book, because they accepted it. He was not asking them to take his word for anything. He was asking them to read their own scroll honestly, and they killed him for it, which tells you the argument landed.

Paul on the Areopagus in Athens had a different audience, so he used a different door. He began with an altar to an unknown god, which he could see, and then quoted their poets.

> *"'For in him we live and move and have our being.' As some of your own poets have said, 'We are his offspring.'"*
>
> — Acts 17:28 (NIV)

The NIV footnotes name two sources, the Cretan philosopher Epimenides and the Cilician Stoic Aratus (Christianity.com, n.d.). Paul read pagan literature carefully enough to quote it accurately and then turned it toward the resurrection. Nobody who did that opened with "the Bible says."

None of this means Scripture drops out. It means a Christian earns the hearing. The case that the New Testament deserves to be taken seriously has real support, and it can be offered as a case. Greek manuscripts of the New Testament number about 5,795 in the tally Clay Jones cites, with Homer's *Iliad* next at 1,757 and Plato at 210, which is far more than the old figure of seven that apologists quoted for years (Jones, 2012/2013). Jones makes a point that I wish more of us took to heart, which is that many popular figures in our circles are "woefully out of date," and that being careless with them does us no favours. The New Testament still comes out by far the best attested ancient work. And the claim that Jesus rose was not a legend that took centuries to grow. Paul passes on a statement of the gospel in 1 Corinthians 15 that scholars across the spectrum, including the sceptics Bart Ehrman and Gerd Lüdemann, place before Paul wrote. Lüdemann dates the elements of the tradition to the first two years after the crucifixion, and Ehrman says the creed was already circulating before Paul's letters (Credo House, n.d.). That is a claim made in the lifetime of people who could have contradicted it.

A man who rejects the resurrection will still need to deal with that, and a verse thrown at him will not do it for him. Every Christian is an apologist in this plain sense, and no one needs a philosophy degree for it. What each of us needs is to know why we believe in terms that someone outside the faith can examine. Paul told Timothy to be a worker who handles the word of truth correctly (2 Timothy 2:15), and handling it correctly includes knowing when it is the argument and when the argument has to introduce it. On that bus, it needed an introduction.

## The tyre and the shout

Back to the bus, which has now reached one of those recently resurfaced stretches that make a driver lower his guard.

A tyre bursts.

It is loud, and the bus lurches, and for a second or two every position in the vehicle is suspended, the pastor's and the cleric's and the sceptic's. There are only people who have been told without warning that they might not arrive.

And the man who twenty minutes earlier had promoted the satellites to guardian angels shouts the name of Jesus. Not as punctuation, and not ironically. He shouts it like someone in the dark with nobody to impress.

The driver is a professional. His hands know what to do before his mind has caught up. The bus slows, and stops, and everyone is alive. Then comes the laughter that people laugh when they have been shown something about themselves and are not sure what to do with it.

It would be easy to stop here and announce that God has been proved. Plenty of Christians would. I'd rather be honest about how much a shout is worth, and the best place to start is with the people who find that announcement most irritating.

## The case against foxhole faith

The saying behind our scene is old. Quote Investigator traces the foxhole wording to an Associated Press story in April 1942, from fighting on Bataan, in which an unnamed sergeant is reported to have said there are no atheists in foxholes, and finds earlier versions from the First World War about "the front" (Quote Investigator, 2016). Nobody can say who first said it. That matters, because the saying has been used ever since as if it were a finding.

Here is the objection in the form an honest atheist would accept. First, the saying is an argument from fear to truth, and fear is a poor guide to truth. Terrified people will cling to anything, a lucky charm or a name, and nobody concludes that charms work. Second, it quietly insults a great many people who have faced death and stayed unpersuaded. The Military Association of Atheists and Freethinkers took the phrase "Atheists in Foxholes" as its own slogan to say exactly that, and in 2015 the Freedom from Religion Foundation put up a monument to atheist and freethinking veterans under that name, calling the saying a "tired, old, untrue cliché" (Wikipedia, n.d.). The novelist James Morrow, an atheist, put the point in a line I'd pay to have written, that the saying is not an argument against atheism (Wikipedia, n.d.). It is an observation about foxholes, and what it shows is that danger is distressing. Third, and this is the one I would press if I were on their side, the argument cannot be used by one party only. If a cry under terror is evidence for God, then a calm denial under no terror is evidence against him, and we would both throw the other's evidence out.

I think those points are largely right. A Christian who builds his case on the tyre has built it on sand.

Now the research, which people on both sides tend to quote in the form they like. The first well known study in this area, by Ara Norenzayan and Ian Hansen (2006), found that people reminded of their own death were more inclined to believe in supernatural agents. But the effect appeared mainly among people who already had a religious affiliation, and the nonreligious participants showed no effect (Norenzayan & Hansen, 2006). In 2012 Jonathan Jong, Jamin Halberstadt and Matthias Bluemke ran three studies with 265 university students and measured belief two ways. When asked directly, the death primed religious students reported stronger belief and the death primed nonreligious students reported stronger disbelief. When the researchers measured reaction times instead, the death primed nonreligious students were slower to deny God's existence, which the authors read as a sign of less confidence in their disbelief (ScienceDaily, 2012). A report of the wider literature put it the other way round, that atheists "showed none of these responses to thoughts of death" and "do not rely on religion when confronted with the awareness of death" (LiveScience, 2012). And Heflick and Goldenberg (2012) found that atheists' anxiety about death eased when their own belief that there is no afterlife was affirmed, which is exactly what you would see in believers too.

So the evidence is mixed in the part that matters. The folk wisdom that every sceptic cracks in the end is not safe to build on, and a thoughtful atheist who knows this literature will say we have misread our own side's data. There is a small, honest sign in the implicit results that something stirs under the stated position, but it is small, and it comes from student volunteers writing about death in a lab. It is not a burst tyre at speed. I could not find a comparable study of Nigerian atheists, which would be worth having, since nearly everything above is Western, and a Nigerian who says he does not believe in God has usually had to think harder about it than most.

## What a shout can and cannot show

What the scene can honestly claim is smaller than the proof some of us would like, and larger than nothing.

The man did not plan the shout. It came from below the positions a person builds over years, and it came in Jesus' name and not the name of a satellite. That is worth noticing, gently, and then worth asking about. Why that name? The honest answer might be culture, since in this country that is a name many people have heard since childhood. It might be more. A sceptic is entitled to say the first, and a believer is entitled to ask whether the first explains everything.

The Christian tradition has a reading of the reflex, and it is worth stating accurately, because it is easy to caricature. Paul argues in Romans 1 that what can be known about God is plain to people, because God has shown it to them, and that his invisible attributes, his eternal power and divine nature, have been clearly perceived in what he has made (Romans 1:19 and 20, ESV, quoted in part). In Romans 2 he adds that Gentiles who do not have the law still show that the work of the law is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness (Romans 2:14 and 15, ESV, quoted in part). John Calvin gave the idea a name in the sixteenth century, the *sensus divinitatis*, a sense of the divine that every person has (Calvin, 1559/1960, Book 1). Ecclesiastes says something close from the side of longing. God has made everything beautiful in its time, and he has put eternity into man's heart (Ecclesiastes 3:11, ESV, quoted in part), and the same verse goes on to say that we cannot work out what he is doing from beginning to end. A longing for what we cannot reach is a stranger description of human beings than either side of our bus would have offered.

Augustine put the same thought in the opening paragraph of his *Confessions*, writing to God that "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Augustine, 397/1996, Book 1). Notice that Augustine says it as a man who spent his twenties and early thirties chasing everything else. In the English tradition C. S. Lewis, who also spent years in unbelief, wrote that "God whispers to us in our pleasures, speaks in our conscience, but shouts in our pains" (Lewis, 1940, as quoted in Christ and Pop Culture, n.d.). In Africa, John Mbiti argued in 1969 that religion runs through African life so thoroughly that it can't be separated off as one department among others (Mbiti, 1969), which is the opposite of the Western picture of belief as a private opinion. If he is right, a Nigerian atheist is arguing against God in a country where God was never an optional extra, and that makes his position harder to hold, and more honest when held, than it would be in a society where unbelief is the quiet default.

Here the Christian reading has to be careful about what it is saying, and about what Romans does with it. Paul is not declaring that atheists secretly believe. He says that people suppress the truth, which is a more modest claim, and he says it in a chapter that builds toward Romans 2, where the person who sits in judgment on the pagans finds he is standing in the dock beside them. The passage is meant to humble a believer before it ever embarrasses a doubter. That is a good reason not to wave Romans 1 at a man who has just survived a burst tyre.

So I would put it like this. The shout is not a proof. It is a datum, and it is consistent with what the Christian claims about human beings. A sceptic is free to explain it otherwise, and then we are in the middle of a real argument that is worth having. The Lewis line suggests only that the megaphone was on. Whether the man on the bus heard anything in it is between him and God.

I should also say what the scene does not teach. It does not teach that the quiet man in the third row was a hypocrite, or that a loud prayer is a bad one. It does teach that faith which has been examined and found true can afford to be calm in the same moment that fear of death makes everyone else noisy.

## Prepared hands and an open invitation

The driver also deserves a word, since I have been so busy with the theology. God rarely suspends the road to protect us. More often he puts competent hands in the right seat at the right moment, and I would call that grace without apology. Nehemiah, rebuilding Jerusalem under threat, prayed and then posted a guard (Nehemiah 4:9). The prayer and the guard were one posture. A believer who prays and does not prepare is not holier than one who prepares and does not pray. They are differently mistaken.

That has a public edge in Nigeria today. On 30 June 2026 the Federal Government confirmed that the Ibadan end of this very expressway was, in the words of the Minister of Works, "failing very badly," and announced that the damaged section would be rebuilt in reinforced concrete (Nairametrics, 2026). Earlier, in May 2025, repairs had begun on failing expansion joints on bridges at Kara, Arepo and Magboro after a crash at Arepo (Nairametrics, 2026). I don't know what burst the tyre in my scene, and it needs no villain. But a believer who prays for his trip and says nothing about the state of the road, the condition of the tyres, or the speed of the driver has not finished praying. Part of the work of faith is to ask for what the Government is paid to supply.

So here is what I would invite you to do with all this. Next time somebody in your family, or your office, or your lecture hall says something outrageous about God, and you feel the verse rising in your throat, wait a moment. Ask them what they actually think and why. Find out what they will grant you. Find a place where you both already stand, and say something true from there, as gently as you can, and see whether they ask a second question. If they do, you are in 1 Peter 3:15 territory, and you will have earned the right to say what the Bible says. And if they never ask, you have still treated an image of God with the respect Peter asks for, and the road ahead is long.

The goal is to be the kind of person whose belief is so clearly examined and so clearly unafraid, even when the road misbehaves, that the sceptic in the next seat pays attention. Not because you prayed longer, and not because you shouted. Because when the tyre burst, you were already calm, since you knew whom you had called on before the road decided to test you.

Jesus Christ is Lord of every road, at every speed, and through every burst tyre and every joke that does not quite land. The tomb outside Jerusalem is empty, and the witnesses are on record. That is worth saying quietly to someone who has asked.
