## A Billboard on the Ibadan Expressway

Picture the billboard. It stands on the expressway out of Ibadan, a little past the point where the traffic thins and drivers start to relax. A smiling man in an excellent suit, the date of a programme called the Year of Open Doors, and in the corner, in smaller letters, *The blessing of the LORD makes rich.* Proverbs 10:22. (This is a scene, not a particular billboard, but anyone who has driven that road will recognise the species.)

Then picture the same verse arriving on your phone as a WhatsApp broadcast that has already been forwarded seventeen times, and again on Sunday, when a hand is stretched toward a financial breakthrough and the offering basket makes its third and increasingly insistent round. On this reading the verse is God's signature on a blank cheque, and the congregation's job is to find where to deposit it.

It would be easy to mock all that, and it would be unfair. The people who put verses on billboards are not all fools or crooks, and the teaching behind them has real roots. The Bible is not embarrassed by wealth. God told Abraham he would make him a great nation and make his name great (Genesis 12:2). Proverbs 22:4 says that the reward for humility and the fear of the LORD is riches and honour and life. A pastor in a country where money is short can fairly say that God is no admirer of poverty and does not hand out suffering as a spiritual credential.

And the money is short. The World Bank's Nigeria Development Update of April 2026, as the Nigerian press reported it, put poverty at 56 per cent in 2023 and 63 per cent in 2025, with a slow easing expected towards 59 per cent by 2028. The October 2026 update says the rate has stabilised for the first time since 2019, which is the kind of good news that sounds better in Abuja than in a market in Mushin, because stable here means about six in ten. A different yardstick, the UNDP's 2026 Multidimensional Poverty Index, counts 48.7 per cent of Nigerians, about 113.3 million people, as poor in 2024. The UNDP itself notes that this measure and the dollar a day kind capture different things, so the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

The measures differ and the picture isn't tidy, but the scale isn't in doubt. On every one of these yardsticks, tens of millions of Nigerians are poor, and the person reading that billboard may well be one of them. A hungry reader hears a verse about riches differently from a comfortable one, and she may plan a household budget around it. The preacher on the billboard may mean every word and want her to have hope. Hope built on a misreading is still a loan, though, and someone ends up repaying it. So it matters what the verse says.

## What the Hebrew Actually Says

Here is the verse in the English Standard Version, with its footnote.

> *“The blessing of the LORD makes rich, and he adds no sorrow with it.”*
> — Proverbs 10:22 (ESV)

The ESV adds a note on the last clause: *Or and toil adds nothing to it.* That note is the key to the verse, and most billboards leave it out.

The verb "makes rich" is the same one we meet eighteen verses earlier in the same chapter, in verse 4. And the word translated *sorrow*, etsev, is glossed in the standard lexicons as toil or a pang, so it can lean towards labour as easily as towards grief. Translators divide. Read one way, the LORD adds no sorrow to his gift. Read the other way, toil adds nothing to it. The United Bible Societies handbook written for translators prefers the second reading and points to the Good News Translation, which has it as hard work making you no richer, and the older commentators Ellicott and Pulpit offer the same alternative.

Take either reading and notice what happens to the formula. If the LORD's gift comes with no sorrow, then it is clean, and it cannot be had by exploitation or by leaving your conscience at the door. If instead toil adds nothing to it, then it cannot be manufactured, and that includes manufacturing it by planting the right seed in the right account at the right hour. A thirteen step seed formula printed in the church bulletin is toil by another name. Both readings put the source of wealth in God and the source of the formula in nobody.

That's the first and best thing the verse has to say, and it's worth having without any of the extras. What God gives, he gives all the way down. If what you are calling a blessing is making you anxious, or cruel, or someone your own mother would not recognise, it is worth asking whose name is on the cheque.

Picture a contractor in Abuja who wins a road contract by paying a fee to the right clerk, and who then pays the clerk again every quarter to keep quiet about it. The money is real. The new car is real. So is the habit of checking his phone before he answers any unknown number, and the way he now chooses his seat in a restaurant. Nothing in his life resembles the verse, because the verse describes a gift that arrives without that second invoice, and he is paying one every month. A pastor who tells him that the contract is his seed harvest has handed him a sedative, and a sedative is not a blessing.

Anyone who has watched a gifted public man fall knows how long a platform can run ahead of a person's character. The gift can go on working for years after the man has stopped being clean. That is a warning to anyone who measures blessing by how well things appear to be going. Whatever arrives with fear attached, a promotion bought with someone else's dignity or a contract that needs your silence, was never his gift to begin with.

The other side needs saying too, because honest people read this verse and feel accused. A trader who plays fair and still has a lean month has not been shown to be unblessed. Proverbs 10:22 says where clean wealth comes from. It doesn't promise that every honest ledger will be fat.

## What the Old Testament Means by Blessing

The next question is what the writer had in mind when he said blessing at all, because the English word has been worn smooth. In church talk it has become a synonym for a windfall. Ask a congregation what they are believing God for this year, and the answers will mostly be things you could put on a receipt.

The Hebrew word is *berakah*, and the verb behind it, *barak*, is first used of God speaking over living things at creation. He blesses the creatures of the sea and sky and says, be fruitful and multiply. Claus Westermann, in his study *Blessing in the Bible and the Life of the Church*, argued that blessing in Scripture is a different kind of divine action from rescue. Deliverance, like the Exodus, is God acting in a crisis. Blessing is the quiet and continuous power by which God keeps life going, in fertility, in harvests, in the health of a family and the peace of a village, handed on from one generation to the next. (I am giving the shape of his argument and not quoting him. Check the page references before anyone cites it.)

That sense is clear in the blessing that Aaron and his sons were told to speak over Israel.

> *“The LORD bless you and keep you; the LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you; the LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace.”*
> — Numbers 6:24 to 26 (ESV)

Look at what is on the list. Protection. The face of God turned toward you. Grace. Peace, which is the Hebrew *shalom*, the condition of a life with nothing broken in it. Rain and cattle and grain are certainly part of it, because the Old Testament is a book about people who farm. But the centre of gravity is a life held in the regard of God, with everything else arranged around that.

So when Proverbs 10:22 says the blessing of the LORD makes rich, it speaks as a book of wisdom whose own idea of riches is wider than a bank balance. The same collection can say that a good name is to be chosen over great riches (Proverbs 22:1), and that better is a little with righteousness than great revenues with injustice (Proverbs 16:8). A writer who says both things cannot be using the word the way the billboard does.

## Blessed So That You Can Bless

The first great blessing in the Bible after creation is given to Abraham, and its shape is the most important thing to understand about the word.

> *“And I will make of you a great nation, and I will bless you and make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you, and him who dishonors you I will curse, and in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed.”*
> — Genesis 12:2 and 3 (ESV)

Read the clause in the middle again. The blessing comes with a purpose clause attached. *So that you will be a blessing.* Abraham is not given a fortune to hold. He is given a fortune to pass along, and the end of the sentence is not Abraham at all. It is all the families of the earth. Christopher Wright, in *The Mission of God*, makes this the hinge of the whole Bible story: Genesis 12 answers the nations scattered at Babel in chapter 11, and the blessing exists for their sake. Paul reads it the same way in Galatians 3:14, where the blessing of Abraham comes to the Gentiles in Christ Jesus.

Here is the point for the billboard. In the first and fullest instance of divine blessing, the blessed person is a channel. A channel that keeps all the water at its own end has stopped being a channel. It has become a pond, and ponds go green.

That is not a theory invented by modern critics of the prosperity message. The Old Testament law builds it in. The farmer was told to leave the corners of his field for the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9 and 10). The one who was blessed with a harvest was to remember the one who had none. And when the prophets denounced Israel, the charge was seldom that the people had too little faith in their own breakthrough. It was that the wealthy had stopped being a blessing to the people beside them.

## Where Proverbs Stops Short of a Promise

Eighteen verses back, the same chapter says something that sounds almost like a rival.

> *“A slack hand causes poverty, but the hand of the diligent makes rich.”*
> — Proverbs 10:4 (ESV)

The Hebrew verb is the same in both verses, so the book doesn't think of them as rivals. The diligent hand makes rich, and the LORD's blessing makes rich. The book never imagined a person receiving blessing in a chair with folded arms. God is the source and the working hand is the channel, and the farmer who prays for rain still ploughs.

Yet Proverbs does not offer a guarantee, and the billboard needs one. The book is a collection of sayings about how life in God's world usually goes, and it is honest enough to say where the usual breaks down. Look at the prayer of Agur, near the end of the book.

> *“Remove far from me falsehood and lying; give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me, lest I be full and deny you and say, ‘Who is the LORD?’ or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God.”*
> — Proverbs 30:8 and 9 (ESV)

Agur fears being full as much as being empty. He knows that abundance can make a man forget God, and that destitution can make him a thief. Neither condition is a verdict on his standing with heaven. The poor believer is not cursed, and the rich man is not thereby blessed.

Other parts of the Bible press the same point. Job lost everything while the narrator still calls him blameless and upright. The Psalmist in Psalm 73 nearly lost his faith watching the wicked grow fat. And the eleventh chapter of Hebrews, after its great roll of the faithful who conquered kingdoms, turns without warning to others who were destitute, afflicted and mistreated, and says that the world was not worthy of them (Hebrews 11:37 and 38). Nobody who reads that chapter to the end can use a bank statement as a measure of how God regards a person.

That distinction has a pastoral edge. Turn a proverb into a promise, and the promise is something that can be collected, and when it is not collected the explanation is waiting. She lacked faith. She gave too little. She sowed in the wrong season. Picture a widow who gave all she had and is still behind on her rent. She is told the fault is hers. I think that teaching, whatever its intentions, does real harm.

Paul puts the other side of the same warning in a single stern line.

> *“But those who desire to be rich fall into temptation, into a snare, into many senseless and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction.”*
> — 1 Timothy 6:9 (ESV)

Notice that Paul says desire. He is not talking about a particular salary. A man can have a great deal of money and be free of the craving, and a man with almost none can be eaten alive by it. The Bible's concern is where the heart is pointed.

## The Prosperity Message Fairly Weighed

Since the argument has arrived at the prosperity gospel, it should be stated the way its own defenders would state it. They would say that salvation in the Bible is not a ticket to a distant heaven and nothing else. It covers the whole person, body and household and harvest, and a gospel that has nothing to say about hunger or rent is a thin gospel. They would add that African religion never separated the sacred from the material, and that the church which tells people to be content with their poverty has sometimes served the interest of those who benefit from it. Ogbu Kalu, the late Nigerian church historian, read the movement in something like that light, as an answer to an old African concern for wellbeing in this life. (That is my summary of his argument in *African Pentecostalism: An Introduction*. I have not quoted him, and the page reference should be checked.) Scholars writing in *Christianity Today* in 2007 made a related point, that for most Africans prosperity means a roof and clothes, and that it is unfair to treat every preacher in the movement as a swindler.

That is a serious position, and some of it is right. The Bible does care about bread. Jesus fed crowds. The Lord's Prayer asks for daily bread. A church that cannot say so should not be surprised when the poor go looking for one that can.

The evidence that the message is widely held is plain. The Pew Forum's 2006 survey of Pentecostals found that 95 per cent of Nigerian Pentecostals said God would grant material prosperity to believers who have enough faith, according to the *Christianity Today* report of the survey. Whatever else that figure shows, it shows that the teaching is not fringe. It is the water in which a great many Nigerian Christians swim.

Now the objections, and they come from inside the faith as well as outside it. The first is the problem of the formula. Notice the phrase in the survey question, *enough faith*. A promise that depends on how much faith you have is a promise whose failure can always be blamed on the person it failed. A first person account in *Christianity Today* in 2025 recalls a Nigerian student the writer met abroad, who put it with chilling clarity. Asked what happens if the blessing doesn't come, he said, "That would be your fault." (The article gives only his first name, Caleb.) There is a moral difficulty in a doctrine that cannot be wrong and can only be failed.

The second is the problem of the money trail. A Stears analysis of the prosperity gospel in Nigeria reports Bishop David Oyedepo as saying "If you are not a tither, you end up a beggar," and notes that, unlike Rwanda, Nigeria has no legal or financial rules governing how churches run. The same article estimates the weekly tithe of one large auditorium by assuming half the congregation gives a tenth of the minimum wage. That is an estimate, and I would not build an accusation on it. A Babcock University paper on tithing and material prosperity makes the sharper theological observation that the movement often leans on the Old Testament command to tithe while treating the rest of the Old Testament as superseded. Whether or not you accept every part of that paper, the inconsistency it names is real, and a preacher should be willing to explain it.

The third is the problem of Scripture itself. Matthew 6:33 promises that those who seek first the kingdom of God will have all these things added, but the things in the context are food and clothing, the basics a worried person fears to lose. It is not a promise of a bigger house. And the Jesus who said it also told a man with plenty of grain in his barns that his life would be required of him that night (Luke 12:16 to 21).

One more observation, and it comes from the other direction. The same *Christianity Today* article in 2025 reports that in 2011 a university service asked students to give fifty thousand naira, and when nobody could afford it the figure fell to thirty thousand, then ten thousand, then five. The writer, a student then, could not afford any of those numbers and wrote it down years later. Picture the room and do not mock anyone in it. The students were not wicked, and neither necessarily was the preacher. But a request pitched to the height of the speaker's need, and lowered when it fails, is not how Paul asked for a collection.

Which brings us to Paul.

## Seed and Harvest in Second Corinthians

Every seed sowing sermon in Nigeria leans on the ninth chapter of Paul's second letter to the Corinthians, and rightly, because it is the longest New Testament discussion of giving. It is worth reading as Paul wrote it.

The context is a collection. The church in Jerusalem was poor, and Paul was gathering money from the Gentile churches to relieve it. Notice who the money is for. It is not for Paul, and it is not for the apostolic team. In chapter 8 he holds up the Macedonians, who gave out of what he calls extreme poverty, and then he states the principle outright: the aim is that there be fairness, "your abundance at the present time should supply their need" (2 Corinthians 8:14, ESV). He backs it with the manna story, where the one who gathered much had nothing left over and the one who gathered little had no lack.

Then comes the passage everyone knows.

> *“The point is this: whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly, and whoever sows bountifully will also reap bountifully. Each one must give as he has decided in his heart, not reluctantly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver.”*
> — 2 Corinthians 9:6 and 7 (ESV)

The principle stands. Generosity is not a loss. But three features of the paragraph are usually left out of the sermon. The first is that giving here is decided in the heart, not dictated from the pulpit, and not under compulsion. A giving that is extracted by pressure, by a pastor lowering the number until the room can reach it, fails the test Paul sets.

The second is where the seed goes. It goes to a particular need, the saints in Jerusalem, and the harvest Paul promises is described in the next lines.

> *“And God is able to make all grace abound to you, so that having all sufficiency in all things at all times, you may abound in every good work.”*
> — 2 Corinthians 9:8 (ESV)

The sufficiency is given for a purpose, which is that you may abound in good work. Paul goes on to say that God supplies seed to the sower and will increase the harvest of your righteousness, and that you will be enriched in every way to be generous in every way, which produces thanksgiving to God (9:10 and 11). Read plainly, the harvest is righteousness and thanksgiving, and the enrichment is for generosity. Nobody in that paragraph is told to expect a new car. The paragraph does not forbid God to give more than you need, but it never promises it as the return on a gift.

The third is the shape of the thanks. Paul says that the Jerusalem believers will glorify God because of the obedience of your confession, and will pray for you with longing (9:13 and 14). The result of giving, in other words, is a bond between churches, with the poor praying for the generous. It looks like a body, and a body does not run on a transaction.

None of this makes Paul an enemy of Nigerian generosity, and I would not want to be one either. Anyone who has attended a wedding, a burial or a naming ceremony in this country knows how readily a household gives out of its own thin supply for a neighbour in need. The practice of giving is not the problem. The problem begins when a verse about relieving the poor is turned into a mechanism for enriching the person who collects.

## Much Given and Much Required

There is a sentence of Jesus that belongs next to Proverbs 10:22, because it describes what happens after a person receives.

> *“Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required, and from him to whom they entrusted much, they will demand the more.”*
> — Luke 12:48 (ESV)

Notice the setting. Jesus has just told the story of the rich fool who stored up grain and called it security, and he has told his disciples to sell possessions and give alms. Then he tells the parable of the manager left in charge of the household while the master is away. The faithful manager gives the servants their food at the proper time. The unfaithful one starts beating them and drinking. The line about much given and much required closes the parable. It is a sentence about stewardship, and the thing entrusted is not yours. You are holding it for someone.

Stewardship is the word that the billboard never uses. A steward does not own the estate and does not get to ask why God has given him less than his neighbour, and he does not get to ask what he can extract from the master by the right declaration. He asks what the owner wants done with what has been put in his hands.

And the entrusting is not only money. A degree is an entrusting. So is a voice, a trade, a seat on a committee, a relationship with the one man in the village who owns a tractor. Much has been given to anyone who can read this sentence, and the demand on them is correspondingly larger. That is not an unwelcome burden. It is the dignity of being treated as someone who can be trusted with something real.

So the blessing does ask something of you. It isn't a price. It comes with the gift the way a key comes with a house, and anyone who takes the house and throws away the key hasn't understood what was given.

Luke records what it looked like in the early church in Jerusalem.

> *“There was not a needy person among them, for as many as were owners of lands or houses sold them and brought the proceeds of what was sold and laid it at the apostles' feet, and it was distributed to each as any had need.”*
> — Acts 4:34 and 35 (ESV)

I wouldn't read that as a command to sell every house. What it shows is what a community looks like when the blessing of God circulates, and the measure is plain enough. Nobody who belonged was needy. Put that beside six in ten and a church could ask itself a plain question, without any scolding in it. If the LORD's blessing really were landing among us, how many people in our own pews would still be going without?

## The Word and the Widows in Acts 6

There's an odd detail in what comes after. The first quarrel Luke reports inside that church is about food. Greek speaking widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution, and the apostles were expected to fix it. Their reply is the part that gets quoted.

> *“It is not right that we should give up preaching the word of God to serve tables. Therefore, brothers, pick out from among you seven men of good repute, full of the Spirit and of wisdom, whom we will appoint to this duty. But we will devote ourselves to prayer and to the ministry of the word.”*
> — Acts 6:2 to 4 (ESV)

People use that passage to explain why senior pastors should be kept clear of administration. Read it again. The apostles did not call the widows a distraction. They said the matter was serious enough to need seven men who were full of the Spirit and wisdom, the very qualities they wanted for themselves. Feeding the widows was not a lesser calling than teaching. It was a different gift aimed at the same God. And the apostles were not guarding a diary. The verb for devoting themselves means to continue steadfastly, and any teacher knows that a sermon on Sunday is the visible end of days in the text and on the knees.

Then comes the line most people stop before.

> *“And the word of God continued to increase, and the number of the disciples multiplied greatly in Jerusalem, and a great many of the priests became obedient to the faith.”*
> — Acts 6:7 (ESV)

A church that guarded the pulpit and fed the widows grew, and it grew even among the priests, the people with most to lose from the gospel's advance. A congregation that guards the pulpit and ignores the kitchen has read only half of Acts 6, and so has one that runs the kitchen and neglects the pulpit.

## Handling the Word as a Worker Does

All of this rests on one assumption, that the person is reading the text and not merely mining it. Most of us were taught, one way or another, to use Scripture like a vending machine. Insert the right verse, in the right posture, with enough fervour, and out comes the result. The technical word for reading into a text what you carried in with you is *eisegesis*. It is like diagnosing yourself before the appointment and then hearing everything the doctor says as agreement. You leave satisfied, and quite often wrong. The patient alternative, *exegesis*, is to let the text say something you did not already think.

Paul wrote two letters to a young pastor, Timothy, and they amount to a manual for exactly this.

> *“Do your best to present yourself to God as one approved, a worker who has no need to be ashamed, rightly handling the word of truth.”*
> — 2 Timothy 2:15 (ESV)

The older English version says *study*, and the word fits. It carries effort applied over time, the kind that makes a person look back on their younger understanding with mild embarrassment. In the same chapter Paul compares the worker to a soldier, who does not get tangled in civilian pursuits because his aim is to please the one who enlisted him (2:4). Someone who handles the word seriously has accepted a deployment. It is not a hobby, like stamps or old cars.

And he compares the worker to a household vessel. In 2:21 a person who cleanses himself from what is dishonourable becomes a vessel for honourable use, set apart, useful to the master of the house, ready for every good work. Picture the good plates that come out only for guests, washed carefully, kept apart from daily use. What makes them fit is not size or beauty but cleanness and readiness. This is where Nigerian Pentecostalism at its best has long been stronger than its Western cousins, in knowing that consecration is not ceremonial. A teacher who quarters his conscience in a corner and treats his private life as unconnected to his ministry will find that the disconnect shows. Maybe not at once. But it shows. Gifts are given without being earned, and character has to be built, day by day.

## Zeal Needs Knowledge Behind It

Nobody could accuse us of lacking zeal, and nobody should want to. We have the vigils and the forty day fasts and the mountain prayer meetings, the stadium revivals and the prayer chains that run across WhatsApp groups and time zones. The fervour is real, and so, often, is the presence of God in those rooms. Paul had something to say to a people in exactly that position.

> *“For I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge.”*
> — Romans 10:2 (ESV)

He was writing about his own Jewish kinsmen, and it would be cheap to treat his words as a slap at someone else's church. They apply to anyone whose passion has outrun their reading. Hosea says something similar to a nation whose teachers had let the knowledge of God lapse.

> *“My people are destroyed for lack of knowledge; because you have rejected knowledge, I reject you from being a priest to me.”*
> — Hosea 4:6 (ESV)

The word for destroyed means cut off, brought to silence. And the cause is not a shortage of passion. It is a refusal of the plain labour of learning what God said. It isn't a charge confined to preachers, either. The congregation that never opens the book is just as exposed, and a verse treated as a coin for a vending machine will occasionally drop something, and then the habit hardens.

What the reader finds when she does open it is a different posture altogether. The psalmist who wrote the forty second Psalm did not come to the text with demands.

> *“As a deer pants for flowing streams, so pants my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God.”*
> — Psalm 42:1 and 2 (ESV)

He came thirsty. And Paul tells the Corinthians that the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God (1 Corinthians 2:10). *Depths* is the word, the water you find by diving and not by wading. Familiar verses begin to yield what you had been reading past for years. A text you learned at six carries the weight it was always carrying, and you wonder how you missed it. A verse read slowly, with its footnote and its neighbours, may disappoint the billboard and still feed the reader.

So here is a small task for anyone willing. The next time you pass that billboard, read Proverbs 10 through to the end, with a pen. Mark every verse about the diligent hand, the honest word and the open hand, and see how many it takes before you notice that the blessing of the LORD has company. Then ask yourself a question the billboard will never print. If that blessing arrived in your account on Monday morning, and it came clean, with no sorrow attached and no hidden invoice, who would you ring first?
