## The Queue Outside the Filling Station

Picture a filling station on a major road in any Nigerian city, in one of those weeks when the queue runs past the church, past the mosque, past the primary school and round a corner it has no business reaching. Children do their homework on car bonnets. Somebody's phone is on ten per cent. A man in a white shirt has been there since five, and he is not leaving, because leaving would mean the hours he has already spent were wasted. (This is a scene, not a memory. Anyone who has lived through a fuel scarcity will know it without being told.)

Ask anyone in that queue what they're waiting for and they'll say fuel. Ask what they're really waiting for and, if they're honest, the answer gets bigger. A leader, a policy, an election, a coup in the bad old days, a prophecy, anything that will finally make the waiting stop. We don't call it faith when we do this to politicians. We should. It has every feature of faith. There's an object we can't control, an outcome we can't verify, and a willingness to keep believing after each earlier disappointment.

So here is the uncomfortable thing I want to say, as gently and as firmly as I can. The rescue you are queuing for isn't coming in the form you expect. That is not because Nigeria is beyond help. It is because the Bible never describes help arriving that way.

## What We Keep Waiting For

Look at the pump, since it's where most of us feel the weather. In September, Reuters reported that petrol had risen to about N1,400 a litre in Lagos and Abuja, from about N1,200 a month earlier, with some northern stations charging up to N1,500, after the Dangote refinery lifted its gantry price to N1,350. Reuters put the pressure down to Middle East tensions pushing up crude. In early October the government announced a temporary discount at NNPC stations and said it was negotiating a ceiling of N1,350 a litre, though the finance minister insisted that "it's not a subsidy". The National Bureau of Statistics put headline inflation for September at 14.63 per cent, down from 15.39 per cent in August, even as energy inflation more than doubled to 10.85 per cent. A truck driver in Abuja told Reuters that after buying fuel there is very little left for his family.

I've given you those figures because nobody who has stood in that queue needs convincing that it's hard. The point I want to take from them is a different one. A refinery built by a Nigerian businessman, running at a reported 700,000 barrels a day, was supposed to change the story, and it has changed some of it. But the price still moves when the world's oil moves and when depot owners and the government haggle. One rich man, however capable, isn't a rescue either. I'm not asking anyone to worship Aliko Dangote. I'm asking you to notice what his refinery did and did not do. It showed that something large can be built by a person who got up and worked instead of waiting for the state, and it showed how quickly that relief is hostage to forces nobody in the queue controls.

And the next rescuer already has a date. INEC's revised timetable puts the presidential and National Assembly elections on 16 January 2027, with governorship and state assembly elections on 6 February. The commission has published a final list of eighteen presidential candidates. The liturgy has started up again, and it is an old one. Every election season Nigerians sing a hymn with no creed. Baba is coming. This time is different. He understands suffering, or he used to be poor, or he has a doctorate, or he says the right things at the right rally, or he simply hasn't had the chance yet to disappoint us as the last three did. We sing it with the sincerity of people who've never stopped to ask why the miracle keeps missing its appointment.

I'm not telling you whom to vote for, and I won't. Voting matters, and so does leadership, and I'll argue that harder further down. But there's a difference between choosing a leader carefully and handing your whole moral life to whoever wins, then blaming him for every pothole and every hour of darkness while you wait and complain, as though citizenship were something you watched from the stands.

## Why Israel Wanted a King

Read 1 Samuel 8 slowly, the way you'd read a letter from someone who knows you too well. Samuel is old, and his sons are taking bribes. The elders of Israel come to him with an understandable request: make us a king to judge us. Good government is a reasonable thing to want. So what went wrong?

Samuel is hurt and takes it to God. God tells him that the elders haven't rejected Samuel. They have rejected God as their king. Then God has Samuel list what a king will do. He will take their sons for his chariots and armies and their daughters for his kitchens, the best of their fields, and a tenth of their grain and flocks. The day will come when they cry out because of the king they chose, and God says he will not answer them on that day. The elders hear all this, and they answer with something I find chilling in its ordinariness.

> *"that we also may be like all the nations; and that our king may judge us, and go out before us, and fight our battles."*
>
> — 1 Samuel 8:20 (KJV)

Fight our battles. That is the sentence. I should be fair here, because it would be easy to read this passage as a ban on government. It isn't one. Deuteronomy 17 makes provision for a king, and Israel's story later has good ones. Nor does 1 Samuel 8 say that wanting justice is a sin. The trouble is what the elders wanted the king to do for them. They were a covenant people with a law, with elders, with prophets, and with a God who had brought them out of Egypt, and they had grown tired of the slow shared work of keeping faith with one another. They wanted a shortcut with a crown on it. Nigeria has been shopping for that same shortcut at every election since 1999, under a different name. We call it the next administration.

## What Ezekiel Says to the Shepherds

None of this lets rulers off. Scripture isn't that polite to the powerful.

> *"Son of man, prophesy against the shepherds of Israel, prophesy, and say unto them, Thus saith the Lord GOD unto the shepherds; Woe be to the shepherds of Israel that do feed themselves! should not the shepherds feed the flocks?"*
>
> — Ezekiel 34:2 (KJV)

The prophet goes on to list what these shepherds failed to do. They didn't strengthen the weak, heal the sick or bind up the injured, and they didn't go after the strays. They ruled the sheep harshly and ate well themselves. Picture a state where the same borehole is commissioned before every election, and somewhere a contractor is putting up a bigger signboard each time. Nobody needs a degree in theology to recognise Ezekiel's complaint in that picture. The accusation is aimed at a system of leadership that treats a people as something to harvest.

The outside world reads the system much as Ezekiel would. Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index for 2025 gave Nigeria 26 out of 100, unchanged from the year before, and a rank of 142nd out of 182 countries, two places lower than the 140th it held for 2024. A score like that proves nothing against any one office holder, and I wouldn't use it to accuse anyone. What it records is a perception of the system, and the system is what Ezekiel was addressing. The national grid, which the Nigerian Independent System Operator's data showed falling to zero megawatts on the afternoon of 23 January, is another kind of evidence, one you can't argue with by candlelight.

Ezekiel 34 doesn't stop at the shepherds' condemnation, though. It turns to God saying that he himself will search for his sheep and seek them out. Christians read that promise as fulfilled when Jesus called himself the good shepherd (John 10:11). I'm not saying nobody has come. I'm saying nobody is coming to do your part of the work for you, and that the promise of a shepherd was never a licence for the flock to sit down.

## Achebe Was Half Right

Chinua Achebe made the same diagnosis in *The Trouble with Nigeria*, published in 1983, in a sentence people have been quoting ever since.

> *"The trouble with Nigeria is simply and squarely a failure of leadership."*
>
> — Chinua Achebe, *The Trouble with Nigeria* (1983)

He went on to insist that there was nothing wrong with the Nigerian land, climate, water or air. The problem, as he put it, was a failure of leaders to rise to the responsibility of changing the character of the nation. I think he was right, and it's why I'm not here to scold anybody. Nigeria's history since independence, from the coups of the 1960s through the oil boom mismanagement of the 1970s to the structural adjustment years of the 1980s and the long argument over subsidies and the naira, reads in large part as a record of rulers who mistook the presidency for an inheritance instead of a stewardship.

But a diagnosis isn't a treatment plan. Plenty of readers have taken Achebe to mean that only one job remains for the rest of us, which is to vote until we find the right man. Scripture agrees that leadership matters. It doesn't agree that leadership is the whole story. James, writing about personal faith, puts the principle in a way that carries over to the national kind with some force.

> *"Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone."*
>
> — James 2:17 (KJV)

A congregation that prays for Nigeria on Sunday and does nothing on the other six days, that reports every pothole to God and never to the ward office, that complains about corruption and then pays at the checkpoint because it's quicker, has a faith that's all prayer and no hands. James doesn't call that weak. He calls it dead.

## Nehemiah Also Needed a King's Letters

Here is the strongest objection to everything so far, and I want to put it fairly. It runs like this. It's easy for a preacher to say, repair your section of the wall. But no household can repair a national grid. Nobody lights a hospital with a trowel. A citizen at a checkpoint pays the bribe because the officer holds the keys to his afternoon. And look at your own hero. Nehemiah didn't start with a trowel. He started by asking a king for permission, for letters to the governors on the road and for timber from the royal forest. If Nehemiah needed the state, so do we.

I concede most of that. Nehemiah did go to the king, and the road opened because a ruler wrote the letters. Many things in a nation can only be done by those who hold its offices, which is exactly why Ezekiel is angry with them. A people who say "we'll just build it ourselves" while ignoring the grid and the courts are fooling themselves.

But look at the order of events. Nehemiah was a cupbearer in a Persian palace, comfortable, employed and safe, a long way from the ruins. When the news reached him that the wall of his ancestral city lay broken and its gates burned, he wept, fasted and prayed for days. Then he asked, and when the king said yes he went. When he got to Jerusalem he didn't wait for the Persian treasury to produce a wall. He walked the ruins at night, told the people what he had seen, and said to them, "Ye see the distress that we are in, how Jerusalem lieth waste." The people's answer is the line I'd put on every town hall in this country.

> *"And they said, Let us rise up and build. So they strengthened their hands for this good work."*
>
> — Nehemiah 2:18 (KJV)

The letters opened the road. The wall went up household by household. And when, in Nehemiah 5, the wealthy among his own people were squeezing the poor, he confronted them to their faces. So the pattern includes building and it includes holding the powerful to account. It leaves out waiting.

## The Case for Keeping the Church Out

There is a second objection, and in Nigeria it comes from inside the church, not from outside. It says that all this talk of civic duty is a step too far for a congregation. The church's business is the gospel. Jesus told Pilate that his kingdom was not of this world (John 18:36). The moment pastors start talking about elections they will start choosing sides, and the church will become one more interest group fighting for a share. Better to pray for the nation and leave politics to those who enjoy it.

I want to give that view its due, because it is held by serious people for serious reasons, and because Nigerian history has handed them evidence. The Pew Forum's historical overview records that in 2003, after an election that European Union observers called flawed, Pentecostal leaders endorsed the winner. Anyone who has watched a pastor on a campaign platform, or a church hall rented out with a blessing attached, knows how quickly spiritual authority can be spent as political capital. A church that backs a candidate has to defend whatever that candidate later does. There is also an honest theological worry. If the gospel is a message of reconciliation to everyone, then the pulpit is not the place to tell half of the congregation they have voted for the wrong party.

I think the objection is right about partisanship and wrong about citizenship, and the Bible draws that line more sharply than we do. Start with Jeremiah's letter to the exiles in Babylon, who were about as far from power as a people can get. They were told to build houses, plant gardens and marry, and then comes the instruction that no one has ever found comfortable.

> *"But seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the LORD on its behalf,"*
>
> — Jeremiah 29:7 (ESV)

The verse goes on to say that in its welfare they will find theirs. Notice that prayer and seeking the city's welfare are joined by an "and". God did not tell the exiles to pray for Babylon and avoid it. He told them to work for its good, under a government they had not chosen and could not remove.

Then Romans 13, which gets quoted at Nigerians mostly by people who want them quiet. "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities," Paul writes, and a few lines later he says of the ruler, "for he is God's servant for your good" (Romans 13:1, 4, ESV). Read it with the end of chapter 12 still in your ears, where Paul has just told Christians to overcome evil with good. The ruler is described by his purpose, which is the good of the people he governs, and that purpose is the standard by which any ruler is measured. A Christian who believes the ruler exists for the public good is the very person who has a duty to ask whether he is serving it. And where a command contradicts God, the apostles had already settled the matter in the council at Jerusalem: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29, KJV).

In a country where citizens choose their rulers, even if imperfectly, the question changes shape. Paul addressed people who had no vote. We have one. To refuse to use it, or to use it for a bribe of rice, is to decline a stewardship Paul's readers would have given a great deal to hold.

## What Nigerian Christians Have Done With Power

The record is mixed, and I'd rather give you all of it. Samuel Ajayi Crowther was seized as a boy of about twelve by slave traders, sold in the Lagos market, freed by a British naval vessel and taken to Freetown, where he was baptised in 1825. He became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864 and finished a complete Yoruba Bible in 1884. It was public work in every sense, because a people given the Scriptures in their own language were handed a standard to measure rulers against. In 1890, a year before he died, he resigned in protest at racist remarks from mission leaders. He had learned that serving the public good sometimes means confronting your own church first.

The Christian Association of Nigeria was founded in 1976. Over the decades that followed Christians campaigned against Nigeria's joining the Organisation of the Islamic Conference in 1986, against proposals to extend Sharia court jurisdiction in 1988 and 1989, and in 1993, according to the Pew overview, backed Moshood Abiola, whose apparent win was annulled, and appealed to the military to hand over power. Those are public acts by believers, and they were not all wise. Some of them were about the church's share, not the country's. It is fair to ask which of them would pass the test of Jeremiah 29:7, whether they sought the welfare of the whole city or only of a part of it.

The better instincts are still alive. In February 2026 the Catholic bishops' conference told Nigerians to elect "holy politicians", and its president, Archbishop Lucius Ugorji, argued that only the mandatory real time electronic transmission of results can restore citizens' trust. On 2 November the Catholic Secretariat's secretary general, Father Michael Banjo, appealed for free, fair, credible and peaceful elections, warned about "the criminalisation of dissent, the weakening of opposition parties and the shrinking of democratic space", and called for three days of prayer and fasting under the theme "Let Justice and Peace Flow". Nothing in those reports tells a priest to endorse a candidate. They ask for clean process, fearless voters and leaders who treat office as service. That is the shape I'm arguing for, a church that is loud about the rules and quiet about the ticket.

## The Section of Wall Outside Your House

Read Nehemiah 3 sometime, the chapter most of us skip because it's a list. It names who repaired which stretch, and it is one of the most democratic documents in the Bible. Priests repaired near the Sheep Gate. Goldsmiths and perfumers took other sections. A ruler and his daughters did their share with their own hands. Several of them repaired the stretch opposite their own house. Nobody was told to fix the whole wall, and everybody was given a piece.

Which brings me to you. I'm writing as though to one reader, because that's how it works. Your piece might be the classroom where you won't accept money for a grade. It might be the small business you run without the shortcut everyone says is normal. It could be the church you serve faithfully when a bigger platform was on offer, or the letter you write to your local government chairman about the road instead of only praying about it. It could be the vote you cast after reading a manifesto in place of following the bloc you were born into. If you're abroad, it might be the money you send home to build something and not just to cover this month, or the refusal to let distance turn into an excuse. For some of you it's staying, when leaving would be easier.

Notice what Nehemiah 3 does not contain. It doesn't contain a single speech about the Persian Empire. The builders had no say in who sat on the throne at Susa. They did have a say in the stretch of wall outside their own door, and they used it. There is a freedom in that, and it's the freedom the Nigerian queue forgets. You may not control the price at the pump. You do control a good deal that sits closer than the price at the pump.

## Before the Vote in January

Between now and 16 January there will be rallies, billboards and a good deal of promising. The Catholic Secretariat's reports put the provisional register at about 103.4 million voters, which is a great many people to be told that nothing they do matters. Go and listen, and vote after you've read what's on offer. Then do the harder thing, which nobody will put on a billboard. Pick the street, the school, the ward or the office that's yours. Find two neighbours who are as tired as you are. Meet on Saturday and decide what you can repair, whether that's a drain, a clinic's roster, a school's lesson plans or a church's care for its widows, and who will write to whom about the rest.

Nobody is coming to save you, and that's meant to free you up as much as to sting. God hasn't abandoned this country. His way, from Samuel's Israel to Nehemiah's Jerusalem, has always run through a people who stopped queuing and started building, with the shepherd himself walking ahead of them.

What is the section of wall outside your door? I'd like you to be able to name it by Sunday.
