## The Advertisement God Never Placed

Imagine the notice. Wanted, a deliverer for a nation of slaves. Must speak well, carry himself like a man who has stood before kings, and have nothing in his past that a newspaper could use. No speech difficulty. No family trouble. Certainly no record of killing a man and burying him in the sand. Apply in person.

Nobody posted that notice, and the man who got the job would have failed every line of it. He was somewhere at the back of the Midian desert, a shepherd who had spent decades away from the palace that raised him, and when God told him he was the one, he did the sensible thing and listed his reasons why not.

Moses said, in effect, that the Lord had clearly not thought this through.

I'd like to put a question to you, as one person to another, and spend the rest of this essay answering it. If you carry something that makes you feel you're the wrong person for whatever God has been stirring in you, an illness, an old wound, a failure you can't undo, a loss that has not finished arriving, does that thing disqualify you? The Bible's answer is no. But it's a stranger answer than a plain no, and a good deal less comfortable than most of what gets said on the subject, because it says that the breaking and the calling are often the same event seen from two sides.

## Who Counts as Broken Here

Let me say what I mean, because the word can be made too small. I'm not only talking about people with a medical diagnosis, though they're included. I mean the woman whose pain has outlasted six months of prayer and a good many visitors who told her to claim her healing. I mean the man who walks into church with depression sitting on his chest like a stone, and sings loudly because he's afraid that if he stops the stone will get heavier. I mean the leader who mentors fifty people and then drives home alone to cry. I mean the family that buried someone this year and is still expected to say that it is well. I mean people whose nervous systems were set to high alert by something that happened before they were old enough to say no to it, and people whose failures were public and humiliating and still come back at three in the morning.

Sickness, weakness, grief, trauma, failure. The whole territory. The question is whether any of it takes you out of the running, and I'll try to answer slowly, because the people who most need the answer are the ones least likely to believe a quick one.

## Three Callings That Began With an Excuse

Start with Moses, because he's the plainest case. In Exodus 4 he lays out his objection honestly.

> *"Oh, my Lord, I am not eloquent, either in the past or since you have spoken to your servant, but I am slow of speech and of tongue."*
>
> — Exodus 4:10 (ESV)

The Hebrew behind "slow" means heavy, and the older English Bibles keep something of that weight, so that he is heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue. It sounds less like nerves and more like a real difficulty with speech. I wouldn't diagnose a man across three thousand years. I'd only say that he believed it about himself, and God doesn't argue the point. He answers it.

> *"Who has made man's mouth? Who makes him mute, or deaf, or seeing, or blind? Is it not I, the LORD? Now therefore go, and I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall speak."*
>
> — Exodus 4:11 to 12 (ESV)

Notice what God doesn't do. He doesn't heal Moses first and send him afterwards. He sends him with the mouth he has, and promises to be present in it. And the man who could not talk his way out of a conversation with God ended up at the head of a nation, which is a fair joke on every committee that has ever shortlisted for confidence.

Gideon is the next, and he's a funnier case. The angel finds him threshing wheat in a winepress, hiding from the Midianites, and greets him as "O mighty man of valor" (Judges 6:12, ESV). Gideon, who knows exactly what he is, replies with a complaint about his family. His clan, he says, is the weakest in Manasseh, and he is "the least in my father's house" (Judges 6:15, ESV). God doesn't correct the CV. He says, "But I will be with you" (Judges 6:16, ESV).

Then Jeremiah, who tells God he's too young and doesn't know how to speak, and gets the same reply. "Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you, declares the LORD" (Jeremiah 1:8, ESV).

Three men, three excuses, and one answer each time. The excuse is never refuted. It's outweighed by a presence. If you've been waiting for God to argue you out of your list of reasons, you may be waiting for something he has never once done.

## What Jacob, Timothy and Paul Carried

The Bible is not shy about bodies. Jacob is the first case, and the oldest. He had spent his life getting ahead by grasping, and the night before he met the brother he had cheated, a man wrestled with him until daybreak. Jacob would not let go. "I will not let you go unless you bless me," he said (Genesis 32:26, ESV), and he was given a new name, Israel, "for you have striven with God and with men, and have prevailed" (Genesis 32:28, ESV). He named the place Peniel, because he had seen God face to face and his life was spared. The same night his hip was put out of joint, and as the sun rose he passed Penuel "limping because of his hip" (Genesis 32:31, ESV).

I find it striking that the text gives him both and doesn't apologise for either. The blessing and the limp come out of the same night. Jacob didn't receive his new name and then recover his old gait. The man who walked into the promised land as Israel walked with a limp, and every step he took was a record of where he had been.

Timothy was young, and sent by Paul to carry weight in a major church. Paul also tells him, in the middle of a letter about church order, to stop drinking only water and "use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent ailments" (1 Timothy 5:23, ESV). A man with a stomach that gave him trouble, leading a congregation. The second letter reads to me like an older man steadying a nervous younger one, though that's my reading and not a diagnosis the text gives. Paul didn't think Timothy was disqualified by his body, and he didn't pretend the body was fine either. He prescribed medicine and kept him in the work.

Paul's own letters add two quieter names. Epaphroditus, the church's messenger to him, "was ill, near to death" (Philippians 2:27, ESV), and Paul says God had mercy on him. And in the last letter Paul wrote, a former travelling companion was simply left behind: "Trophimus I left ill at Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20, ESV). The man who healed the sick in Acts could not always keep his own team on their feet. Nobody in the New Testament seems embarrassed about it.

And then Paul himself, the man credited with thirteen of the New Testament's twenty seven books, who writes about a thorn.

> *"So to keep me from becoming conceited because of the surpassing greatness of the revelations, a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to harass me, to keep me from becoming conceited. Three times I pleaded with the Lord about this, that it should leave me."*
>
> — 2 Corinthians 12:7 to 8 (ESV)

He never names it. Scholars have guessed at an eye complaint, a recurring fever, an opponent, and I think the silence is a kindness, because it means anyone in any century can read their own pain into it. What matters is the answer he got.

> *"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."*
>
> — 2 Corinthians 12:9 (ESV)

Read it slowly. Paul asked for removal and God answered with sufficiency. The weakness stayed, and the power arrived inside it.

## Four Verbs at the Table

There is a scene in the Gospels that I think holds this whole argument in four verbs, and the scene is a meal.

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus sat with the Twelve. Luke records it simply: "he took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke it and gave it to them, saying, 'This is my body, which is given for you'" (Luke 22:19, ESV). Matthew and Mark say he blessed it, and Paul, passing the account on to Corinth, says the same of the thanks and the breaking (Matthew 26:26; Mark 14:22; 1 Corinthians 11:23 to 24). Took, blessed, broke, gave. The order never changes across the accounts.

Now go back some months to a grassy hillside, where a crowd of thousands has nothing to eat. A boy's lunch of five loaves and two fish is brought forward, and the Gospels describe the same four movements. Jesus takes it, looks up to heaven, blesses, breaks, and gives to the disciples, who give to the crowd (Matthew 14:19; Mark 6:41; Luke 9:16; John 6:11). Every one of the four Gospels tells this miracle, which is rare, and in each of them the bread is broken before it feeds anyone.

Think about that as bread. Nobody has ever fed a crowd with a whole loaf held in one hand. The loaf has to be torn before it can pass down the row. In the hands of Jesus, the breaking isn't what goes wrong with the bread, but how the bread becomes food for others.

It would be easy to turn that into a slogan about suffering, and I don't want to. Not every breaking is a feeding. A great deal of what happens to people in this world is plain wreckage, and the Bible calls it that. But the table shows a pattern that the believer can trust. When Jesus takes something into his hands, the breaking doesn't cancel the taking. It's still taken, still blessed, still given. The cracked life that has been placed in his hands isn't a spoiled offering. It may be exactly the shape in which it can be passed along.

And the one who said "this is my body" was about to be broken himself.

## The Man Who Was Broken First

The prophet Isaiah, about seven centuries before Jesus, wrote of a servant whom nobody would have chosen.

> *"He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief."*
>
> — Isaiah 53:3 (ESV)

The chapter goes on to say that he "has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows" (Isaiah 53:4, ESV), that he was "pierced for our transgressions" and "crushed for our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:5, ESV). The Christian reading, from the earliest preaching onwards, is that this is Jesus. The New Testament applies the passage to him more than once, and Philip's conversation with the Ethiopian official on the Gaza road is built on it (Acts 8:32 to 35).

I want to be careful with how this bears on you. The cross wasn't a lesson about how suffering improves people. It was an act of rescue, done once, that nobody else can repeat. But it does settle a question that sits underneath this whole essay. When you ask whether God can use a person who has been crushed, you are asking about a God who chose to be crushed himself. The Lord who calls the broken isn't standing on the shore, calling to swimmers. He went into the water first, and he knows how cold it is.

That is why David could write, out of the wreckage of the worst sin of his life, a line that no polished performance could have produced.

> *"The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a broken and contrite heart, O God, you will not despise."*
>
> — Psalm 51:17 (ESV)

David has been caught. The king who had arranged a man's death was found out by a prophet, and Psalm 51 is the aftermath. He doesn't offer a better programme. He offers a heart in pieces, and the psalm's claim is that God doesn't throw it out. If the broken thing counts as an acceptable sacrifice, then the people who feel least fit to bring anything to God are standing in the right place to bring it.

## Joseph and the Name of His Second Son

Joseph's life is a catalogue of breakings, and he's the figure in Genesis in whom the pattern shows most clearly. Sold by his brothers, carried to a foreign country, falsely accused, forgotten in prison by a man he had helped, he went from a favoured son to a slave to a prisoner without once being the cause of his own trouble. And then, in a day, he was made the second most powerful man in Egypt.

What interests me is what he named his children. His second son was called Ephraim, and Joseph explains why: "God has made me fruitful in the land of my affliction" (Genesis 41:52, ESV). Notice that he doesn't call Egypt the land of his success. He calls it the land of his affliction, and he insists that fruit grew there. He doesn't pretend the pit and the prison never happened. They're written into his family's names.

Decades later, with his brothers standing in front of him and the power to repay them, he gave the line that has comforted more broken people than any sermon I know.

> *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today."*
>
> — Genesis 50:20 (ESV)

Joseph doesn't say the evil was good. He says the brothers meant evil, and God was doing something else with it. Those are two different claims, and the second one is the only one that doesn't insult the person who was hurt. The pit was a pit. What God did with the man who came out of it was a granary.

## Treasure in Jars of Clay

Paul gives the whole idea its picture in 2 Corinthians, a letter written to a church that thought leaders should look impressive.

> *"But we have this treasure in jars of clay, to show that the surpassing power belongs to God and not to us."*
>
> — 2 Corinthians 4:7 (ESV)

The jars were cheap. In the ancient world a clay jar was the equivalent of a plastic bucket, useful, replaceable and fragile. Paul chooses it on purpose. The treasure is in the cheap container so that nobody will mistake the container for the source. He then goes through what this looked like in his own life, pressed on every side and struck down, and in the verses just after he says that he always carries in the body the death of Jesus, so that the life of Jesus may also be visible there (2 Corinthians 4:8 to 10).

That, I think, is why the same Paul can write that God chose the weak things so that "no human being might boast in the presence of God" (1 Corinthians 1:29, ESV). Read the whole sentence against the way many of us pick leaders.

> *"But God chose what is foolish in the world to shame the wise; God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong; God chose what is low and despised in the world, even things that are not, to bring to nothing things that are, so that no human being might boast in the presence of God."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 1:27 to 29 (ESV)

We look for the flawless testimony, the confident voice, the suit that sits right on the platform, and we quietly decide that anyone with a visible struggle should sit nearer the back. Paul's list is the reverse of that seating plan. The foolish, the weak, the low and the despised aren't a reserve team called on when the strong ones are unavailable. They're the first choice.

The reason is in the last clause. When a gifted, polished, healthy person does great things, people talk about the gift, and they're not wrong to, because that's how attention works. When it is somebody sick, shaking or limping, the conversation has nowhere else to land. Dane Ortlund puts it well in an article for Crossway on Paul's thorn: "Competence is not where God's power lies. Frailty is."

C. S. Lewis made a related point in *The Problem of Pain* (1940). God, he wrote, "shouts in our pains," and he called pain God's megaphone to a deaf world. I don't think he meant that suffering is good. Lewis himself later wrote about grief after his wife died, and he never wrote as if pain were pleasant. He meant that pain can strip away the noise that keeps us from hearing. Tim Keller, writing on suffering, makes a similar claim from the pastor's side of the desk, that suffering is "one of the main ways we move from abstract knowledge about God to a personal encounter with him as a living reality." It is a claim about what happens to people, and the people to whom it has happened tend to confirm it quietly. They don't preach about it. They pray differently.

## A Man Who Still Prays for Arms and Legs

You may want a living example, and there's one I find hard to dismiss. I should say that I know Nick Vujicic only through what he and others have published.

He was born in Melbourne on 4 December 1982 without arms or legs, and nobody could say why. Three sonograms had shown nothing unusual. His parents, according to an ABC News profile, went through grief and confusion, and it took about four months before they came to terms with it. His father, Boris, was a church founder and an author. By the age of ten Nick has said he was suicidal, and that he blamed God for his pain. I mention that plainly because it's part of the story and because some readers will have walked that road. If the stone on your chest is that heavy, tell a doctor or someone you trust this week, and don't wait for a better moment.

Today his ministry's own website reports that it has reached 946 million people digitally and 11 million in person, that 1.5 million have started a relationship with Jesus Christ, and that he has spoken in 87 countries, with a goal of one billion more by 2028. Those are the organisation's figures. They're counted by the ministry and not audited by anyone I can point you to, and the numbers on other pages differ a little, so hold them as an estimate of scale and not a score.

What I find more telling is what he said this year. A documentary about his life, called *No Limbs, No Limits*, was scheduled for release in American cinemas from 25 September, and I haven't seen it. In coverage of it in May, he was quoted as saying, "I still pray for arms and legs," and "God is not a genie." He is not a man who got his miracle and then told the story. He is a man who didn't, and told it anyway. The film also deals with the death of his father in 2017, a loss that he says he found hard to talk about.

So here is where I'd turn the whole argument. The strongest case for God using broken people isn't the person who got well and then became useful. It's the person who is still praying, still limited, and being used in the meantime.

## The Strongest Objection From the Prosperity Pulpit

At this point a serious believer, and I don't mean a cynic, will want to push back, and the push comes from a tradition large enough in Nigeria that it deserves its best statement. It runs like this. God is good, and the goodness of God is shown in blessing. Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13), and sickness and poverty belong to the curse. Isaiah 53:5 says that by his stripes we are healed, and Matthew 8:17 applies Isaiah's words about bearing our sicknesses to the healings Jesus performed. John writes to Gaius that he prays he may prosper and be in health (3 John 2). Jesus himself said he came that his sheep might have life abundantly (John 10:10). So to celebrate weakness, on this view, is to make peace with the very thing Christ came to defeat. It is faith's surrender to the enemy's terms, and a believer who calls the thorn a gift has stopped believing the promises. Ogbu Kalu, the Nigerian historian of African Pentecostalism, documented how widely this emphasis on health and wealth runs across the continent's churches, and it grew in soil where sickness and poverty were not abstractions.

I'd rather say that fairly than dismiss it, because most of those who hold it are not frauds. Many are people who have watched a loved one die of something treatable because there was no money, and who have found in the promise of God's goodness a refusal to accept that this is how the world has to be. That refusal is godly. Nobody who has read the Gospels can doubt that Jesus hated illness, wept at graves, and healed in large numbers. I'd never tell the sick to stop asking.

But the Scriptures that the objection leans on can't carry the whole weight put on them. Matthew's use of Isaiah 53:4 fits the healings Jesus actually did, and the Bible's own authors saw nothing inconsistent in also saying that the great price was the forgiveness of sins. First Peter reaches for the same verse and puts its weight on healing from sin (1 Peter 2:24 to 25). And the people the New Testament leaves us with are the evidence against a promise of universal, guaranteed health. Paul's three prayers weren't weak. Timothy, Epaphroditus and Trophimus weren't short of faith or of apostolic friends. Hebrews 11 ends its roll of the faithful with some who were delivered and some who were mocked, imprisoned, stoned and destitute, and says of all of them that the world was not worthy of them. If suffering meant a shortage of faith, that chapter would be an indictment.

The second point is the one I would press hardest. The objection treats weakness as the enemy of God's power. Paul says the reverse, in so many words: "for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, ESV). He doesn't say the weakness is good. He says it's the place where God's power shows up. A theology that can only celebrate the healed has nothing to say to the family at the bedside who prayed for a month and buried their mother anyway, and a theology that has nothing to say to them is too small for the book it quotes.

So I'd hold both. Pray boldly for healing, because the God of the Gospels heals. And refuse to tell the unhealed that they have failed. Whatever happens in the sickroom, the calling goes on.

## When Healing Doesn't Come

There's a second objection, quieter than the first, and it comes from the sickroom itself. Preachers who tell the sick that their weakness is a calling are doing something suspect, the sceptic says. They're dressing up unanswered prayer as strategy. Jesus healed people on the roadside, Peter's mother in law was up from her fever, a paralysed man was carried through a roof (Matthew 8:14 to 15; Mark 2:1 to 12). If God can heal, then talk of power in weakness is a theology for people who haven't been healed, and a convenient one for pastors who can't deliver.

I'll concede a good deal of that. It would be cruel to read this essay to someone in a hospital ward and imply that their illness was God's plan for their ministry. Anyone who has sat beside a sickbed in a Nigerian hospital knows the visitor who says the patient only needs more faith, and knows what that sentence does to a family at two in the morning. It isn't in the Bible.

Here's where the objection stops short, though. It assumes that the only options are healing or abandonment. Paul's story has a third. The prayer was heard, the thorn stayed, and the call went on. Whether God heals is one question. Whether the unhealed are still wanted is another, and the Bible answers the second with a firm yes whatever happens with the first.

## What Loss Teaches in Nigerian Churches

Anyone who has spent time among the churches of Nigeria, or of Congo, Uganda and Ghana, knows that prosperity and ease aren't the soil in which the most powerful testimonies grow. The testimony that stops a room usually begins with devastation. A widow stands to give thanks, and the congregation goes quiet because everyone knows what the year cost her. A man who lost his job and then his house prays with an authority that no seminary taught him. The intercession that carries weight is prayed by people who have needed God at the level of desperation and not convenience. It isn't taught. It's forged.

The history is the same. Samuel Ajayi Crowther was taken from his home in March 1821, at about twelve years old, by slave raiders, and sold to Portuguese traders. A Royal Navy ship freed the captives and landed them at Freetown, and he went on to become the first African Anglican bishop, consecrated at Canterbury in 1864, and to supervise the translation of the Bible into Yoruba (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). The man who gave a language its Scriptures began as a child who had been stolen from the language he was born in. I don't say God caused the raid. The Christian record is unanimous that the slave trade was evil, and I wouldn't call the raid a plan. I say that the God who took a stolen boy into his hands and gave him to a whole people is the same one who sits at the table.

Tim Keller's remark about the furnace has a Nigerian echo in every prayer meeting. The furnace burns away the pretending. What's left when everything else is gone is either real faith or nothing, and real faith leaning on a God who is present is the most powerful thing most of us will ever see. We should say, though, that the furnace isn't to be wished on anyone, and a congregation that is proud of its sufferers while neglecting to help them has missed the point of the table. Bread is broken so that people can eat. Where there are broken believers, the church's first work is to sit with them, feed them, pay the hospital bill and refuse to make a performance of their pain.

## What You Might Do This Week

If you've been reading this with something on your chest, I'd offer one small thing, and not a slogan.

Think of the line you've been using to tell yourself that you're the wrong person. Maybe it's the health condition, the divorce, the breakdown, the old failure, the stammer, the grave you visited in the spring. Now notice that Moses, Gideon, Jeremiah and Paul all had a line like that, and God answered each of them in the same way. Not with a refutation, but with himself.

> *"The LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves the crushed in spirit."*
>
> — Psalm 34:18 (ESV)

He isn't waiting for you to get well before he turns up. Paul came to the same conclusion about his thorn, and wrote it down.

> *"For when I am weak, then I am strong."*
>
> — 2 Corinthians 12:10 (ESV)

So say the thing out loud to one person this week. Tell a friend, a pastor, a cell leader, or your sister on the phone what you think disqualifies you, and ask them to pray with you about it. And if you are the one who is sitting across from them, listen first and give advice later. The bread on the table was taken, blessed, broken and given, in that order. You've been taken. You may be in the breaking. Ask him what he plans to give.
