## The Question That Kills Conversations

Somewhere in Lagos, right now, a young man is trying to explain to his Muslim colleague why there are over forty thousand Christian denominations in the world. His colleague is smiling. It is not a kind smile.

"You people cannot even agree among yourselves," he says, and there is a question buried inside the statement — which is the more lethal kind. "How do you expect me to believe that your religion is true?"

The young man opens his mouth. Then he closes it again.

He has been a Christian his whole life. He was baptised as an infant in the Anglican church his grandfather helped build in Ibadan. He had his "born again" experience in a Pentecostal fellowship hall at the University of Lagos, where the worship team played until the ceiling shook. He now attends a Reformed Baptist church where the pastor wears a suit and cites John Calvin more than he cites the Holy Spirit. He has lived in three rooms of the same house and still cannot tell you with confidence what separates the rooms, why they exist, or which one he should be sleeping in.

This article is for him. It is also for the Muslim colleague with the sharp smile, for the diaspora believer in London who wandered away from church partly because no one could explain why every branch seems to be quarrelling with the others, and for the new convert who has just discovered that accepting Jesus is the easy part — navigating what comes after is where things get genuinely complicated.

The existence of Christian denominations is either a scandal or a strength. My argument is that it is mostly the latter, occasionally the former, and almost always misunderstood. But to make that case properly, we need to do what Nigerian drivers do when approaching a junction in the dark: slow down, use your headlights, and think before you move.

## The Continent Before the Country

Let me give you a map.

Not a spiritual map — an actual geographic metaphor, because the best ideas are the ones that give your imagination a place to stand.

Imagine the entire Christian world as a continent. All the Baptists, the Anglicans, the Presbyterians, the Pentecostals, the Catholics, the Orthodox — all of them are on the same landmass. Different countries, different landscapes, different climates, but the same continent. What puts them on the continent? One thing. One thing alone.

They all agree that Jesus of Nazareth is the Christ, the Son of the living God crucified, buried, and raised on the third day, ascending to the Father, and returning to judge the living and the dead.

This is not a compromise formula invented by nervous ecumenists in a hotel conference room in Geneva. It is the bedrock confession of the church from its earliest hours. The Nicene Creed, hammered out in 325 AD in the heat of the Arian controversy, exists precisely to draw this line — declaring that Jesus is "of the same substance as the Father," not a lesser deity, not a prophet, not a moral exemplar who got confused about his own identity. The Son of God, fully divine, fully human, risen from the dead.

> *"For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures."*
> — 1 Corinthians 15:3-4 (ESV)

Notice what Paul says. First importance. Not "among the important things." Not "one of many foundational doctrines." First importance. Paul does not waste words. When he tells the Corinthians that Christ died for sins, was buried, and was raised, and that this is of first importance, he is performing a kind of theological triage that every Christian needs to understand.

Now. There are other continents.

Mormonism is not on the Christian continent. Mormons are sincere, often decent, deeply religious people — and they are not Christians. Not because Christians are unkind, but because Mormons themselves will tell you, if you press them, that they are not "creedal Christians." They reject the Nicene Creed. They teach that Jesus and the Father are separate beings, that God was once a man, that humans can become gods. This is not a variation on Christianity. It is a different religion wearing Christianity's clothes.

Islam is not on the continent. Islam reveres Jesus as a prophet of the highest order and categorically denies his crucifixion and resurrection. A Muslim and a Christian, standing together before the Nicene Creed, are on different continents. They may be neighbours. They may share much that is good and true. But they are not on the same landmass when it comes to the person of Jesus Christ.

Jehovah's Witnesses are not on the continent. Their version of Jesus — a created being, the first and greatest of God's creations, but not co-equal with the Father — is the same Arianism that Nicaea was called to refute. The church settled that argument seventeen centuries ago, and the Witnesses have been relitigating it ever since.

To say these things is not bigotry. It is cartography. You cannot have a map that places everything on the same continent regardless of where it actually sits. That is not inclusion. That is the destruction of the map itself.

## The Art of Triage

Now we are on the continent. The question is: what are the countries, and what are merely the states within those countries?

Here is a theological framework that has been around in various forms since the Reformation but deserves wider circulation: theological triage.

Triage is what emergency physicians do when multiple patients arrive at once. Not every wound is the same. Not every wound demands the same urgency. A broken finger and a ruptured aorta are both injuries. They are not the same injury. Treating them with identical urgency is not fairness — it is incompetence.

The same logic applies to doctrine.

**First-tier issues** are the ones where it is more important to be right than to be together. These are the load-bearing walls — remove them and the entire structure collapses. The physical resurrection of Jesus. Salvation by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone. The Trinity. The authority and sufficiency of Scripture. These are not tribal preferences. They are the ground on which the whole gospel stands. To disagree here is not to have a family argument. It is to have a different faith.

The Apostle Paul understood this with an intensity that makes modern Christians uncomfortable:

> *"But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed."*
> — Galatians 1:8 (ESV)

That is not the language of a man who believed all doctrinal roads lead to Rome. That is the language of a man who understood that getting the gospel wrong is not a theological error — it is a spiritual catastrophe. The same Paul who rebuked the Corinthians for their divisions was the Paul who said let the false teacher be accursed. Unity in the wrong thing is not unity. It is shared destruction.

**Second-tier issues** are the ones where it is more important to be together than to be right but where the disagreement is significant enough that people with strong convictions on opposite sides will probably worship most faithfully in different churches. The mode of baptism. Church governance. Whether salvation, once truly received, can be lost. Whether the miraculous gifts of the Spirit are still in operation today.

These are real disagreements. They are worth having. They are not worth breaking fellowship over on the street, but they are worth forming separate congregations over because a church is not merely a gathering of people who agree on Jesus. A church is a community of shared practice, shared teaching, shared sacraments. If half a congregation believes baptism belongs only to professing believers and the other half believes infants should be baptised into the covenant community, someone will perpetually be uncomfortable. Perpetual discomfort does not produce flourishing discipleship. It produces low-grade bitterness and the slow erosion of worship.

**Third-tier issues** are the ones where we can sit in the same pew, eat from the same communion table, and simply disagree without drama, without splinter groups, without passive-aggressive sermon illustrations directed at the people across the aisle. Questions of eschatology: are we premillennial, postmillennial, amillennial? What year did creation begin? What exactly happens in the intermediate state between death and resurrection?

These are interesting questions. Theologians have spent careers on them. They are not worth dividing a church over, and treating them as such is the theological equivalent of calling the fire brigade because your toast burned.

## A Brief, Honest, and Occasionally Irreverent Tour of the Family Estate

With that map in hand, let us walk through the major branches of the Christian family.

### The Room That Built Itself on the Local Church

The Baptists emerged from the English Separatist and Puritan movements of the seventeenth century. Their name is a declaration of their most distinctive conviction: baptism belongs to believers, not to infants. You cannot be baptised into the faith before you can confess the faith. This conviction is argued with admirable stubbornness across four centuries.

Baptists are congregationalists — the highest authority in church governance is the local congregation, not a bishop, not a synod, not a pope. Each church governs itself. This is either beautiful decentralisation or a recipe for ecclesiastical chaos, depending on which Baptist church you attend on any given Sunday.

Their default worship style is what liturgists call "low church" — informal, flexible, sermon-heavy, unbothered by set prayers and ancient rites. The sermon is the main event. Communion is a memorial, a looking-back to Calvary, not a vehicle of ongoing grace. The Bible is the final authority, full stop, and their preachers have historically understood the pulpit the way a surgeon understands the operating theatre: this is where the real work happens.

### The Room That Tried to Be Everything

The Anglican Church was born in the sixteenth century out of a political crisis that happened to have theological consequences. Henry VIII wanted a divorce. The Pope said no. Henry said fine and invented his own church. The break with Rome was initially about royal prerogative, not Protestant conviction. But history has a habit of outrunning its own origins, and later Anglican leaders — Cranmer, Ridley, Latimer, men who died for their theology — brought genuine Reformed conviction into the structure that Henry left behind.

The Anglican Church is an attempt to occupy the middle ground between Roman Catholicism and Protestantism. Its worship is formal and liturgical, shaped by the *Book of Common Prayer* — one of the genuine gifts of English prose to Christian devotion, a text that has formed the prayers of bishops and labourers alike for five centuries. Its governance is episcopal: bishops oversee regions of churches. Its theology tries to balance scripture, tradition, and reason as three interlocking sources of authority.

The American version of Anglicanism is called the Episcopal Church, and parts of it have done what happens when "reason" is elevated to the point where it begins to override scripture rather than serve it: they became officially affirming of same-sex marriage and LGBT clergy. When that happened, Anglicans across Africa — who make up a substantial and growing portion of global Anglicanism — broke away in theological protest. This is not a footnote. This is the Anglican Communion nearly fracturing along a first-tier fault line, and the African church was the one holding the line.

Conservative Anglican and Episcopal communities remain on the continent. The progressive wing has wandered into territory the map cannot endorse.

### The Room That Wept During Worship

John Wesley is one of the more compelling figures in the history of Protestant Christianity. He was a man who knew his theology, knew his Bible, and knew, somehow, that something was missing until the night in 1738 when he sat in a room in Aldersgate Street, London, and felt his heart, as he wrote, "strangely warmed."

That warmth became a movement.

Methodism emerged as a renewal within the Church of England. Its name came from the disciplined, methodical approach to Christian formation that Wesley developed — small groups, accountability structures, intentional patterns of prayer and service. The name was originally mockery. Wesley wore it as decoration. When the Church of England showed insufficient enthusiasm for his revival, he took it outside, and it spread across England, Wales, and the American colonies like a fire that knows exactly which dry ground to find.

Methodists emphasise experience alongside scripture, tradition, and reason — Wesley's quadrilateral of authority. They believe in free will and in synergistic salvation: God initiates, but human cooperation is real and meaningful. Their most controversial distinctive is the doctrine of Christian perfection — the belief that a believer can, in this life, reach a state of such complete sanctification that they no longer sin. Wesley believed it. Most subsequent theologians have not. The debate has been productive.

Here is where a Nigerian Christian must exercise discernment. The United Methodist Church in the United States became, in 2024, officially affirming of same-sex marriage and LGBT ordination. This is not a second-tier disagreement about infant baptism. This is a repudiation of the plain and consistent teaching of Scripture on matters of sexual ethics — which falls squarely within Christian orthopraxy, the domain of first-tier conviction. The global Methodists, particularly in Africa and Asia, declared that this was not where they were going, and a formal schism followed.

The lesson is not that Methodism is wrong. The lesson is that a denomination, like a person, can walk away from the gospel it once preached. When it does, the faithful remnant must have somewhere else to go.

### The Room That Posted the Theses

On October 31, 1517, a German monk named Martin Luther nailed ninety-five theses to the door of the Castle Church in Wittenberg, and nothing in the Western world has been quite the same since.

Luther was not trying to start a new church. He was trying to correct an old one. The Catholic Church of his day had developed the practice of selling indulgences — purchasing remission of sin through monetary contribution. A Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel was reportedly travelling through Germany with a box and a rhyme: "As soon as the coin in the coffer rings, the soul from purgatory springs." Luther found this not merely financially corrupt but theologically catastrophic. It meant grace could be bought. And if grace could be bought, the grace of God was not grace at all. It was a transaction. A business arrangement between a sinner and an institution.

His great discovery, hammered out in his reading of Romans and Galatians, was justification by faith alone — that a sinner is declared righteous before God not by anything they do or buy or say or attend, but by grace alone, through faith alone, in Christ alone.

> *"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast."*
> — Ephesians 2:8-9 (ESV)

Luther was emphatic because he had lived the alternative. He had flagellated himself, fasted to near collapse, confessed his sins hour by hour, climbed the *Scala Sancta* in Rome on his knees. And still found no peace. The achievement of peace through personal effort is a project that always ends in the same place: exhaustion, or delusion, or both. Until grace found him, Luther had been trying to earn what could only be received.

Lutherans baptise infants and hold a high view of communion — not transubstantiation, the Catholic teaching that the bread and wine become the literal body and blood of Christ, but something Luther called the real presence: Christ is truly present in, with, and under the elements. When he was debating the Swiss reformer Zwingli over this at Marburg in 1529, Luther reportedly carved into the table in Latin: *Hoc est corpus meum* — "This is my body." They could not agree, shook hands, and parted ways. The Reformation was learning, from its earliest days, to divide wisely over things worth dividing over.

### The Room That Loved Sovereignty

John Calvin worked from Geneva. John Knox carried the Reformed tradition to Scotland with a ferocity that made even Calvin nervous. Together, they gave the church Presbyterianism a system of governance named after the presbytery, the council of elders who govern not just a single congregation but a region of churches together.

The two words that illuminate a Presbyterian's eyes are sovereignty and covenant. God is sovereign in salvation — he elects those who will be saved from before the foundation of the world. This is predestination, and it either thrills you or makes you want to leave the room, depending on your theological upbringing. For Presbyterians, it is not a cold mechanical doctrine but the deepest possible pastoral assurance: your salvation does not rest on the consistency of your feelings, the fervour of your prayer, or the state of your spiritual temperature on any given Thursday morning. It rests on the eternal purpose of a God who does not change his mind.

Presbyterians baptise infants as a sign of covenant inclusion, believe in the spiritual presence of Christ in communion, and have produced a disproportionate number of the church's most rigorous theologians. The tradition carries its convictions with intellectual seriousness that has produced, generation after generation, people who read the Bible slowly and think carefully about what it says.

## The Fastest Fire on Earth

Let me slow down here. Because this section is not merely church history. For most Nigerians reading this, it is also autobiography.

The Pentecostal movement began formally on Azusa Street in Los Angeles in 1906, under the preaching of William Seymour a Black minister with one blind eye and a theology that set the twentieth century ablaze. But its roots go deeper: back through the Holiness movement, back through Wesley's strangely warmed heart, back to the book of Acts, back to that upper room in Jerusalem where something happened that none of the disciples had language for until it was already happening to them.

The Pentecostal claim is simple and enormous: the power of Pentecost is still available. The gifts of the Spirit described in 1 Corinthians 12 — tongues, healing, prophecy, miracles — did not retire with the last apostle. They are for the church today, in every generation, in every nation, in every language.

> *"And it shall come to pass in the last days, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh."*
> — Acts 2:17 (ESV)

The Pentecostals took that personally. And then the movement crossed the Atlantic. And then something happened.

In Nigeria, in West Africa, across the continent the Pentecostal gospel fell into soil that had been quietly waiting for it. Not because African Christians are credulous or unsophisticated, as colonial-minded observers sometimes implied with the particular condescension that mistakes their own scepticism for intelligence. But because African Christianity already knew, at the level of lived experience, that the world is not merely material. That spiritual forces are real. That prayer is not a metaphor for wishful thinking. That the supernatural is not an embarrassment to be softened in politely demythologised sermons.

The grandmother who visited the babalawo before your parents were born did not do so because she was ignorant. She did so because she understood, correctly, that the visible world has an invisible dimension that must be reckoned with. The Pentecostal gospel said: you are right about that — and here is the God who rules over both dimensions. That was not a small thing to hear.

The RCCG. Winners Chapel. Mountain of Fire and Miracles Ministries. Living Faith Church. These are not small organisations held together by marketing and spectacle. They are among the largest Christian communities in human history, with satellite congregations on every continent, sending missionaries back to the nations that once sent missionaries to them. The fastest-growing movement in global Christianity is not the sophisticated, theologically credentialled mainline denominations of the Western academy. It is the Spirit-filled, prayer-soaked communities in Africa, Latin America, and Asia who decided to take the whole Bible at its word.

Someone once said of the charismatic movement that it began as "an experience in search of a theology." That is simultaneously high praise and a warning. High praise because the experience was real — these were not performances or mass hypnosis, and the condescension that treats African Pentecostalism as religious hysteria says more about the condescension than about the movement. A warning because an experience without theology is not stable. Feeling without framework is not discipleship. It is spiritual adrenaline, and adrenaline wears off.

The shadow side of the movement is something every Nigerian Christian knows: the prosperity gospel. The teaching that financial wealth is a reliable mark of divine favour. That poverty is evidence of insufficient faith, hidden sin, or a generational curse requiring prophetic intervention and a substantial seed offering. The packaging of prayer as a commercial product — anointing oil that costs more than a week's groceries, declarations sold like securities, prophecies that bear an uncanny resemblance to whatever the congregation most wants to hear.

The Nigerian apologist stands inside this landscape, not outside it. He has seen the fire and the frauds. He has no interest in dismissing the entire movement because of its excesses, nor in defending the excesses because of the movement's genuine fruit. The Spirit of God is not responsible for what charlatans do in his name. And charlatans are not evidence that the Spirit has stopped moving. What they are evidence of is the same thing Elijah encountered on Mount Carmel — the human capacity to build elaborate religious machinery around a fire that went out long ago, and to keep dancing anyway.

What is needed — what has always been needed — is reform. Not rejection. Reform. The Word and the Spirit together, neither one permitted to silence the other.

## The Two Rooms at the Far End of the Hall

Roman Catholicism and Eastern Orthodox Christianity are both older than Protestantism, both claim direct apostolic succession, and both carry a richness of tradition, liturgy, and theological depth that Protestants often mine more freely than they acknowledge.

Catholics trace their authority through the bishop of Rome, understood to sit in the chair of Peter — the first pope — as Catholic theology reads Matthew 16:18. This is the passage where Jesus says to Peter: "On this rock I will build my church." Catholics read "this rock" as Peter himself. Protestants read it as the confession Peter has just made — "You are the Christ, the Son of the living God" — and find support in the observation that this confession, this exact confession, is the one thing every Christian in history across every denomination has consistently and unanimously affirmed. If anything is the rock on which the church is built, it is not a man who will later deny his Lord three times before sunrise. It is the truth that the man confessed.

Catholic theology holds that scripture, tradition, and the teaching authority of the Church — the magisterium, ultimately expressed through the pope — are co-equal sources of authority. This is the hinge on which the Reformation swung. Sola scriptura was not a Protestant novelty. It was a recovery of what the early church actually practised before centuries of accumulated tradition had begun to sit alongside and sometimes above the text.

The Eastern Orthodox Church separated from Rome in 1054 in the Great Schism — a divorce over theological and political differences that had been accumulating for generations. The Orthodox tradition places enormous emphasis on mystery, on *theosis* (the understanding that salvation involves genuine participation in the divine nature), and on liturgy that has changed little in a thousand years. It is a tradition of great beauty. Protestant Christians who have never sat in an Orthodox service should do so at least once — not because they will change denominations, but because they will understand something about the weight of worship that an informal evangelical gathering sometimes does not teach.

## The Question Everyone Is Actually Asking

So. Forty thousand denominations. Scandal or strength?

Here is where the argument makes its turn.

The scandal is not that there are many denominations. The scandal is that some of what calls itself Christian has walked off the continent entirely and the church has sometimes been too polite, too conflict-averse, too anxious about appearing unkind, to say so clearly.

The scandal is a prosperity gospel that is functionally indistinguishable from the spiritual extortion of false prophets, wrapped in the language of seed-faith and divine favour and preached from stages to people who are genuinely suffering and genuinely hoping that someone is telling them the truth.

The scandal is a denomination that ordains bishops who no longer believe in the physical resurrection of Jesus and then expresses bewilderment when the people in the pews start leaving.

The scandal is a church that has decided the accumulated cultural preferences of a particular moment in Western history are a better guide to sexual ethics than the consistent testimony of both Testaments across three thousand years.

The scandal is not Baptist versus Presbyterian. Not Anglican versus Pentecostal. Not high church versus low church, spoken liturgy versus spontaneous prayer, ancient hymns versus modern worship. These are family arguments, and a family that is arguing is at least still a family. The arguments have produced theology. The disagreements have sharpened convictions. The distinct denominations have reached people that other denominations could not have reached because God's people are not one type of person, and the church that tries to be everything for everyone usually succeeds at being nothing much for anyone.

The federalist principle applies here. Not every question requires a single answer imposed by a single authority. Where Christians disagree on second-tier issues — where they hold strong, scripturally grounded convictions about baptism, governance, spiritual gifts, eschatology — the denomination is not a sign of failure. It is a solution to a real problem. Different communities, each shaped by its own theological emphases, each accountable to the whole body, each pursuing the same mission: to know Christ, to make him known, to love the world he died for. This is not fragmentation. This is the body of Christ expressing what one single congregation in one single building could never contain.

But where a community departs from the first tier — where it denies the resurrection, preaches a different gospel, systematically teaches what the New Testament calls the broad road while labelling it grace — that community is no longer in a different room of the same house. It is no longer on the same continent. And the faithful Christian should neither pretend otherwise nor feel guilty about naming it plainly.

Paul did not feel guilty. He said: let them be accursed. He said it twice, in the same chapter, in case you missed it the first time.

## What to Do With All This

You do not need to change denominations.

You need to understand why yours exists, what it believes at the level of first-tier conviction, and whether it is genuinely under the authority of Scripture or merely paying it ceremonial respect while it goes its own way.

You need to know the difference between a second-tier disagreement — which should make you humble and ecumenical and genuinely curious about what your brothers and sisters in other rooms of the house have seen that you have missed — and a first-tier departure, which should make you sorrowful, firm, and unwilling to pretend that nothing has changed.

You need to stop performing denominational tribalism as though the Baptists and the Presbyterians are genuinely at war and all roads lead to eternal consequences. They are brothers who disagree about how to baptise their children. There is a rich tradition of Christian fellowship that survives and is in fact enriched by that disagreement.

And if you are in the Pentecostal and charismatic stream — as millions of Nigerians are, as many of the fastest-growing churches in the world are — hold experience accountable to Scripture, not the other way around. The God of Pentecost is also the God of Nicaea. The God who fills you with the Spirit is the same God who gave you the Book. Neither replaces the other. Both are gifts. Both are necessary. An experience that cannot survive theological examination was never as solid as it felt.

## The Door Is Still Open

The young man in Lagos, facing his colleague's sharp smile, has an answer. It is not a complicated answer. But it requires him first to know what he actually believes — not just which church he attends, but why, and on what foundation the whole structure stands.

Tell him this.

Christianity does not ask you to pretend that everyone who calls themselves a Christian believes the same things. It asks you to look at what every Christian, across forty thousand denominations, in every country on earth, across two thousand years of history, has consistently and unanimously declared:

That Jesus Christ is the Son of the living God.
That he died for our sins according to the Scriptures.
That he was buried.
That he was raised on the third day.
That he is coming again.

This is not a lowest common denominator. This is the diamond at the centre of everything. The denominations are the various ways that human communities shaped by their cultures, their histories, their temperaments, their theological emphases, their colonial inheritances and their post-colonial renewals have tried to build something faithful around that diamond. Some have built well. Some have built poorly. A few have wandered away from the diamond entirely and are no longer building the same thing.

But the diamond remains.

And if you have never held it — if you have been circling the church your whole life, putting your head inside the door on Sundays and then retreating to your own conclusions, waiting for someone to give you a good reason to come all the way in — I want to ask you something.

Not which denomination to join. Not whether the Anglicans or the Pentecostals have the better argument about spiritual gifts. Not whether the Presbyterians or the Arminians have the more accurate read on the mechanics of divine election.

What do you believe about Jesus of Nazareth?

That is the question. That has always been the question. It was the question he asked his disciples at Caesarea Philippi, and it is the question he is still asking, in Lagos and London and Lima and everywhere else where a human being sits with the evidence and has to decide.

The rest, I promise you, can wait.
