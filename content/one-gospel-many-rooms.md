## The Number Your Colleague Will Quote

Picture a young man in Lagos on a Monday morning, standing at the water dispenser with a Muslim colleague who has been saving something up since Friday. The young man is an invention. The conversation is not.

"You people can't even agree among yourselves," the colleague says, smiling. "I read that there are fifty thousand Christian denominations. How do I take your religion seriously?"

The young man opens his mouth and shuts it again. He was baptised as a baby in the Anglican church his grandfather helped to build in Ibadan. He gave his life to Christ in a fellowship hall at university, where the worship team played until the ceiling rattled. These days he sits in a Baptist church whose pastor quotes Calvin more often than he quotes the Holy Spirit. He has lived in three rooms of the same house, and he couldn't tell you what separates them, why they exist, or which one he ought to be sleeping in.

The colleague's figure isn't made up, and it's out of date. The Center for the Study of Global Christianity, which keeps the most quoted count, put the world's denominations at 47,000 in its 2024 table and at 50,000 in 2025. Its 2026 table reads 53,000, on a line it labels "Denominations / Rites". In 1900 the Center's estimate was 2,000. So if anything the colleague was being modest.

This essay is for the young man. It is also for the colleague, who deserves a straight answer and not a dodge, for the believer in London who drifted from church partly because nobody could explain why every branch seems to be quarrelling with the others, and for the new convert who has just discovered that saying yes to Jesus was the easy part. My argument is that the number measures how fast a living faith spreads far more than it measures how badly it has broken, that the breaks which do matter are mostly invisible to a headcount, and that a Christian can tell the two apart if he knows what to look for. To make that case we will need a map, a set of scales, a walk through the family house, and a clear head about where the real fault lines are running in Nigeria this year.

## Why the Objection Deserves Respect

Say it as well as the colleague could. Paul told the Ephesians there was one body, one Lord, one faith, one baptism. Jesus prayed on the night before his death that his followers would be one, so that the world would believe (John 17:20 to 23). A faith that claims one Spirit leading it into truth ought to produce more agreement than a committee of accountants. Instead, every revival seems to end with a new signboard. You don't need a statistic in Nigeria. Walk down any commercial street in Ibadan or Enugu and count the church names between the pharmacy and the bank.

He would add that Christians haven't merely disagreed. They've excommunicated each other, and in Europe they went to war with each other for generations. A religion that preaches reconciliation has a poor record at it. And the sharpest form of his question concerns the book, and has little to do with Christians. If the Bible is clear, why has it produced all this?

To be fair to the colleague, his own tradition has its schools of thought and its disagreements, and I won't score that point against him. He didn't ask for a lecture on his family's quarrels, and I'd be annoyed if he gave me one on mine. The honest question is what division shows, in any faith.

I concede most of that. Divisions among Christians are a grief, and nobody should defend the pettiness behind some of them. What I'd challenge is the inference, that a big number proves the message is false or the Spirit absent. To test that, we have to know what is being counted.

## What the Big Number Actually Counts

The Center's own commentary says the count rises every year, and it credits Protestant divisions in the twentieth century, the rise of Pentecostalism, and the founding of indigenous churches around the world. That is a mixed bag. A church planted by a Nigerian evangelist in a rented hall is a new entry on the list, and it is also a sign that the gospel reached somewhere it hadn't been.

Then there is the question of method. A sceptical writer, Jeffery Lowder (2019), looked at an earlier edition of the same research and noted that its authors "count each country in which a denomination has a presence as a separate denomination," which turns the Catholic Church alone into 242 entries once its rites are broken down. He was working from secondhand sources, and he said so. The Center's current pages that I could read don't spell out their counting rules, so I can't tell you how much of the 53,000 is the same family showing up in many countries. Treat the figure as an order of magnitude and distrust anyone who uses it as precise.

The shape of Christianity is more useful than the count. The Pew Research Center's 2011 report on the world's Christians, using 2010 data, found that about half were Catholic, Protestants broadly defined made up 37 percent, and Orthodox Christians 12 percent. The great majority of Christians live inside three large families, each with its own long internal arguments. The tens of thousands of entries sit on a small number of trunks. And Pew's 2025 report, which compared 2010 with 2020, found about 2.3 billion Christians, some 29 percent of the world, and counted 697 million of them in Africa south of the Sahara, which now holds 31 percent of all the world's Christians and has overtaken Europe as the region with the most.

I don't know how many of those 53,000 entries are real quarrels. Nobody does. But the count can't tell you the thing your colleague really wants to know, which is whether the people on it believe different gospels or argue about different things inside one gospel. That question needs a different instrument.

## Who Is Inside the House at All

Start with a map, because the best arguments give the imagination a place to stand. Picture the whole Christian world as one continent. Baptists, Anglicans, Presbyterians, Pentecostals, Catholics and Orthodox are different countries on it, with different climates and customs and some border disputes. What puts them on the same landmass? A shared confession about who Jesus is and what he has done. Worship style and views of bishops have nothing to do with it.

The story of how that confession got settled is partly an African one. Around the year 319, a presbyter in Alexandria in Egypt named Arius began teaching that the Son of God was made, not eternal, and was a lesser being beneath the Father. His bishop, Alexander, excommunicated him. Alexander's young deacon, Athanasius, went with him to the great council at Nicaea in 325, and spent most of his long life afterwards defending what it decided. The council confessed that the Son shares the very being of the Father, using a Greek word meaning "of one substance," and it condemned the claim that there was a time when he was not. Arius and two companions refused to sign and were exiled. The creed we recite on Sundays took its final form at Constantinople in 381. The church was not cooking up a compromise formula or dressing a simple message in Greek philosophy. It was saying out loud what it had always prayed and baptised into, because someone had started teaching otherwise.

Here is that creed, in an English rendering that follows the Greek, since many of us say it every week and seldom hear it.

> *"We believe in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible; And in one Lord, Jesus Christ, the only begotten Son of God, begotten from the Father before all ages, light from light, true God from true God, begotten not made, of one substance with the Father, through Whom all things came into existence, Who because of us men and because of our salvation came down from the heavens, and was incarnate from the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary and became man, and was crucified for us under Pontius Pilate, and suffered and was buried, and rose again on the third day according to the Scriptures and ascended to heaven, and sits on the right hand of the Father, and will come again with glory to judge living and dead, of Whose kingdom there will be no end;"*
> — The Creed of Constantinople (381), English text from Early Church Texts

The creed goes on to confess the Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life, and then continues in these words.

> *"Who proceeds from the Father, Who with the Father and the Son is together worshipped and together glorified, Who spoke through the prophets; in one holy Catholic and apostolic Church. We confess one baptism to the remission of sins; we look forward to the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Amen"*
> — The Creed of Constantinople (381), English text from Early Church Texts

Notice the structure. It has three paragraphs, one for the Father, one for the Son and one for the Spirit, and the middle one is longest because the quarrel of the day was about the Son. Notice, too, the words "who proceeds from the Father." The Latin West later added "and the Son," and the Eastern churches have never accepted the addition. That is a real division, and I will return to it. But even there, both sides recite the same creed with one clause in dispute.

Before Nicaea the church had a shorter confession, the Apostles' Creed, which grew out of the baptismal questions put to converts in Rome. No council wrote it, and Christians have used it for well over a thousand years to say what they are baptised into: God the Father, Jesus Christ his only Son, the Holy Spirit, the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the body. In 451 the Council of Chalcedon added the last piece of the structure, confessing that Christ is one person in two natures, fully God and fully man, without confusion and without division. Not every ancient church accepted that formula. The Ethiopian and Coptic churches, which are the African core of what is called Oriental Orthodoxy, parted from the others over how to state it, and they hold the faith of Nicaea to this day (Christianity Today, 2018). So the continent has an old African coast, and any map that leaves it out is a bad map.

Paul had set down the same confession long before any council, as the thing he received and passed on.

> *"For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received: that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures."*
> — 1 Corinthians 15:3 to 4 (ESV)

Look at the words "of first importance." Paul isn't listing one important thing among many. He is sorting.

That is a line about who is in the house. Some groups sit outside it by their own account. Jehovah's Witnesses teach that the Son is a created being, which is the position of Arius, and the church ruled on it seventeen centuries ago. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter day Saints calls itself Christian and means it, and its members are often among the kindest people you could meet. But it also rejects the Nicene Creed, teaches that the Father and the Son are separate beings, and holds that human beings can become like God. The disagreement is over what the word Christian has meant since Nicaea. Muslims honour Jesus as a great prophet and the Messiah, and they deny his death on the cross and his divine sonship, which the creed confesses. None of this is a judgement on anyone's sincerity or decency. It describes where each group sits relative to a line the church drew long ago. A boundary that took everybody in would mark nothing, and a map that placed every religion on one continent would not be inclusive. It would be useless.

## The Families Inside the House

Within the continent there are a handful of great families, and each can speak for itself. I want to state each in the form its own members would accept, with the key term explained, and then say where the others disagree.

The Oriental Orthodox churches, the Coptic Church in Egypt and the Ethiopian Tewahedo Church among them, say that they have held the faith of the apostles in unbroken continuity since the first centuries. Christianity reached the kingdom of Aksum in the fourth century, and in 328 Athanasius, by then the bishop of Alexandria, consecrated a former captive named Frumentius as its first bishop. The Ethiopian church today has more than 45 million adherents (Christianity Today, 2018). Their claim is that Africa did not receive Christianity from Europe. It gave it to Europe, and kept it.

The Eastern Orthodox Church, which separated from Rome in 1054, holds that it is the one church of the creed, and that its bishops, councils and liturgy carry the faith unchanged. Orthodox Christians stress mystery, the authority of the seven ecumenical councils, and what they call theosis, which means that salvation is a real sharing in the life of God, a healing that makes human beings what they were created to be, and not merely a legal pardon. They reject the Roman claim of a pope's universal authority, and they do not accept the Western addition to the creed.

The Roman Catholic Church holds that the one church founded by Christ continues in unbroken succession from the apostles, under the bishop of Rome as the successor of Peter. Catholics read Matthew 16:18 that way. They hold that the Scriptures, apostolic tradition and the teaching authority of the church, the magisterium, belong together, and that a visible teaching authority is how Christ keeps the church in the truth. In its own careful wording, the Vatican's doctrinal office has said that the Church of Christ "subsists" in the Catholic Church, meaning it is concretely found there, and that outside her structure there are "elements of sanctification and of truth" which belong to Christ's Church and which God uses in salvation (Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 2007). That statement treats the Orthodox as true churches that lack something, and Protestant communities as ecclesial communities, not churches in the proper sense. Protestants hear that and wince, and the Catholic reply is that she is being honest about what she believes. I would rather be told plainly than flattered.

The Reformation families took shape in the sixteenth century. The Lutherans trace themselves to 31 October 1517, the day Martin Luther dated his Ninety five Theses against the sale of indulgences. The story of him nailing them to the church door at Wittenberg is traditional, and some historians doubt it. What's certain is that he sent them to Archbishop Albert of Mainz that day. The jingle ascribed to the indulgence preacher Johann Tetzel, about a coin ringing in the coffer and a soul springing from purgatory, is quoted everywhere, and I could not trace it to a first source, so I won't repeat it as his words. What matters is the theology behind Luther's anger. If forgiveness could be bought, grace was not grace. His discovery, from Romans and Galatians, was justification by faith, the teaching that a sinner is declared righteous before God by grace through faith in Christ and not by anything he does or buys. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 became the Lutheran statement of it. Luther knew the alternative from the inside, as a monk who fasted, confessed hourly and, in the traditional telling, climbed the Holy Stairs in Rome on his knees, and still found no peace. Lutherans baptise infants and hold that Christ is truly present in the Lord's Supper, a position distinct from Rome's doctrine of transubstantiation, which says the bread and wine become the body and blood, and from the memorial view of Zwingli, who held that the meal is mainly a remembrance. At Marburg in 1529, Luther and Zwingli reached agreement on fourteen points and broke on the fifteenth, about the Lord's Supper (Wikipedia contributors, n.d., Marburg Colloquy). Luther is said to have chalked the words "This is my body" on the table. They parted, and the Reformation went on dividing, wisely sometimes and sometimes not.

The Reformed and Presbyterian family followed Calvin in Geneva and Knox in Scotland, and takes its name from the councils of elders, the presbyters, who govern a region of churches together. Their keywords are sovereignty and covenant. In their own formulation, God from eternity chose a people in Christ, not because of anything he foresaw in them, and those he chose he calls, justifies and will bring safely home. That is what the word predestination means in the Westminster Confession of 1646 and the Canons of Dort of 1619. It either thrills you or makes you want to leave the room, but for the Reformed it is meant as pastoral comfort, since a believer's salvation doesn't hang on the temperature of his prayers on a Thursday morning. Presbyterians baptise infants as a sign of covenant inclusion and hold that Christ is spiritually present at the table.

The Anglicans began, as the story is usually told, with Henry VIII's marriage problem, and a break with Rome that was more about a crown than a creed. But men who came after Henry, Cranmer and Ridley and Latimer among them, put real Reformation convictions into the structure and were burned for them. The Book of Common Prayer, first issued in 1549, has shaped the prayers of bishops and farmers for nearly five centuries. The Thirty Nine Articles of 1571 gave the church its Reformed doctrine, and the bishops, the liturgy and the creeds gave it its Catholic shape. Anglicans give Scripture the first voice, with tradition and reason helping them to read it. In 1888 the bishops at Lambeth named four things that Anglicans hold as a basis for any future unity: the Scriptures, the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds, the two sacraments of baptism and the Supper, and the historic episcopate. That list is itself an act of triage.

The Baptists grew out of the English Separatists of the seventeenth century, and their name is their argument. Baptism belongs to those who can confess the faith, so a baby cannot be baptised into it, and the proper form is immersion. Each congregation governs itself, with Christ alone as its head. That's either admirable decentralisation or a recipe for chaos, depending on which Baptist church you visited last Sunday. For Baptists the Lord's Supper is a memorial and a proclamation, and the sermon is the main event. The Baptist claim is that the New Testament church was a gathered company of believers, and that infant baptism and state churches were later departures.

John Wesley was a priest of the Church of England who never intended to leave it. On 24 May 1738, at a Moravian meeting in Aldersgate Street in London where someone read Luther's preface to Romans, he wrote in his journal, "I felt my heart strangely warmed." The movement that grew from that night stressed disciplined holiness, small groups, and the real participation of the believer in salvation. Methodists teach that God's grace goes before us and makes our response possible, which is why they speak of prevenient grace, and that a believer can grow in holiness until sin loses its grip. Wesley called it Christian perfection, and most later theologians haven't followed him all the way, but the debate has been productive and nobody ought to split a church over it. Methodists often speak of a Wesleyan quadrilateral of Scripture, tradition, reason and experience. The term wasn't Wesley's. A twentieth century Methodist scholar, Albert Outler, coined it to summarise how Wesley worked.

I have left out the Pentecostal and charismatic family for now, because for most Nigerians reading this it is a chapter of their own lives as much as of church history, and it needs its own room.

## Sorting Doctrines by Their Weight

Inside the house, not every disagreement weighs the same. The tool I find most useful is what R. Albert Mohler (2005) called theological triage, a term borrowed from emergency medicine that comes from the French for "to sort." Without it, he wrote, "the scraped knee would receive the same urgency of consideration as a gunshot wound to the chest." Mohler asks Christians to "determine a scale of theological urgency," and he proposes three levels, so I'll use his and add my own gloss. A broken finger and a ruptured artery are both injuries. Treating them with the same urgency is not fairness, it is incompetence.

At the first level sit the doctrines the gospel stands on: the Trinity, the full deity and humanity of Christ, his atoning death and bodily resurrection, salvation by grace through faith, and the authority of Scripture as God's word. Here it matters more to be right than to be together. Paul was fierce about it.

> *"But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be accursed."*
> — Galatians 1:8 (ESV)

He said it twice, in two consecutive verses, in case anyone missed it. A man who thought all roads lead to Rome, or anywhere else, would not write that way. The same Paul who rebuked the Corinthians for their divisions told the Galatians to hold a hard line, because unity around the wrong thing is not unity. It is shared loss.

At the second level are the questions on which faithful readers of the same Bible land differently, and where the difference shapes how a congregation lives. These include the meaning and mode of baptism, how a church is governed, how Christ is present in the Lord's Supper, whether the miraculous gifts continue, and whether women may be ordained as pastors and bishops. They include, too, one that every Nigerian church seems to argue about at some point, which is whether a true believer can lose his salvation.

I should state that last one in the form each side would accept, because it is often caricatured. The Reformed position is called the perseverance of the saints. It holds that those whom God has truly called and joined to Christ will not fall away finally, because their security rests on God's faithfulness and not their own. If a person abandons the faith for good, the Reformed would say that he was never truly joined to Christ at all, and that his profession, however sincere it looked, was not the fruit of regeneration. The Arminian position, which comes from the Dutch theologian Jacobus Arminius and his followers the Remonstrants, who set out their case in 1610, holds that God's election is in view of faith, that grace can be resisted, and that a believer who turns away in persistent unbelief can forfeit the salvation he once received. Wesleyan Methodists hold a form of this, and many Pentecostals do as well. Catholics and the Orthodox have their own versions. Notice what each side shares. Both say that salvation is by grace and that the believer must continue in faith, and both agree that Scripture warns and that Scripture comforts. They differ on how the warnings and the comforts fit together. These are real disagreements, and worth having. They are not worth breaking fellowship over on the street. But they are worth forming separate congregations over, because a church isn't merely a gathering of people who agree on Jesus. It is a community of shared practice, shared teaching and shared sacraments. If half the congregation believes baptism belongs only to professing believers and the other half believes infants should be baptised into the covenant community, somebody will be uncomfortable every Sunday. Perpetual discomfort doesn't produce flourishing discipleship. It produces low grade bitterness and the slow erosion of worship. So separate congregations are a sensible arrangement and needn't be a hostile one.

At the third level are the matters Christians can disagree about over the same communion table. Mohler describes them as "doctrines over which Christians may disagree and remain in close fellowship," and puts most debates about the end times there. Some are premillennial, believing Christ returns before a thousand year reign, some postmillennial, believing he returns after a golden age brought by the gospel, and some amillennial, taking the thousand years as a figure for the whole age between his comings. Others differ on the age of the earth, or on what exactly happens between death and the resurrection. These are interesting, and careers have been spent on them. Dividing a church over the order of events in Revelation is a little like calling the fire brigade because the toast burned.

Two cautions. The levels don't hang on the wall of a seminary with every doctrine already labelled, and Christians argue hard over which level a given question belongs on. A Catholic will say the papacy is first tier. A Baptist will say baptism is nearly so. I think those are honest disagreements, and I'd rather name them than pretend triage is a neutral tool. And the earliest Protestants showed how costly that argument can be, at Marburg and afterwards.

This is also how the young man at the water dispenser can answer without raising his voice. He doesn't need to defend all fifty three thousand entries. He needs to say which of the arguments in his own church's history sit on which tier, and to say it without embarrassment.

## The Case for One Visible Church

There is a better version of the colleague's objection, and it comes from inside the family. Catholic and Orthodox Christians have been making it to Protestants for five hundred years. If every believer and every congregation reads the Bible for themselves, they say, you will get exactly the long list we've been counting. What holds a church together is a visible teaching authority: a bishop of Rome and the councils for Catholics, the bishops gathered in council for the Orthodox. Early Christians like Cyprian of Carthage, an African bishop of the third century, spoke of the unity of the bishops as the bond of the church. Without a referee, the game fragments.

I think that's the strongest argument against my own tradition, and I won't pretend it has no force. Protestants have no referee, and it shows. The council at Nicaea that gave us the creed was a gathering of bishops, and those who wave away every council and creed since the apostles have thrown away a gift.

But the argument has a limit. Rome and Constantinople separated in 1054, and each still has its own internal quarrels, so a referee has never been enough to guarantee unity. Which referee is the right one is itself a dispute at the first tier, and it was argued long before the Reformation. And the claim the Reformers made was not what its critics say. By sola scriptura, which means Scripture alone, they did not mean that every believer is his own pope, or that the creeds and the Fathers are worthless. They meant that Scripture is the supreme and final authority, and that councils and bishops are to be tested against it. Paul asked the Galatians to test even an angel against the gospel they had received. Catholics reply that the church is what gave us the canon of Scripture and so cannot be judged by it. That reply deserves a full hearing, and I have tried to give it one. My own conviction is that when a council or a bishop and the apostles' teaching in the Bible diverge, the Bible must win, and that I would rather live with the mess that follows than with a unity that depends on not asking.

The Protestant reading of Matthew 16:18 belongs here as well. Jesus says to Peter, "on this rock I will build my church," and Catholics hear the rock as Peter himself. Many Protestants hear the confession Peter has just made, that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God. That confession is the one thing that Christians of every family have affirmed across the centuries, and the man who made it denied his Lord three times before sunrise a few chapters later. I find it hard to believe that the church was built on his consistency. It was built on what he said.

## African Roots Older Than Europe

The history I have just told so far has a European accent, and it isn't the whole story, so the Nigerian reader deserves more.

Thomas Oden, an American Methodist theologian, argued in *How Africa Shaped the Christian Mind* that the formative flow of early Christian thought ran from Africa to Europe, and the reverse. He pointed to Mark in Egypt, to the school of Alexandria and its great teachers such as Origen and Athanasius, and to Tertullian and Cyprian in Carthage and Augustine in Hippo (Oden, 2008, as reviewed in Voice, 2010). A reviewer thought he overstated the case in places, and I'd agree that you can claim too much for any one region. But the claim that Christianity is a European import to Africa cannot survive a look at the map of the fourth century. Egypt, Carthage, Aksum and Hippo were not footnotes. They were the centre.

Nigerian Christianity has a younger history, and its beginnings are mostly the story of returned slaves. Samuel Ajayi Crowther was a Yoruba boy captured by slave raiders in the early 1820s, freed at Freetown by a British cruiser, and baptised in 1825. He became the first African graduate of the Church Missionary Society's college at Fourah Bay, was ordained in England, and was posted to Abeokuta, where he met his mother again and baptised her in 1848. He translated the New Testament and the Book of Common Prayer into Yoruba, and in 1864 he was consecrated Bishop for West Africa beyond British Jurisdiction, the first African bishop in the Anglican Church (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d., Crowther). The Church Missionary Society's Henry Townsend, who had reached Abeokuta in 1843 and returned in 1846 with Crowther, later opposed Crowther's consecration, and the Dictionary of African Christian Biography links that opposition to Townsend's racial prejudice (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d., Townsend). When Crowther was an old man he was forced from his own mission, and he resigned in protest. A story that began with a boy in chains ended with an African bishop being humiliated by his own church. It shows that missionary history is neither a story of pure heroes nor a story of pure villains. Other missions followed, including Methodists, Baptists, Presbyterians in Calabar and Catholics in Lagos, and each planted the family it came from. That is why a young Nigerian can be baptised into an Anglican church, converted in a Pentecostal fellowship and end up in a Baptist pew, and why the answer to the colleague includes the fact that the denominations came to us as gifts and as import, in the same boat.

African Christians soon began to ask for forms that were their own. After the First World War, praying churches, called Aladura churches, grew out of the ministries of prophets in Yorubaland. The Cherubim and Seraphim Society was founded in 1925, the Church of the Lord (Aladura) in 1930, and the great revival of the 1930s under Joseph Babalola, a road grader driver who became a prophet after a series of visions, brought thousands to Aladura and mission churches alike (Christian History Institute, n.d.). Babalola founded the Christ Apostolic Church in 1941, and foreign Pentecostal bodies arrived alongside, among them the Apostolic Church in 1931 and the Assemblies of God in 1939 (Pew Research Center, 2006). Then came the Redeemed Christian Church of God in 1952 under Josiah Akindayomi, the Church of God Mission in 1972 under Benson Idahosa, the Deeper Life Bible Church in 1975, and the great Pentecostal houses of the 1980s. By the Pew Research Center's 2006 survey, about 30 percent of Nigerians were renewalists, meaning Pentecostals or charismatics, and about 60 percent of Nigerian Protestants fell into that group (Pew Research Center, 2006).

Evangelical Nigerians also asked what the faith was for. Byang Kato, born in 1936 at Kwoi in Kaduna State, was dedicated to a local deity by his father as a child, baptised at about twelve, trained at Igbaja Bible College and went on to a doctorate at Dallas Theological Seminary in 1974. In 1973 he became the first African General Secretary of the Association of Evangelicals in Africa, and he became an outspoken critic of what he saw as syncretism and universalism in the African church (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d., Kato). He died by drowning off Mombasa in December 1975. You do not have to agree with every judgement Kato made, and many African theologians have pushed back on his sharpest lines, to see why a Nigerian reader should honour him. He asked, in the middle of the great African growth, what is the gospel that is growing, and what must it never be allowed to become. That is the triage question in a West African accent.

## The Fastest Growing Room in the House

For most Nigerians reading this, the next room is autobiography.

The Pentecostal movement is usually dated from Azusa Street in Los Angeles, which began in April 1906 under William J. Seymour, a Black preacher (1870 to 1922) who had lost the sight of his left eye to smallpox. Its roots run back through the Holiness movement and Wesley to the upper room in Acts. Its claim is simple and enormous. The power of Pentecost didn't retire with the apostles, and the gifts described in 1 Corinthians 12, including tongues, healing, prophecy and miracles, are for the church today, in every generation and every language. Pentecostals take Peter's quotation from the prophet Joel personally.

> *"And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh"*
> — Acts 2:17 (ESV)

Whether the gifts of the Spirit continue is a second tier question, and it's one on which Baptists and Pentecostals have long differed. Neither side's answer makes the other less Christian. A Pentecostal and a Baptist worship the same Christ, and what separates them is their expectation of what he does today.

The message crossed the Atlantic and landed in soil that was ready. That isn't because Africans are credulous, as the occasional condescending observer has implied. Nigerian religious life already assumed that the world isn't only material. Plenty of Nigerian families have a grandmother who consulted a traditional diviner before their own parents were born, and she wasn't a fool. She understood that the visible world has an invisible side that has to be reckoned with. The Pentecostal gospel told her she was right about that and told her who rules over both sides. It was a big thing to hear. And it is why the Redeemed Christian Church of God, Winners' Chapel, Mountain of Fire and Miracles Ministries and Living Faith Church, whatever one thinks of them, are not small organisations held together by marketing. They are among the largest Christian communities in the world, and they are sending missionaries to the nations that once sent missionaries to us.

Look at where the church now stands. The Center's 2026 table counts almost 780 million Christians in Africa, and a summary of the same edition reported by Hope Media says nearly 70 percent of the world's Christians now live in the Global South, up from 17 percent in 1900. A movement that grows by new congregations planting newer ones will always produce a long list of names. Whatever else the number of denominations means, it follows a faith that has put down roots across this continent.

A saying goes round about the charismatic movement, that it began as an experience in search of a theology. I could not trace it to a first source, so I won't pin it on anyone. It is a fair remark all the same. It is high praise, because the experience was real, and the condescension that treats African Pentecostalism as religious hysteria says more about the condescension than the movement. And it is a warning, because an experience without a framework is not stable. Feeling alone makes poor discipleship. It runs on adrenaline, and adrenaline wears off.

This room has a shadow, and every Nigerian Christian knows it. Wealth treated as proof of God's favour. Poverty read as hidden sin or a generational curse that needs a prophetic intervention and a generous seed. Prayer sold like a commodity. I will say plainly what I think about the teaching. It touches grace, because it puts a price on a blessing, and grace is first tier. But the Spirit isn't responsible for what is done in his name, and the existence of a counterfeit is poor evidence that the real thing has stopped. What counterfeit religion shows is the same thing Elijah met on Carmel, a human talent for building machinery around an altar where no fire falls, and dancing anyway. Many Pentecostal pastors in this country have no part in any of it, and some of the sharpest critics of the prosperity message are Pentecostals themselves. What is needed is reform and not rejection. The Word and the Spirit together, with neither allowed to silence the other.

It is worth noticing that Pentecostals are arguing about this. In October 2025 the national president of the Pentecostal Fellowship of Nigeria, Bishop Wale Oke, told The Guardian that the fellowship was strengthening accountability and creating a national register to distinguish genuine Pentecostal churches from impostors. I can't say how that will work, and registers have their own problems. But the instinct is the right one. The movement wants to know who is actually in the room.

## Where the Real Fractures Are Running

Here the story gets harder, and the tidy answer stops working.

A family argument is one thing. Some recent events look like something else. In 2015 the Episcopal Church in the United States changed its definition of marriage, and in 2016 the Communion's primates restricted its role in doctrinal decisions for three years. Several African provinces, the Church of Nigeria among them, aligned themselves with Episcopalians who left over it. On 3 October 2025 Sarah Mullally was named Archbishop of Canterbury, the first woman to hold the office. Three days later the Church of Nigeria's Primate, Henry Ndukuba, called the appointment "devastating." His statement gave two reasons: the conviction of many Anglicans against women serving as bishops, and Mullally's support for same sex marriage.

In March 2026 the Global Fellowship of Confessing Anglicans, Gafcon, met in Abuja. According to MinistryWatch the meeting drew 347 bishops and 121 other leaders from 27 provinces, and it renamed the body the Global Anglican Communion and elected Archbishop Laurent Mbanda of Rwanda as its chairman. America magazine reports the new body as a Global Anglican Council, so even the name was reported two ways. Gafcon claims to speak for at least half of the world's 85 million Anglicans, and the Communion itself has 42 member churches. The Abuja Affirmation called on conservative churches to remove any reference to being in communion with the See of Canterbury (America, 2026). Meanwhile, on 25 March, Mullally was installed at Canterbury Cathedral, with 26 Primates present in person. The Primates of Nigeria, Uganda, Rwanda, Congo, Kenya, Myanmar and Chile were among those absent, and the report I read gives no reasons for most of those absences (Church Times, 2026). They insisted they weren't a breakaway, and there is a real argument between that claim and the headlines asking whether the Anglican Church would split. History may call March 2026 a schism or a reorganisation. I don't know, and I distrust anyone who's sure.

The Methodists went through something similar. In 2022 a new Global Methodist Church began, formed by traditionalists leaving the United Methodist Church. By December 2023 about 7,673 congregations had left the older body. At the General Conference of 2024 in Charlotte, delegates voted 692 to 51 to remove the ban on ordaining people in same sex relationships, and by wide margins removed the penalties against clergy who bless same sex unions. Four former United Methodist conferences in Nigeria joined the Global Methodist Church in July 2024.

Now apply the triage honestly, because it cuts more than one way. The creeds say nothing about same sex marriage, and those who changed their churches' teaching would tell you that they confess the same Christ and read the same Bible, and that they act out of love for people in their own pews. That is a fair statement of their case, and the creed is untouched. Equally, the Church of Nigeria's statement bundles together two questions. The ordination of women is one on which careful readers of the Bible, including many who treat Scripture as their final authority, have landed in different places, and I'd put it on the second tier.

The other question I put higher. When a church decides that the settled reading of the Bible on a point of ethics can be overridden by a new consensus, the matter has stopped being about marriage. It has become a question about who holds the authority in the church, Scripture or the consensus of the moment. And the authority of Scripture, on my own reading, is a first level matter. I know thoughtful believers who see it differently, and I'd rather argue with them over a meal than about them from a platform.

The same test applies to the shadow in the Pentecostal room. When a church puts a price on a blessing, it touches grace, and grace is first tier. And it applies to any denomination, of any size, that stops confessing the resurrection of Jesus. A church can wear a mitre or a suit or a white garment and still have walked off the continent.

So the scandal isn't the number. The number counts Baptists and Presbyterians arguing over how to baptise their children, and a Nigerian church planted last year in a rented hall. The real fractures run along the seam of the first tier, and they don't show up in any count. They tend to widen where the church has been too polite or too nervous to say plainly that a line has been crossed. Families that argue are still families. A family that has stopped arguing, because nobody believes anything worth arguing about, is in worse trouble.

## What This Asks of an Ordinary Believer

You don't need to change denominations. You do need to know why yours exists, what it holds at the first level, and whether it is under the authority of Scripture or only pays it ceremonial respect.

You need to be able to tell a second tier disagreement from a first tier departure. The first should make you humble and curious about what your brothers and sisters in the next room have seen that you haven't. The second should make you sorrowful and firm, and unwilling to pretend nothing has changed.

Go and sit in a room that isn't yours. If you've never attended a Catholic Mass or an Orthodox service, go once, and if you've never been to a Pentecostal vigil, go once. A Protestant who has never stood through an Orthodox liturgy has missed an education in how weighty worship can feel. You're not defecting. You're finding out what your brothers and sisters have seen that you haven't, and you'll understand your own room better for it.

And you need to stop treating Baptists and Presbyterians as if they were at war. They're a family arguing over how to baptise the children. Nigerian Christians already know how to share a room. The Christian Association of Nigeria, founded in 1976, brings together five blocs, from the Catholic Secretariat to the Pentecostal Fellowship. It argues like any family does. In March 2025 the Christian Media Forum was urging two men who each claimed to lead its northern states to end their dispute.

Anyone who has been to a Nigerian burial knows how this works in practice. The family in the front pew may include an Anglican mother, a Catholic uncle, a cousin who worships at a Pentecostal church with a car park the size of a stadium, and a Baptist neighbour who has come to help with the food. They sing different hymns at different speeds. And when the minister starts the Lord's Prayer, nobody asks to see anyone's denominational papers.

If you're in the Pentecostal or charismatic stream, as millions of Nigerians are, hold your experience accountable to the text. The God of Pentecost is also the God of Nicaea. The God who fills you with the Spirit is the same God who gave you the Book. An experience that can't survive being compared with Scripture was never as solid as it felt.

## The Question Beneath All the Rooms

Back at the water dispenser, the young man has an answer, and it isn't complicated. He can say, "Yes, there are a lot of us, and some of the arguments are petty and some of them hurt. Most of us are arguing about how to baptise and who governs the church, and a few arguments are about whether the Bible still decides anything. But go into any room that belongs to the house on a Sunday and you'll hear the same thing said out loud, that Christ died for our sins, was buried, rose on the third day, and is coming again."

That won't end the conversation with the colleague, and it shouldn't pretend the disagreements away. It does shift the question. The one Jesus asked his disciples at Caesarea Philippi is still the one that sorts everything.

> *"He said to them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Simon Peter replied, 'You are the Christ, the Son of the living God.'"*
> — Matthew 16:15 to 16 (ESV)

If you've spent years standing in the corridor, with your head round the door of one room after another and never going in, waiting for somebody to rule on which denomination is right, I'd like to ask something before you walk into any of them. Not which church. What do you say about Jesus of Nazareth? The rest of it, the baptism and the bishops and the end times, can wait until you have an answer. And once you do, there are plenty of rooms where people will be glad to argue it through with you.
