A message reached me on WhatsApp one evening. Hello sir. Good evening. How are you doing, and how was your day. Only after all that courtesy did the real business arrive, introduced as a quick question.

Here it is, as it was asked. If the law reveals sin and condemns it, and faith in the finished work of Christ is what saves us, why can't we abandon the law? And why does Paul say, in Romans 3:31, that we can't?

I've been smiling at the word "quick" ever since. People have argued over this one for nearly two thousand years, and some of the best minds in the church went to their graves a little annoyed with one another about it. But the question is a good one, and the answer, once you see it, is kinder than the question expects.

A word on terms first. By "the law" the questioner means the commandments God gave to Israel through Moses, the Ten Commandments and everything that grew around them. By "faith" he means trusting Christ. We will tighten both as we go.

## The Question at Its Strongest

Let me put the objection the way its best defender would put it, because it has earned that much.

Paul tells the Romans that no human being will be justified by works of the law. "Justified" is a courtroom word. It means declared righteous, and so acquitted and accepted, and Paul's whole argument is about how a guilty person gets that verdict. He says the righteousness of God has been shown apart from the law. Elsewhere he says believers are not under law but under grace (Romans 6:14). On Paul's own account the law saved nobody. What it did was tell the truth about us, and the truth was damning. So the reasoning goes like this. A woman who has been acquitted doesn't keep reading the charge sheet. A patient who has been healed doesn't carry the scan report about like a family heirloom. If the law's work was to expose and condemn, and Christ has dealt with the condemnation, then the law has done its job and can be thanked and sent home.

The long word for this position is antinomianism, which means "against law" and describes the view that Christians are not bound by God's commandments. Nobody wears that label proudly, so let me give the view the words its holder would use. Picture a young man who grew up counting rules. "I spent years trying to be good enough," he says, "and every year I knew I wasn't. Then I heard that Christ had done it all, and I could breathe. Now you tell me the commandments are back on the table. You've handed me the old chains with a nicer ribbon on them." That is a scene, not a transcript, but anyone who has sat in a Nigerian church for long enough has heard the sentiment, and the relief in it is real.

It's a sensible argument, and it isn't foreign to Paul. Romans 3:31 exists because he can hear it coming. He has just spent twelve verses saying that nobody is declared righteous by keeping the law, and he knows what the sharpest person in the room will say next. He had even been slandered with it. Some said his message amounted to "let us do evil that good may come" (Romans 3:8), and he calls that charge deserving of condemnation.

> *"Do we then overthrow the law by this faith? By no means! On the contrary, we uphold the law."*
>
> — Romans 3:31 (ESV)

Look at what he does. He raises the objection himself. He answers with the short, hard refusal he uses whenever an idea is unthinkable to him (the old King James Bible renders it "God forbid"). And then he says the reverse of what the objector expected. Faith doesn't leave the law lying on the floor. It sets it on its feet.

Anyone who has sat through enough Nigerian church services will know this isn't a classroom quarrel. There are two kinds of preaching, and plenty of us have sat under both in the same month. One makes the commandments the whole of the gospel, and the congregation goes home tired and counting. The other makes grace so light that the commandments evaporate, and the congregation goes home comfortable and unchanged. Each could use Romans 3:31 a good deal more than it does.

## The Other Objector Speaks Too

There's a second person in the room, and he deserves a hearing. He is the legalist. Legalism is the habit of treating obedience as the thing that earns God's favour, or keeps it from running out. In his best words, he says this. "I've watched what happens when a church stops talking about commandments. The young people drift, the standards go soft, and nobody can say what the church stands for. Jesus himself said, 'If you love me, you will keep my commandments' (John 14:15). Whatever grace is, it can't make holiness a matter of mood."

He has seen real damage, and he has the words of Jesus on his side. His trouble is the antinomian's turned the other way round. The young man has heard that faith saves and concluded that the law is finished. The legalist has heard that the law matters and concluded that it must be what saves. Romans 3:31 is addressed to the first of them, but a few verses earlier Paul has already dealt with the second. If keeping the law could put you right with God, boasting would be possible, and the verdict doesn't come that way.

> *"For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law."*
>
> — Romans 3:28 (ESV)

So the question is narrower than it first looked. The legalist says the law matters and the antinomian says it doesn't, and Paul says it does, in a different way from either of them. What the law is for, once Christ has come, is the real question.

## What the Word Law Covers

Much of the confusion starts with the word itself. Paul slides between several meanings without warning his readers. Sometimes "law" is the five books of Moses. Sometimes it is the whole Old Testament, which is why in Romans 3:19 he can say "whatever the law says" right after quoting a string of Psalms and Isaiah. Sometimes it is a principle, as in the "law of faith" of verse 27. And sometimes it is the specific commands of the Mosaic covenant.

Christians have long sorted those commands into three bundles. The ceremonial law covers the sacrifices, the priesthood, the festivals, the food rules and circumcision, everything that marked Israel off as a holy people and pointed forward to Christ. The civil law covers the courts and penalties of Israel as a nation. The moral law covers how to treat God and other people, with the Ten Commandments at its centre.

This sorting is a tool later readers made. A Jewish reader in Paul's day would have said there was one Torah, a single gift from a single God. The tool is useful as a map, as long as we don't mistake it for the territory. Whoever says the ceremonial law has ended but the moral law remains needs the rest of the New Testament behind him, and we'll see how much of it is.

## Where Verse 31 Sits in Romans

Paul has walked a long road to reach verse 31. He begins with a charge against the whole human race. The Gentiles, the non Jewish nations, have turned from the God they could see in creation (Romans 1:18 to 32). The Jews, who had the law, have used it as a badge of privilege while breaking it (Romans 2:17 to 24). Even Gentiles who never heard the law at Sinai show by their consciences that some of its demand is written within them (Romans 2:14 to 15). Then he closes the case.

> *"Now we know that whatever the law says it speaks to those who are under the law, so that every mouth may be stopped, and the whole world may be held accountable to God."*
>
> — Romans 3:19 (ESV)

> *"For by works of the law no human being will be justified in his sight, since through the law comes knowledge of sin."*
>
> — Romans 3:20 (ESV)

Then comes the turn many readers call the "but now" of Romans. In verse 21 Paul says that the righteousness of God has been made known apart from the law, and in the same breath that the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it. Read that twice. Verses 22 to 26 explain how. Everyone has sinned, everyone is justified freely by grace through the redemption in Christ Jesus, and God put Christ forward as a sacrifice of atonement, so that he might be both just and the one who justifies. Verses 27 to 30 then ask who this is for, and whether God belongs to Jews only.

Paul's answer is no, God is the God of Gentiles also (Romans 3:29). Modern readers tend to miss how much this matters. The law was Israel's possession, and Paul is arguing that one God, justifying by one route of faith, has opened his family to everyone. A Gentile in Ibadan or Aberdeen need not become a Jew to belong. That's why the law could not be avoided. If the law is the entry ticket, the Gentile has to buy it. If faith is the ticket, what becomes of the law? Verse 31 is Paul's answer.

The rest of the letter is the answer. Chapter 4 brings Abraham, chapter 5 sets Adam and Christ side by side, chapter 6 faces the charge that grace invites sin, chapter 7 admits that the law is holy and still helpless, and chapter 8 announces that the Spirit does what the law could not.

## What the Law Was Built to Do

Two jobs showed up in Romans 3:19 and 20. The law stops mouths. When it speaks, nobody has a defence left to offer, and the whole world stands accountable. And it gives knowledge of sin, which is a diagnosis.

Picture a man in Ibadan whose leg has hurt for weeks. He finally goes to the hospital, and the radiographer holds the X ray up to the light. A clean break, right there on the film. The film didn't break his leg and it can't mend it. But he'd be a fool to throw it away, because the surgeon will need it to set the bone in the right place.

That's the law's first job, and Paul is careful not to blame the law for being unable to heal. He calls the law holy, and the commandment holy and righteous and good (Romans 7:12). The weakness sits somewhere else.

> *"For God has done what the law, weakened by the flesh, could not do."*
>
> — Romans 8:3 (ESV)

The trouble was never the instruction. It was us. A perfect standard handed to people who can't keep it produces a perfect record of failure, and that record is what the law kept producing. It said what was right and supplied no power to do it. A verdict, with no strength attached.

So when the objector says the law has done its work, he's half right. It did the work of a diagnosis, and a diagnosis isn't a cure. The question is whether you throw out the diagnosis once the cure arrives. Paul says no, and I think the next sections show why.

## The Two Verbs Paul Chose

Paul chooses his verbs with care, and the Greek repays a slow look. The first is *katargoumen*, from a word that means idle or without work. To "overthrow" or "nullify" the law is to leave it with nothing to do, to put it out of business. The second is *histanomen*, a form of the common verb for making something stand. To "uphold" or "establish" the law is to set it upright and give it ground to stand on. The translators have wandered between "establish," "uphold," and "fulfil," and they were right to wander, because Paul means something wide.

Two details sharpen the verbs. In Romans 7 Paul says believers have been released from the law, and the verb there comes from the same family as the one in 3:31. I wouldn't build much on a single word, but it suggests Paul can say two things at once. The law has lost its claim over believers as their judge, and it has not been put out of business. Think of a soldier discharged from an army. He is no longer under its orders, yet nobody would say the army has been abolished. The second verb turns up in Romans 10:3, where Paul says his fellow Israelites sought to establish their own righteousness. So the question is who does the establishing. A person who tries to stand the law up by his own effort ends up with his own righteousness. A person who trusts Christ finds the law standing on firmer ground than he could have given it.

Here I should be honest about where scholars disagree, because they do. Nobody has settled exactly what "the law" means in this verse, or exactly how faith "establishes" it. Two readings dominate. One says Paul means the Old Testament as a whole, and faith establishes it by confirming what it was saying all along, that God declares sinners righteous on the ground of promise and trust. The other says Paul means the law's moral demand, and faith establishes it by producing, through the Spirit, the obedience the law could command and never create. I don't think the two compete. Paul would likely say yes to both. And I'd add a third thing, which needs its own section.

John Calvin read the verse in that wide way. He argued that faith confirms the law because the law was given to drive people to Christ, and in Christ they find the righteousness the law demands. He added that the ceremonies of the law stop when Christ comes and yet find their real substance in him. John Wesley, in his sermon on this very text, narrowed it, holding that it was the moral law only of which Paul could truly say that we do not make it void but establish it through faith. They differ on how much of the law Paul has in view. They agree on the direction.

## Abraham Before the Law Arrived

Chapter 4 opens with a man, and the man is Abraham, the ancestor every Jewish reader would put first in any argument about God's favour.

> *"Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness."*
>
> — Romans 4:3 (ESV)

Paul quotes Genesis 15, and then asks a question a good lawyer would admire. When was it counted, before Abraham was circumcised or after? Before, and circumcision came afterwards as a sign of the righteousness he already had by faith (Romans 4:9 to 12). Elsewhere Paul adds that the law came 430 years after the promise, so it could not cancel the promise or turn it into a wage (Galatians 3:17). Abraham stands at a point in history with no Sinai, no temple and no priesthood, and a man is put right with God by trusting what God has said.

Two things follow. Faith is older than the law, so nobody can say Paul invented a route around it. And Abraham becomes the father of Jew and Gentile together, since he was righteous before there was any difference between them (Romans 4:11 to 12). The law's own record supports Paul, as he claimed in 3:21.

Genesis doesn't stop at belief, though. Abraham trusted, and then Abraham walked. He left Haran, he lived in tents, he took the knife up the mountain. Faith goes first and obedience follows on the same road.

## Why Grace Cannot Waive the Verdict

Here is what I think the objection misses, and it's the centre of the matter.

The objection assumes that the way to honour grace is to treat the law as something God has quietly dropped, as though the charge sheet were a clerical error that the Judge has now noticed and withdrawn. But look at how Paul describes what happened. In the verses just before ours, he says God put Christ forward as a sacrifice of atonement so that he might be both just and the one who justifies those who have faith in Jesus (Romans 3:25 and 26). Just, and justifier. Both at once.

That is a strange claim if the law can simply be abandoned. A judge who lets everybody go home hasn't shown mercy. He has closed the court. Mercy that means anything has to be mercy that someone pays for, and the New Testament says exactly that. The law said sin deserves death, and death happened. The debt wasn't cancelled by a clerk's pen. It was paid, at a cost nobody could have named in advance.

So when somebody tells me the law has been abandoned, I want to ask what Christ was doing on the cross. If God's verdict on sin could have been shrugged off, then his death was an expensive way of doing nothing. The cross is the proof that the law was never optional. Grace doesn't ignore the law's verdict. It carries the verdict out, on someone else, in full.

Jesus himself said as much. He told his hearers he had come not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfil them (Matthew 5:17). The word he uses is the word of a man finishing a job, and abandonment was never what he had in mind.

There's a pastoral edge to this that matters. If the verdict had been waived, you could never be sure it wouldn't be reopened. A man who is told the law doesn't count anymore has no firm ground under him; he's only been told the rules are on holiday. A man who knows the verdict was executed, and that Christ took it, can stand. Release from prison doesn't prove the penal code is void. It proves the sentence was served.

## How the Law Gets Written on the Heart

If faith upholds the law's verdict by way of the cross, it upholds the law's demand by way of the Spirit. This is the part of Paul's answer that people most often skip, and it's the part that makes the verse a promise and not only a defence.

Return to Romans 8, where the law's weakness was named. Paul doesn't stop at the weakness. He says God sent his Son and condemned sin in the flesh in order that "the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us," and he adds that this happens in those who walk by the Spirit and not by the flesh (Romans 8:4, ESV). Fulfilled in us. The law isn't set aside. It's finally met, and met from the inside.

That was the promise of the prophets. Jeremiah heard God say he would make a new covenant and write his law on his people's hearts (Jeremiah 31:33). Ezekiel heard it from another angle, with God's Spirit placed inside people so that they would walk in his statutes (Ezekiel 36:27). The same law, in a new place. Not on stone tablets that could only accuse, but in people who've been changed enough to want what it asks.

Martin Luther saw this clearly. In his preface to Romans he deals with this very objection. "No, he says, we uphold the law through faith, i.e. we fulfill it through faith," Luther writes of Paul's answer. And he explains what fulfilling means. "To fulfill the law means to do its work eagerly, lovingly and freely, without the constraint of the law." Think of two drivers. One stops at the red light only when an officer is standing at the junction. The other stops because there might be a child crossing and he cares about the child. Both obey the same rule. Only one of them has been changed by it.

Wesley put the order of things plainly. Good works, he said, though as necessary as ever, "are not antecedent to our acceptance, but consequent upon it." We don't obey in order to be accepted. We are accepted, and obedience follows like fruit. The sequence matters because it keeps both errors out. The legalist has the order backwards, and the one who says the law no longer matters has dropped the second half.

Augustine, bishop of Hippo in what is now Algeria, made the same point in his treatise *On the Spirit and the Letter*, written in 412 in the middle of the Pelagian controversy, when many were claiming that people could keep God's commands by their own strength. As Augustine reads Paul, the law commands and grace enables. In the English translation I read, he says that the law is established through faith "since faith procures grace whereby the law is fulfilled" (chapter 30). Take away grace and the command only condemns. Add grace and the command becomes something a person can walk in. That is one African father's way of saying what Romans 3:31 says in one line.

Paul gives the same summary in plainer words near the end of the letter.

> *"Owe no one anything, except to love each other, for the one who loves another has fulfilled the law."*
>
> — Romans 13:8 (ESV)

Love is not an escape from the commandments. It's what they were pointing at all along, and it's what the Spirit now makes possible.

## Which Law, and Free From What

At this point someone will say, fairly, that Paul does seem to say we are free from the law. He does. So it matters what exactly we are free from.

We are free from the law's curse, because Christ bore it. We are free from the law as a way of earning acceptance, which was never going to work. As the Jerusalem council and Galatians 3 show, Gentile believers were not put under circumcision and the full Mosaic system as a condition of belonging to God's people. The ceremonial order, with its sacrifices and its priests, finds its end in the one sacrifice and the one priest it was always pointing to. Romans 10:4 says Christ is the "end" of the law, and the Greek word can mean both a stopping point and a goal. I suspect Paul intends both.

What we are not free from is God's moral will. The New Testament repeats it again and again, in the teaching of Jesus, in the letters, in the plain instruction to love, to refuse theft and lies and sexual immorality, to honour parents, to care for the widow. These commands aren't kept as a ladder to heaven. They're kept as the shape that a changed life takes.

I should admit that Christians draw the lines a little differently. Some, following the Reformers, speak of the "uses" of the law, meaning the several jobs it does. Calvin named three. The law restrains evil by frightening people into order, as any society's laws do. It exposes sin and drives people to Christ, which is the diagnostic work we saw in Romans 3. And it guides those who already believe. Others prefer to speak of the "law of Christ," a phrase we will meet shortly, and avoid carving the old law into parts. I don't think we need to settle every dispute to see the direction. We aren't free to say the commandments are none of our business, and we aren't free to treat them as our salvation. Either move would be a mistake, and Romans 3:31 is the verse that stands between them.

## What Jesus Said About the Law

If Paul is right, Jesus ought to have said something that sounds like Romans 3:31, and he did. The most direct place is the Sermon on the Mount.

> *"Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them."*
>
> — Matthew 5:17 (ESV)

He expected someone to think the opposite, and his hearers had watched him heal on the Sabbath and eat with the wrong people. He adds that not the smallest stroke of a letter will pass from the law until all is accomplished (Matthew 5:18), and that whoever relaxes the least of the commandments and teaches others to do so will be called least in the kingdom of heaven (Matthew 5:19).

"Fulfil" is a roomy word. It can mean to complete, to bring something to the point it was aiming at. Some Christians say Jesus fulfilled the law by keeping it perfectly in our place, some that he fulfilled its shadows and prophecies, and some that he fulfilled it by teaching its depth. The sermon supports all three, since Jesus spends the rest of Matthew 5 showing that anger and lust break the commandments at the root.

Then comes the line that unsettled the best behaved people in the crowd.

> *"For I tell you, unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven."*
>
> — Matthew 5:20 (ESV)

A righteousness greater than that of the most careful law keepers in Israel is not something any of us can produce. That sentence is where Matthew 5 and Romans 3 meet. It strips the legalist of his comfort, and it tells the antinomian that Jesus did not think the law a small matter. A righteousness we cannot produce has to be given, and Paul says that is what God does through faith. Later, asked which commandment is greatest, Jesus says the whole Law and the Prophets hang on loving God and loving your neighbour (Matthew 22:37 to 40). He is telling us what the law was for.

## Galatians and the Law of Christ

Galatians is Paul's angriest letter, and the anger is connected to our verse. Teachers had arrived in the churches of Galatia saying that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be properly saved. Paul answers that anyone relying on works of the law is under a curse, because the law demands all of it (Galatians 3:10), and that Christ redeemed us from the curse by becoming a curse for us (Galatians 3:13). He compares the law to a guardian.

> *"So then, the law was our guardian until Christ came, so that we might be justified by faith. But now that faith has come, we are no longer under a guardian."*
>
> — Galatians 3:24 to 25 (ESV)

The guardian was a trusted servant who walked a child to school and kept him out of trouble. Nobody despised him, and nobody kept him on once the child had grown. The Galatians were in danger of hiring him back.

But the same letter answers the objection we started with. In chapter 5 Paul warns that freedom is easily abused, and that believers are to serve one another through love (Galatians 5:13).

> *"For the whole law is fulfilled in one word: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'"*
>
> — Galatians 5:14 (ESV)

Read that against the young man's complaint. The same Paul who says we are free says the whole law is fulfilled in love of neighbour, and says it to people who have just been told they are not under the law. In Galatians 6:2 he says that bearing one another's burdens fulfils "the law of Christ." The phrase appears again when Paul describes his habits of mission.

> *"To those outside the law I became as one outside the law (not being outside the law of God but under the law of Christ) that I might win those outside the law."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 9:21 (ESV)

The law of Christ means the way of life Christ taught and lived, the pattern of self giving love that fulfils what the commandments were after. Scholars argue over whether it points to his specific teaching, to the old law as he interprets it, or to the shape of his own cross. The safe thing to say is that Paul will not let a believer be "outside the law of God." Whether you say the law of God or the law of Christ, the obligation is love, defined by a person and carried out by the Spirit.

## Why James Does Not Contradict Paul

Paul says a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law. James appears to disagree.

> *"You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone."*
>
> — James 2:24 (ESV)

It reads like a head on crash, but the two writers are answering different questions. Paul asks how a guilty sinner gets a verdict of righteous from God, and answers that it comes by trust in Christ, with no wage owed. James asks what a claim to faith is worth when it produces nothing. Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead, he concludes (James 2:17).

The word "justified" does slightly different work in the two letters. Paul uses it for God's verdict. James seems to use it for the proof that a faith is real, the way a claim is vindicated when tested. His own words give it away. "Show me your faith apart from your works," he says, "and I will show you my faith by my works" (James 2:18). Both writers use Abraham. Paul quotes Genesis 15 for the moment the verdict was counted. James uses the offering of Isaac in Genesis 22 as the moment the faith showed itself. These are two photographs of the same man on the same road.

And James has his own version of our verse. He calls the commandment to love your neighbour "the royal law" (James 2:8) and speaks of "the law of liberty" (James 2:12), pairing law and freedom exactly as Paul does in Galatians 5, where what counts is "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6). If the two men sat in one room, neither would be embarrassed by what the other had written.

## What the Jerusalem Council Settled

The church faced this question in a real meeting, recorded in Acts 15. Men came down to Antioch from Judea teaching that unless Gentile converts were circumcised according to the custom of Moses, they could not be saved. Peter answered at Jerusalem. He had watched the Holy Spirit fall on Gentiles who had never kept a ceremonial rule, and he asked why anyone should place on them a yoke "that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear." Then came the sentence that is Romans 3:28 to 30 in the mouth of a fisherman. "But we believe that we will be saved through the grace of the Lord Jesus, just as they will" (Acts 15:10 to 11, ESV). Peter, a Jew, doesn't say the Gentiles will be saved as we are. He says we will be saved as they are.

Yet the council did not conclude that anything goes. James proposed a letter asking the Gentile believers to abstain from things polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from what is strangled, and from blood (Acts 15:20). Two of those belong to the moral law on any reading. The other two concerned Jews and Gentiles sharing meals in one church. The Gentiles were freed from the ceremonial yoke, and the moral life was left untouched.

## What the Church Has Said Since

The church fathers read the verse as we have. John Chrysostom, the great preacher of Constantinople in the fourth century, gave a homily to this very verse. He argued that faith accomplished what the law aimed at and could not reach, and in the English translation I opened, his conclusion is that faith "has not disannulled, but perfected it" (Homily VII on Romans). Augustine, whom we have met, said the same against the Pelagians with more fire. For both men the law is a road that ends in Christ, and nobody abolishes a road by arriving.

The Westminster Confession of Faith, the doctrinal standard of Presbyterian churches around the world, gives chapter 19 to the subject, and it sorts the law into three kinds as we did. It says the ceremonial laws "are now abrogated, under the New Testament," meaning formally ended. It says the moral law "doth for ever bind all," believers and unbelievers alike. And it adds a careful sentence that fits Romans 3:31. Believers are not under the law as a covenant of works, a deal in which obedience earns the verdict, so it cannot justify or condemn them, yet it remains for them "a rule of life, informing them of the will of God and their duty." The Spirit of Christ is "subduing and enabling the will of man" to do freely what the law requires.

An African note belongs here, and not only because Augustine was one of ours. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, consecrated in 1864 as a bishop for West Africa, translated the New Testament into Yoruba, so a reader in Ibadan can meet Romans 3:31 in a mother tongue because an African pastor thought it worth the labour. I looked for what Nigerian and wider African evangelical writers have said on this verse and could not confirm a statement to quote, so I won't invent one.

## The New Perspective on Paul Fairly Stated

One more voice has changed how many scholars read Romans. It is called the New Perspective on Paul, and it deserves a fair statement.

The story begins with E. P. Sanders and his book *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977). Sanders argued that the older Protestant picture of first century Judaism as a religion of self made merit was a distortion. In the sources he read, God chose Israel first, and the law was given to people already in the covenant to shape their lives. He called this "covenantal nomism," roughly "life under the law within a covenant of grace." Nobody was trying to earn their way in. Obedience was how you stayed in.

James Dunn took this further in a 1982 lecture that gave the movement its name. Dunn suggested that when Paul says "works of the law" he has in mind practices that marked Jews off from their neighbours, such as circumcision, the food laws and the Sabbath. The trouble in Paul's churches was that some wanted to make those badges compulsory for Gentiles. N. T. Wright, in *What Saint Paul Really Said* (1997), pushed the point in a pastoral direction. For Wright the question Paul's gospel answers is who belongs to Abraham's family, and the answer is everyone who believes, which fits Romans 3:29 to 30. On his reading the Spirit writes the law on the hearts of this one family, and Romans 3:31 is about the Torah being truly kept by people the Spirit has changed.

That reading has taught us something real, since Paul was fighting about Jew and Gentile at one table. Critics have answered. Thomas Schreiner, in his commentary on Romans (1998), is among the most careful. He accepts that the boundary issue was real, and then argues that "works of the law" cannot be reduced to badges. Paul's case in Romans 1 to 3 is that no human being can keep the law as God requires, and that is why the verdict has to come by faith. As I read Schreiner, faith establishes the law because it fulfils what the law pointed to and because the Spirit finally enables the obedience the law demanded. In that, his reading ends where the older Reformed one does.

I'm not going to settle a debate that has filled libraries. Both sides hold that Paul does not abolish the law, that faith in Christ is the one way into God's family, and that the Spirit's work is what Romans 8 says it is. Whether "works of the law" meant earning a place or marking a boundary, the answer in 3:31 is the same. The law is not thrown out. It is carried to its goal.

## When Obedience Starts to Look Like Works

An honest reader will have been waiting to ask something. If believers are to keep the moral law, haven't works come back in through the side door? Isn't obedience just legalism with better manners?

It's a fair worry, and the answer lies in the direction of travel. A legalist obeys to be accepted. The believer obeys because he has been accepted. These look the same from the street and feel different from the inside. Picture a child sweeping the compound on a Saturday morning while her mother cooks. Nobody thinks she is paying rent. She sweeps because it is her home, and if she misses a corner she isn't put out of the house. A paid labourer sweeping the same yard has a different relationship to the broom altogether. Paul says this about Christians.

> *"For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand, that we should walk in them."*
>
> — Ephesians 2:10 (ESV)

The good works come after the creating, and God prepared them beforehand. Even the obedience is part of what he gives. If a believer's good deeds earned the verdict, the boasting Paul shut out in 3:27 would come straight back in. It can't, because the verdict was delivered at the cross, before the first deed.

There is a second answer for the anxious. Because obedience is a fruit and not a wage, a failure in it does not undo the verdict. A believer who stumbles is a child who has fallen in the yard. He is told to get up, and he is not told to leave. Paul says there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:1), and that sentence sits beside every command in the New Testament. The legalist hears the commands without the sentence and despairs. The antinomian hears the sentence without the commands and drifts. Romans 3:31 asks both to hear them together.

Paul's own answer comes in chapter 6. Should we continue in sin that grace may abound? He gives the same refusal he used in 3:31 (Romans 6:1 to 2). Believers have died to sin and been raised to walk in newness of life, and grace is the power that makes holiness possible.

## Where This Leaves the Person Asking

So, to the person who sent me the quick question. Why can't we abandon the law?

Because the law was never the enemy, and what condemned us was our failure to keep it. Christ didn't abolish the law's verdict; he took it. The Spirit doesn't discard the law's demand; he writes it where it can finally be obeyed. And the law that stood in court against us now stands in line with the gospel, a witness that God always meant to save by faith. To abandon it would be to say the cross was unnecessary and the Spirit is superfluous. Paul's answer is that we establish it.

If you have been told that grace means you need no longer care what God commands, I'd ask you to read Romans 6 through 8 in one sitting this week. Read slowly, and notice how often Paul answers the same question you're asking with the same refusal. And if you've been counting rules as though they might win God's favour, then read Romans 3:19 to 31 and rest. The verdict has been carried. You don't need to carry it too.

And one small thing after that. Think of a single commandment you have been treating as optional because you are under grace. It may be honesty in a small matter, or forgiving a relative, or what you do with your money. Tell God in plain words that you want to keep it and can't do it alone, and ask the Spirit for the wanting and the strength. That is Romans 8:4 in an ordinary week.

Then do what you did before. Send another quick question. I'll answer it as soon as the generator is running.
