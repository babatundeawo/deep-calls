## What Proverbs Means by Favour

Anyone who has stood in a Nigerian church car park after the second service will have seen the word. It is on banners and car stickers, on programme themes, on the backs of shirts, and in the mouth of the preacher who has just asked the whole hall to say it together. Favour. Say it loudly enough, the implication runs, and the gates of heaven, the visa office and the admissions list will all give way. Somewhere between the pulpit and the car park the word became one of our most overworked theological exports, and in the process we have often chased the thing while losing the map that leads to it.

The people who say it are not fools. A young graduate with no uncle in the ministry, no cousin at the bank and no name that opens doors needs to hear that God is not limited to the network she lacks. Daniel Jordan Smith, an anthropologist who has written on Nigerian prosperity churches, reports that church leaders often present their own wealth as the evidence of God's blessing, and that members mostly accept this even though they are far poorer than the people leading them (Smith, 2021). The teaching has real pull because the need is real. A pastor can fairly add that God does open doors, that Joseph was lifted out of a prison, and that to say otherwise would be to preach a very small God. I agree with every word of that. What I want to ask is whether the word *favour*, as Proverbs uses it, is the right name for the door.

Honest meeting begins with the word itself. In three sayings of Proverbs, 8:35, 12:2 and 18:22, the writer uses the same Hebrew phrase about obtaining favour from the LORD. The word is *ratson*, and the standard lexicons give it as goodwill, acceptance, delight, pleasure. It describes the face of a superior turning warmly towards someone. That is a good deal more intimate than a result. It is a relationship in which God, who owes us nothing, is pleased, and a person who has that doesn't need a password. Doors may open afterwards, or they may stay shut for a long while, but the favour was already there. It cannot be manufactured by seed offerings and declarations, any more than you can manufacture the affection of a father.

Proverbs, that unsentimental book of wisdom, nonetheless tells us there are concrete, liveable places where this favour is met. It gives three, each anchored to a verse, each reaching into a different room of ordinary life. One concerns the pursuit of wisdom, one concerns goodness of character, and one concerns marriage. I want to take them one at a time, set them beside the rest of Scripture, and be honest at each step about what the text says and what it doesn't.

## How Far the Cord Image Goes

The image that holds the three together comes from Ecclesiastes, and it needs a careful word before we lean on it. The writer is talking about companionship. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil. If one falls, the other lifts him up. Two lying together keep warm. And at the end of that run comes the line.

> *"a threefold cord is not quickly broken."*
> — Ecclesiastes 4:12 (ESV)

The translators' handbook on Ecclesiastes by Graham Ogden and Lynell Zogbo calls it an ancient Israelite proverb about the strength of combined effort, one that may well have a counterpart in many cultures, and it adds that the number three need not be literal. Nothing in the verse names wisdom, character and marriage as the three strands, and I won't pretend it does. Nor does it name the Father, Son and Holy Spirit, however many wedding programmes have printed it that way. The verse is about people helping one another against a robber, a fall, or a cold night.

So the cord is a picture borrowed to carry something the verse never says. I think the borrowing is fair, because the three Proverbs really do share a phrase and a God who is doing the favouring, and a Christian reader is entitled to see a further meaning in Scripture when the whole of Scripture supports it. But it is a picture, and when it begins to sound like a formula in which each strand is a lever, it has stopped being one. A cord can be pulled apart by a determined hand. The saying only says it takes more doing.

## Favour in a Prison and a Palace

Before the three sayings, it is worth seeing how widely the Bible spreads this idea, because the spread corrects the common picture. Favour in Scripture is nearly always found in difficult places.

Joseph is the first case. Genesis 39 tells us that the LORD was with him in Potiphar's house and that Joseph found favour in his master's sight. Then Potiphar's wife lies about him, and Joseph is thrown into prison, where the text says again that the LORD was with him and gave him favour in the sight of the keeper of the prison. Read that slowly. Favour was in the house, and favour was in the dungeon, and between the two stood a false accusation and a locked door. It did not keep him out of the cell. It made him trusted inside it. The favour of God in that chapter is the presence of God inside the hardship, with no promise of its removal, and, in time, the credit with other human beings that a person earns by being the same man in every room.

Esther is the second. The book of Esther never names God, which makes it a good book for a generation that complains of a silent heaven. In Esther 2:17 the king loved Esther more than all the other women, and she "won grace and favor in his sight more than all the virgins." That is the verse people put on the poster. The sequel is less often quoted. When Haman's decree threatens her people, she is told that being queen will not protect her. To go to the king uninvited could mean death, and she says as much, deciding to go all the same: "if I perish, I perish" (Esther 4:16). The king did hold out the sceptre (5:2), and she lived. But she did not know that when she walked in. Favour had put her in a position to act. It had not removed the cost of acting.

Then there is Psalm 5:12, which says the LORD blesses the righteous and covers him with favour as with a shield. A shield is a curious image for a prize. Nobody carries a shield who is not expecting a fight.

Put these together and the Bible's account of favour comes into focus. It is the pleasure of God resting on a person, it shows up in prison cells and palaces and battlefields, it opens doors some of the time, and it never exempts anyone from the road. With that in hand we can turn to the three sayings.

## Wisdom Calling at the Crossroads

The first saying is spoken by Wisdom herself.

> *"For whoever finds me finds life and obtains favor from the LORD,"*
> — Proverbs 8:35 (ESV)

Wisdom in Proverbs 8 is not found in a quiet library. She stands where the roads meet and the town gates open and cries out to anyone who passes. The ESV puts it plainly, that on the heights beside the way, at the crossroads, she takes her stand and cries aloud, and her cry is to the children of man. Picture the junction outside any Nigerian market on a busy morning, with every kind of person and every kind of hurry, and someone in the middle of it calling out for people to stop and listen. Most of the traffic goes round her.

Later in the chapter she says she was beside the LORD like a master workman, daily his delight, rejoicing before him. The Hebrew poets are speaking of wisdom as something older than the world, close to God, glad of what he made. Christians have always heard more than poetry here. Paul calls Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.

> *"but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God."*
> — 1 Corinthians 1:24 (ESV)

John's Gospel opens by describing the Word who was with God in the beginning and through whom all things were made, and Paul says of Christ that in him are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge (Colossians 2:3). The early church put these passages together. The voice at the crossroads, who was with God from the beginning and who delights in the human race, is the one who became flesh and walked into the city that would crucify him.

Care is needed, and the early church found it out the hard way. In the fourth century the Arians seized on the Greek form of Proverbs 8:22, in which the LORD *created* Wisdom, as proof that the Son was a creature. Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, answered across his *Four Discourses against the Arians*, written between 356 and 360 during one of his exiles. According to Geoffrey Boyle's account, he held that the speaker is Wisdom, the Son, and that Proverbs must not be expounded in its bare first sense. The ESV has *possessed* where the Arians read *created*, and the translation question is worth a whole sermon on its own. What matters here is that one of the great African theologians of the early church did not treat Proverbs 8 as a self help chapter. He took it as speech from a person, and the Council of Nicaea in 325 had already confessed the Son as of one substance with the Father.

That changes what *finding* wisdom means. It isn't acquiring a skill, or collecting maxims for a good life, though Proverbs has those too. It means coming to the one who calls and learning to take his voice as the voice that settles questions. Plenty of us come to him when the shortcuts have failed. The saying asks us to start there, which is a rather different order of business. Augustine, who was himself a North African and spent years running through every shortcut available, opens his *Confessions* with the line that gives the reason. God has made us for himself, he wrote, and "our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Augustine, trans. 1838, Book 1). Wisdom is what finding the one who ends that restlessness feels like from the inside.

There's an obvious objection, which is that Wisdom's promise sounds like a transaction. Find me, and you get favour. But the text reads as an invitation as much as a promise, and the favour it names is the delight of the one who calls. Anyone who has been wanted by a person they admire knows that nobody buys that. C. S. Lewis made the same complaint from another side in his 1941 sermon *The Weight of Glory*, where he said we desire too little and are far too easily pleased, like a child content with mud pies in a slum because he cannot imagine a holiday at the sea (Taylor, n.d.). The person chasing favour as a result is, in that sense, settling for the mud pie.

Picture a final year student in Zaria choosing between two supervisors and two project topics. She prays about it for a week and fasts on the Friday, and she never once opens Proverbs, where the question of whose counsel to trust is spelled out at length. Nothing is wrong with the prayer or the fast. But the voice at the crossroads has been speaking the whole time, and she has walked past it to ask a different door for a signal. Anyone who has watched a friend buy anointed water by the litre, or sow into every basket within driving distance, while keeping the Gospels shut, will know this scene without needing it named.

## Good Men, Evil Devices and Noah

The second saying is blunter.

> *"A good man obtains favor from the LORD, but a man of evil devices he condemns."*
> — Proverbs 12:2 (ESV)

Proverbs has no gentle category here, and the line is stark. Yet the word *good* deserves a fair hearing. The Hebrew *tov* means beneficial, wholesome, ordered towards the right end. The good man of Proverbs isn't a man who never falls. The writer says in 24:16 that the righteous falls seven times and rises again, and the wicked are the ones who stay down when calamity comes. What marks him is the direction of his getting up.

Picture a quantity surveyor in Port Harcourt, a church member with a clean Sunday record, who has been adding ten per cent to every bill because the clients expect it and everybody does it. (This is a constructed case, though anyone in the building trade will know the type.) For years he has kept his Sunday self and his Monday self from being introduced. One morning, in an ordinary quiet time, he reads Proverbs 12:2 and cannot shake the phrase *evil devices*. He stops. The text doesn't promise him a bigger firm. It promises that the LORD turns his face toward a man like that, and what that looks like in a building trade run on envelopes might be a clear conscience, a name that holds up under audit, one client who stays because the numbers are straight. It might also be a lean year, and some clients lost for good. The saying is honest enough not to say.

The favoured man everybody reaches for is Noah.

> *"But Noah found favor in the eyes of the LORD."*
> — Genesis 6:8 (ESV)

The preacher usually says that Noah got favour because he was righteous. Look at the order. Genesis 6:5 describes a world in which every intention of the human heart was only evil continually, and the very next thing, in verse 8, is that Noah found favour. Only after that does the writer tell us Noah was a righteous man, blameless in his generation, who walked with God. The Hebrew word for favour here is *chen*, which the lexicons give as grace, a different word from the *ratson* of Proverbs. So the two texts don't say the same thing, and the difference matters. Genesis leaves it open whether the righteousness earned the favour or grew out of it. A careful reader should say that and stop. And notice the phrase that closes the description. Noah walked with God, which is the language of company, not of performance. Noah did not understand the flood before it came. He knew the one who warned him about it, and that was enough to build a boat on dry ground.

Paul settles it for those who come after. We are saved by grace through faith, and then, as he goes on in Ephesians 2:10, we are God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works that God prepared beforehand. The good man of Proverbs 12:2 is a person being made good by a God who has already turned toward him, and no one climbs to favour on his own effort. That is the line between religion, where we climb, and the gospel, where we are carried and then taught to walk.

A neighbouring saying shows what that goodness looks like in public.

> *"Let not steadfast love and faithfulness forsake you; bind them around your neck; write them on the tablet of your heart. So you will find favor and good success in the sight of God and man."*
> — Proverbs 3:3 and 4 (ESV)

The ESV footnotes *good success* as *repute*, and that is the sense I'd keep. A life of steady faithfulness collects a reputation slowly, in the eyes of people who had no reason to be generous. Most Nigerians can name the person in their office, their parish or their street whom nobody has ever needed to watch, and everyone knows what that name is worth when a stranger needs a signature or a guarantor. Promotion is not promised. What comes is the kind of standing that makes a person trustable, and it comes only to those who have been trustworthy for years, including in the years when it paid nothing. Joseph in the prison was an example of exactly this.

## Finding a Wife, and Not Finding One

The third saying is the one that makes people shift in their seats.

> *"He who finds a wife finds a good thing and obtains favor from the LORD."*
> — Proverbs 18:22 (ESV)

As specific and unspiritual sounding as a verse can get. The same phrase used for wisdom and godly character now applied to marriage, as though God wants us to know he has definite opinions about this corner of our lives. The ESV cross refers it to Proverbs 31:10, the portrait of the excellent wife, and it is worth noticing what that portrait shows. She is busy, shrewd with money, generous to the poor, and trusted by her husband. Nobody could take the passage for a picture of a decorative companion. It portrays wisdom lived out in a household, and when a man finds someone like that, the text says he has received something from God.

Paul takes this earthy picture and lifts it. Husbands are told to love their wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her (Ephesians 5:25), and Paul calls the whole arrangement a mystery that refers to Christ and the church. Catholic and Orthodox Christians call marriage a sacrament, and most Protestants prefer to say covenant and creation ordinance, but every tradition can agree with Paul's word. Marriage is a living picture. A marriage run on the engine of costly, faithful love shows something to anyone watching, even those who can't say what it is they see. That is why Proverbs' remark carries weight beyond romance. To find a wife and to keep her well is, in a small way, to take part in the work by which God shows himself in the world.

The same Ephesians text speaks to people whose marriages are hard. Nobody who has sat through a counselling session with a couple who haven't spoken properly in a year needs to be told that finding a wife is a good thing. They need to hear that Christ's way of loving, giving himself up, is something a person can begin to practise on a Tuesday evening whatever the other person does, and that the practice is itself a place where God's face turns towards them.

Now say what a careful reader has to say next. Proverbs 18:22 is an observation, a wisdom saying, about what usually happens when a man finds a good wife. No law that everyone must marry sits behind it, and no verdict on anyone who hasn't. Preachers often tell the unmarried that wisdom comes first, then character, then in its proper season the gift, and that you cannot shortcut the order. As preparation, that is sound. A person who goes looking for a spouse with no wisdom and no character will probably wreck the marriage, and the church does well to say so. But think of the widow in her fifties, the man whose wife has left, the woman who prayed for a husband through her thirties and is now at peace with God but still lonely in a hall full of couples. A sermon that sells this verse as the next step in a queue tells those people the queue has closed on them. I think that is cruel, and I don't believe the text intends it.

The better question is whether the verse can bear the weight of a sequence at all. The man about whom Luke twice says that the favour of God rested on him never married.

## Favour in the Life of Jesus

Luke gives us the test case, and it unsettles every neat version of this subject. When the angel came to Mary, he said what the favour talk says.

> *"Greetings, O favored one, the Lord is with you!"*
> — Luke 1:28 (ESV)

Luke does not say that she opened a door, got a job or avoided trouble. The next verse says she was greatly troubled. The angel tells her not to be afraid, for she has found favor with God, and what follows is a pregnancy before the wedding in a small town, the talk that anyone who knows small towns can imagine, and in time the death of her son. Favour in her case looked like being trusted with the hardest assignment in the world.

Then there is the child himself.

> *"And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature and in favor with God and man."*
> — Luke 2:52 (ESV)

Here are the three Proverbs in a single sentence. He grew in wisdom, he was good beyond any man's reach, and he had the favour of God and of men. Yet he never married, he owned almost nothing, and the same people who watched him grow in favour with them ended up demanding his death. If favour meant ease, he is a sad case. If favour is the delight of the Father resting on a person, then no one has ever had more of it, and Calvary falls inside it, not outside.

That is the turn this essay has been working towards, and it lands hard on both of the easy versions. The password version, where favour is a result, has no way to explain Mary or Jesus. The tidy sermon version, where favour is the reward for completing three steps, has no way to explain a Christian who is wise, good and single, or wise, good and married and still ill, still poor, still unanswered. Both versions run out of road at the cross. The cross is the one place where the favour of God and a great deal of suffering meet in the same person, and it tells us the favour was never the same thing as the outcome.

Proverbs has always known its limits. The book is a collection of sayings about how God's world usually runs, and the people who preserved it also preserved Job and Ecclesiastes, which spend a good deal of their length noting that the usual doesn't always happen. To read Proverbs 8:35, 12:2 and 18:22 as guarantees is to ask the book to speak louder than its own editors allowed.

## How the Prosperity Message Uses the Word

It is time to look squarely at the way the word is used in many of our churches, and to do it fairly, because the people using it are often sincere and sometimes right.

State its best case first. Favour language grew in Nigeria in a country where jobs are scarce, institutions often fail the poor and the connected do well. To tell a young person that the favour of the Most High can reach where connections cannot is a kind of pastoral defiance. The Nigerian church historian Ogbu Kalu, in his survey of African Pentecostalism, argued against those who dismiss the prosperity message as a foreign import. As one review of his book summarises him, he held that its appeal rests on African ideas of salvation as wholeness, which include peace of mind, healing, deliverance, fertility and community harmony, and not wealth alone (Christianity Today, 2009). Seen that way, a prayer for favour is a prayer for a whole and flourishing life, which is not a shameful thing to want, and Scripture itself speaks of the LORD's blessing in earthly terms.

Now the cautions. First, the word in Scripture is a relationship, and the preaching often turns it into an outcome. When a shout of "favour" is meant to produce a particular interview result, the word has been shifted from the face of God to the hand of God, and from the Giver to the gift. Second, the teaching reads favour as a reward for a transaction. Seed sown, favour reaped. The same review of Kalu notes that a small number of urban ministries do lean towards crude acquisition and individualism, and Smith's research suggests that members are asked to see leaders' wealth as proof of the system (Smith, 2021). Third, it cannot explain what Scripture actually shows. Joseph had favour in a dungeon, Esther risked her neck, Mary was shamed, and Jesus was crucified. A doctrine of favour that fits only the people who are doing well fits the book of Proverbs badly and fits the Gospels not at all.

I grew up adjacent to that world, and I know the pull of it. I also know the person who leaves it quietly, after the formula failed her, still believing in God, and nobody had told her that the formula was never promised. If we do the pastoral work, we can tell her what the three sayings actually offer. They describe the kind of life in which the face of God is turned toward us, and a promise that it is.

## Three Strands and the Triune God

Having tested the sayings against the wider canon, we can see why they keep company. Paul tells the Corinthians that because of God they are in Christ Jesus, who became to us wisdom from God, righteousness and sanctification and redemption (1 Corinthians 1:30, ESV, quoted in part). The wisdom that calls at the crossroads and the goodness that rises after a fall are two gifts, and the same person gives them to those who belong to him, and the love of Ephesians 5 is the pattern he sets for how it is lived with another person. A cord is strong because the strands run the same way. These do, since each one begins with Christ and returns to him.

There is also a trinitarian echo that the cord image invites, and I want to treat it with the same care I gave the cord. Genesis 1:26 has God saying, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness." Jewish and Christian interpreters have read that plural in several ways, as a heavenly court, as a plural of deliberation, or, in the Christian tradition, as an early sign of the fullness of God that the New Testament makes plain. The verse alone does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity, and I will not claim that it does. What can fairly be said is that when the New Testament shows the Son as the wisdom through whom all things were made and the Spirit as the one who fills, it gives us a God who exists in relationship, and a humanity made in his image for relationship. Genesis 2:24 then joins husband and wife as one flesh, and Genesis 1:28 blesses them to be fruitful. Godly marriage serves more than the two people in it, and is one of the ways in which that relational image is lived out, and when it is lived well the world notices, even when it cannot name what it sees.

It also keeps the whole matter honest. If wisdom, goodness and love are things he gives, then none of us can take the credit, and none of us has to hold the favour in place by performance. We are asked to follow, which is harder on the pride and gentler on the nerves than any formula.

## What to Do on Monday Morning

So what are we to do with a cord of three strands that isn't a formula? I'd offer three small things, and none of them is a slogan.

Start with the one who calls. Some of the people who have chased favour hardest have spent very little time just sitting with the Gospels and hearing Wisdom talk. If you have a hundred hours for a vigil, you can find fifteen minutes with Luke. Let Jesus speak first, before the shortcuts have failed. That is the first strand.

Then look at your work this week the way Proverbs 12:2 would. Is there a device in it, a padded figure, a small silence, a favour owed that nobody has named? You needn't be perfect, and the text doesn't ask for perfect. It asks what happens after you fall, and which direction you rise in.

Third, whether you are married or looking, widowed or content, hold the picture of Ephesians 5 in front of you as a standard for the love you give, because every one of us lives in some kind of relationship that Christ's love is meant to shape. Your parents, your congregation, the flatmate you can't stand. The cord of favour doesn't stop at the door of a church wedding.

And if you are in a season where none of the three appears to be working, where you have prayed and sat with Luke and told the truth about the invoice and still the door has not opened, you are in the same room as Mary on the morning after the angel left, and as Esther on the way to the king's court. They had the favour. They also had a long road ahead of them. Perhaps the next time you hear the word shouted across a car park, you could answer it quietly with a question that Proverbs and the Gospels both seem to ask. Whose delight are you living in, and what has he asked you to carry?
