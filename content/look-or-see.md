## A Woman in a Hospital Corridor

Here is a scene, made up but familiar. A woman stands in a hospital corridor in Lagos holding an X ray up to the fluorescent light. The radiographer left ten minutes ago, and the doctor has spoken to her and gone on to his next patient. Her sister has called twice and she hasn't picked up. She keeps holding the film, though she cannot read it as the doctor reads it, and what she is really doing is something the plastic cannot help with. She is deciding what she believes about her own life.

Everyone who passes that corridor can look at the same grey rectangle. Only she has to decide what she sees in it.

I think this is the oldest argument in Scripture, and it has very little to do with eyesight. From the garden, where a woman looked at fruit and didn't see the death inside it, to a hill outside Jerusalem, where soldiers looked at a dying man and didn't see the Judge of the earth, the Bible keeps making one uncomfortable claim. Two people can stand in the same spot, take in the same facts through the same eyes, and walk away having met two different realities. The difference was never in what was set in front of them. It lay in what they were able, or willing, to receive.

It helps to remember how ordinary eyes work. They gather light and send signals to the brain, and the brain turns the signals into a picture. Two people with perfect vision can be handed the same picture and read it differently, because what they bring to it differs. A farmer and a surveyor look at the same field and see two things. Nobody calls either of them a liar.

Picture another scene, equally made up. A lecturer in a Nigerian university puts a slide on the screen, one photograph of a sunrise over a river. A student in the third row writes down the time of day. The student beside her writes down a question about who made the river. The slide is the same and the room is the same. Something happens between the eye and the mind that the projector doesn't control.

Looking belongs to biology. Seeing is something God has to give. And a good part of the Christian faith rests on the conviction that he cares more about the second than the first. In what follows I want to trace that conviction through the Bible, test it against Nigerian experience and against the sceptic's best objection, and end where the Bible ends this argument, with a prayer for sight.

## Two Verbs and a Wide Gap

Looking is cheap. It costs a working eye and nothing else. A man can look at a bank statement, a result sheet, a wedding ring, a burning bush or a cross and register only data. Looking answers one question, which is what is physically in front of me.

Seeing costs more, because it asks what the thing means, and meaning is never neutral. It makes a claim on whoever receives it. Perhaps that is why the Bible so rarely asks its characters what they are looking at. Again and again it asks them what they see.

The young Jeremiah, trembling under a job he never applied for, gets exactly that question.

> *"And the word of the LORD came to me, saying, 'Jeremiah, what do you see?' And I said, 'I see an almond branch.' Then the LORD said to me, 'You have seen well, for I am watching over my word to perform it.'"*
> — Jeremiah 1:11 to 12 (ESV)

An almond branch is nothing special. It is one of the first trees to flower in the year, ordinary enough that a boy could pass a hundred of them on the way to the well. But in Hebrew the word for almond, *shaqed*, sounds almost exactly like the word for watching, *shoqed*, and that connection can't be seen with the eye. It can only be given. The question was never about eyesight. God was finding out whether the young man could receive a meaning that the branch could never produce on its own. And notice the commendation. "You have seen well" is said to a man who has done nothing more than name a twig. The seeing was in the meaning that followed.

The prophets keep working this way. Amos is shown a plumb line and a basket of summer fruit, and each one is explained to him as a verdict on a nation drifting from justice (Amos 7:7 to 9; 8:1 to 2). Zechariah sees lampstands and flying scrolls and has an angel beside him to say what they are (Zechariah 4:1 to 6; 5:1 to 4). None of them was praised for sharp eyes. They were commended, or corrected, for what they did with the thing once it was in front of them. Revelation is never manufactured by the human eye. It is received.

Jesus puts the same thing in blunter words. He tells his hearers that seeing, they do not see, and he borrows the sentence from Isaiah's commissioning, where the prophet is sent to a people whose eyes will look and not perceive (Matthew 13:13 to 15; Isaiah 6:9 to 10). Both passages are hard, and the hardness is deliberate. They describe a condition that ordinary looking cannot cure, because the trouble is not with the eye.

Mark has an odd little arrangement that makes the point better than any sermon. In chapter 8 Jesus rebukes the disciples in the boat, asking if they have eyes and do not see (Mark 8:18). Then, almost at once, he heals a blind man at Bethsaida in two stages. After the first touch the man sees people, but they look like trees walking about, and it takes a second touch before he sees clearly (Mark 8:22 to 26). A few verses later Peter says, at Caesarea Philippi, that Jesus is the Christ (Mark 8:27 to 29). I do not think the placement is an accident. The Gospel is showing that sight can come in stages, that the first stage can be real and still muddled, and that Peter's confession has to be read as a man who has just been touched once and is not yet seeing clearly. That detail will matter when we get to Peter's rebuke of his Lord.

John 9 is the longest treatment of the theme in the New Testament. A man born blind is healed on the Sabbath, and the religious authorities spend the rest of the chapter interrogating everyone but themselves. At the close Jesus says he came into the world so that those who do not see may see, and those who see may become blind. Some Pharisees ask if they are blind too, and he answers that if they were blind they would have no guilt, but because they say "We see", their guilt remains (John 9:39 to 41). The ones who knew they could not see were helped. The ones who were sure they could were left where they were. I come back to this at the end, because it shapes how an apologist should behave.

## People Who Stood in the Same Place

Scripture keeps an almost embarrassing record of people who stood directly in front of the truth and missed it.

Balaam, a prophet by trade, was riding along when his donkey stopped dead and refused to move. He beat the animal three times, sure it was being stubborn. In the road stood an angel of the LORD with a drawn sword, and the donkey could see him and the professional could not. Only when God opened the prophet's eyes did he see the angel standing in the way (Numbers 22:31). I'm always grateful that the writer tells the story without apology. A donkey outperformed a prophet that day, and Scripture uses the joke to make a serious point about credentials and sight. Training is not the same as seeing. A person can be professionally religious and perfectly blind.

Then there were the twelve scouts sent into Canaan. All twelve crossed the same river and walked the same hills. All twelve stood under the same walls and carried home the same enormous cluster of grapes. Ten came back talking about giants and about feeling like grasshoppers. Two, Joshua and Caleb, came back saying the land could be taken, because the LORD was with them (Numbers 13:25 to 33; 14:6 to 9). The facts were identical. The reports were not, because ten men read the evidence through their fear and two read it through the character of God. Faith, on this telling, does not deny hard facts. It refuses to let hard facts have the last word over a promise.

During the same wilderness years, when poisonous snakes were biting the people, God told Moses to set a bronze serpent on a pole, and anyone who looked at it lived (Numbers 21:4 to 9). The remedy was a look. Not a pilgrimage or a sacrifice, only a look at the thing God had appointed, held up in front of the whole camp. Centuries later Jesus tells Nicodemus that the Son of Man must be lifted up in the same way, so that whoever believes in him may have eternal life (John 3:14 to 15). In John's Gospel the look of the camp and the belief of the sinner are the same act. To look at the lifted Christ and see what he is, is to be saved, and the looking is done with a heart that has been given something to look with.

And there was a man at the Jordan, watching a crowd gather to see a carpenter's son from Galilee wade into the river. Most of that crowd saw Mary's boy. John the Baptist saw something else, and said so aloud, calling him the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (John 1:29). Nothing about the scene had changed. Only the perception had. John himself says that he did not know him until it was shown to him (John 1:31 to 33). Even the one who saw first had to be given the sight.

## What God Looks At

This isn't a human theory about how to read ambiguous signs. It starts with God. When Samuel went to anoint Israel's next king, he took one look at Eliab, the eldest and tallest of Jesse's sons, and assumed the search was over.

> *"But the LORD said to Samuel, 'Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature, because I have rejected him. For the LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.'"*
> — 1 Samuel 16:7 (ESV)

Man looks and God sees. A great deal of the Bible reads like commentary on that contrast. In the opening scene of Job, the accuser appears before God with a report built entirely on what can be observed. Job is prosperous, Job is protected, there is a hedge around his house and his flocks. That was the extent of what the accuser's eyes could register, possessions and fences and circumstance. God's testimony about Job rests on something the report never touched, a man who is blameless and upright, who fears God and turns away from evil (Job 1:1 to 11). The accuser saw wealth. God saw character. The difference between them was not information. It was the faculty of perception.

Jesus presses the contrast against the religious experts of his own day. He tells them they are like whitewashed tombs, handsome on the outside and full of death within (Matthew 23:27 to 28). A tomb whitewashed for a festival looks very good from the road. That is the whole trouble with it.

It would be wrong to leave this as a threat, though, because the seeing God is also the God who sees the afflicted. When the Lord speaks to Moses from the bush he says that he has seen the misery of his people in Egypt (Exodus 3:7). Hagar, a slave woman driven into the wilderness with a child, meets the Lord at a spring and gives him a name nobody else in the Old Testament gives him, calling him the God who sees her (Genesis 16:13). If you are the person in the hospital corridor, that is the sentence to keep. Whatever else is unclear in the film, you are seen, with full understanding, by someone who does not look away.

Anyone who has sat through a Nigerian church service knows the warning about appearances in its everyday form. A preacher says, often with a smile, that a big testimony is not automatically a sign of God's favour, and that a well polished exterior can sit beside a heart that has quietly wandered. The congregation says amen, and then looks at the man in the front row with the best suit. Even the amens need the question God asked Samuel.

## Nigerian Voices on the Seen and the Unseen

Chinua Achebe's novel *Arrow of God* is set among the Igbo people at the point where British power is arriving. Its chief priest, Ezeulu, sends one of his sons to the missionaries to learn what they know. His reasoning is summed up in a saying that Nigerian readers recognise at once.

> *"The world is like a Mask dancing. If you want to see it well you do not stand in one place."*
> — Achebe, *Arrow of God* (1964)

Whatever one makes of Ezeulu's plan (it goes badly wrong, and the son becomes a zealot who wants to kill a sacred python), the saying itself is sound. A single fixed viewpoint gives a partial picture, and a partial picture taken for the whole is a kind of blindness that looks like certainty. It is the mistake the ten scouts made, standing at one spot with one fear and treating it as the whole of the land. Nobody needed a Western classroom to arrive at the saying. It is African wisdom in its own right, and it sits close to what Scripture says about looking from one angle and calling it seeing.

Achebe's world also never treated the unseen as a side issue. In *Things Fall Apart* he writes that "The land of the living was not far removed from the domain of the ancestors." Many nineteenth century missionaries heard that kind of talk and decided it was superstition to be corrected. Yet the Bible they carried has a heavenly council in Job, rulers contending over nations in Daniel, and a war in Ephesians fought against powers in the heavenly places. On this one point the African instinct that the visible world is thin was closer to the pages of Scripture than the tidy rationalism that arrived with some of the missionaries.

The Kenyan theologian John Mbiti spent a career arguing that African Christians had not started from nothing. In *Bible and Theology in African Christianity* (1986), as one summary of the book puts it, he holds that African Christians built their faith on existing traditional religions, aided by the biblical revelation and faith in Jesus Christ. He treats the person as a whole in whom body and spirit belong together, and he is wary of setting prayer for healing against physical remedies, since God can use physical means too. He also notes that prayer is, for many Africans, a practical defence against misfortune, and that the churches that grew out of African initiative have often kept this alive while mission churches were inclined to call it superstition. I would not lean on every conclusion Mbiti draws. But his starting point is right and it bears on our subject. A person who takes the unseen seriously begins with a wider field of vision than a person who has been taught that only what can be measured is real. The question is then what to do with the wider field, and here the Nigerian evangelical Byang Kato is a useful corrective. Kato was born in 1936 in what is now Kaduna State, became the first African general secretary of the continent's evangelical association, and in *Theological Pitfalls in Africa* (1975) pressed his worry that African Christianity was drifting toward universalism and syncretism. His point does not cancel Mbiti's. It adds to it. A wide field is good, and it must be tested against Scripture, because a person who sees spirits everywhere is not thereby seeing truly.

Put the two together and you have the shape of the matter. Western rationalism can narrow the eye, and credulity can crowd it. The biblical answer is to ask for sight that is given and then to check what it shows by the written word.

## A Bishop Nobody Would See

This is where the story of Samuel Ajayi Crowther deserves a long memory rather than a short one.

In 1821 a Yoruba boy called Ajayi was captured and sold, and a British naval patrol intercepted the ship that was carrying him and put him ashore in Sierra Leone. He was baptised in 1825 with the name Samuel Crowther, and in 1864 he was consecrated a bishop, the first African bishop in the Church of England (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.; EBSCO, n.d., Crowther entry). He worked on Yoruba versions of Scripture and liturgy, and he led a mission on the Niger in which, as one account puts it, a network of African pastors under his supervision set up congregations in the interior. Then came the reversal. In the 1880s younger European missionaries challenged the arrangement and took control, a commission investigated alleged abuses in his office, the society reprimanded him, and most of his African pastors were dismissed. He was forced to resign from the finance committee and was pushed out of his leadership in 1890. He died in 1891 (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.; EBSCO, n.d., Crowther entry).

Look at what the evidence was. Crowther's learning, his translation work and his office were all in plain sight. The people who removed him were looking straight at them. They could see an African with a bishop's authority, and some of them could not see a brother in Christ. Looking, again, was cheaper than seeing. Prejudice works the way spiritual blindness always works in the Bible, by letting a person stand in front of overwhelming evidence and stay unconvinced.

The Gambian scholar Lamin Sanneh read this history from the other side. As a reviewer summarises his argument in *Translating the Message* (1989), Sanneh held that once the Bible was put into local languages, African readers could judge Western church assumptions for themselves, and they began to hold the missionaries to what the Scriptures said. African languages were strengthened rather than erased in the process. That is exactly what a Crowther did, and it is why the story does not end in defeat. The translated Bible was a pair of spectacles the missionaries themselves had made and could not take back. Once it was in the hands of Africans, the Bible could be read as it had never been read in Europe, and the first thing a reader saw in it was that there is neither Jew nor Greek in Christ, with the hurt of the Niger mission in the margin (Galatians 3:28).

## Looking Rich and Calling It Sight

I owe honesty to the other side too. The same warmth toward the unseen that gives African Christianity much of its life can be bent into something the older believers would hardly recognise. Prayer, once a costly wrestling before a holy God, is sold by the sachet. A handkerchief soaked in oil is offered like a subscription. A private jet is called a sign. None of that is African wisdom. It is a counterfeit that borrows African dress, and it confuses looking prosperous with seeing God.

Notice that this is Samuel's mistake again, only with the money attached. When the man in the front row has the best suit, and the preacher tells him his suit is the evidence of his faith, the congregation is being taught to look at Eliab and call it seeing. God's reply to Samuel has never been repealed.

The church that produced Crowther, and that still produces vigils where thousands cry out honestly to God in the small hours, deserves a theology that can tell the difference.

## The Disciple Who Saw Only Defeat

The sharpest example in the Gospels is Simon Peter, who loved Jesus more visibly than any of the others and still got the difference between looking and seeing exactly backwards.

Jesus had just told his disciples plainly that he was going to Jerusalem to suffer and die. Peter took him aside and told him it must never happen (Matthew 16:22). To Peter the cross meant defeat, and he said so out of love. Jesus, in the same conversation, saw the redemption of the world folded inside the same instrument. His reply was sharp because the stakes of misperception were so high. He told Peter that he was a hindrance to him, because his mind was set on the things of men and not the things of God (Matthew 16:23).

Peter had devotion to spare. What he lacked was sight, and he had company. Two disciples would later walk to Emmaus describing the week's events in complete despair to a stranger who was the risen Christ, and they did not know him until he broke bread, when their eyes were opened (Luke 24:13 to 31). Mary Magdalene stood outside an empty tomb weeping, and took the Lord for the gardener (John 20:11 to 16). Neither group was lazy or dishonest. Their grief had already told them what the world looked like, and the world had to say it twice before they listened.

Most of us have mistaken a closed door for an ending. We have read a delayed visa, a failed interview or a long silence as God's last word, when it was the hinge on which the next thing turned. I can't promise it always is. But Peter's story warns us how confidently a loving person can be wrong about what he is looking at. And Jesus' response to Peter holds out a hope as well as a rebuke, because the man he called a hindrance on one afternoon was the man who preached on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:14 to 41). Sight can be given to the slowest of us, and it can be given in stages, as at Bethsaida.

## One Hill and Several Verdicts

Which brings us back to the hill outside Jerusalem, the hardest test of the whole argument.

Thousands of people looked at the crucifixion of Jesus. Soldiers looked, and so did religious leaders and passersby, and his mother. The conclusions they drew from one public scene went in every direction. Some saw a criminal getting overdue justice, and some a failed revolutionary. One of the two men crucified beside him, in the middle of his own agony, saw a King and asked to be remembered when his kingdom came (Luke 23:39 to 43). And a Roman centurion, a man paid to execute rebels without sentiment, looked at the same body.

> *"And when the centurion, who stood facing him, saw that in this way he breathed his last, he said, 'Truly this man was the Son of God!'"*
> — Mark 15:39 (ESV)

The evidence was the same for everyone standing there. The perceptions weren't. I think that is the most important fact an honest apologist has to reckon with, and it leads to an uncomfortable conclusion. We tend to say that we understood the cross and then believed. The Gospels tell it the other way. The ones who believed had been given sight before belief had a chance to form. Even Jesus' own prayer from the cross, asking the Father to forgive those who did not know what they were doing (Luke 23:34), is a recognition that they were looking at him and could not see.

## Why Evidence Alone Has Never Been Enough

A lot of apologetics, including some I have admired, proceeds as if unbelief were a shortage of information. One more argument, one more archaeological find, one more neat proof, and the sceptic will tip over into faith. Scripture is more sobering. Jesus raised Lazarus in front of a crowd, and the chief priests who heard about it began to plot his death, and even Lazarus's (John 11:45 to 53; 12:10 to 11). In one of his parables, a rich man in torment begs that his brothers be warned by someone coming back from the dead, and he is told that if they do not listen to Moses and the Prophets they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead (Luke 16:27 to 31). The story is a forecast, and the Gospels record its fulfilment.

Paul gives the diagnosis in plain terms, saying that the god of this age has blinded the minds of unbelievers so that they cannot see the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ (2 Corinthians 4:4). A few verses on he describes the remedy, which is the same God who said let light shine out of darkness shining in our hearts to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Christ (2 Corinthians 4:6). The remedy is creation language. Sight begins with God saying a word in the dark. Elsewhere Paul prays that the eyes of the Ephesians' hearts would be enlightened (Ephesians 1:18), and he tells the Corinthians that the natural person does not accept the things of the Spirit, which are folly to him, because they are spiritually discerned (1 Corinthians 2:14).

The Reformed tradition built a doctrine on these texts. John Calvin taught that the certainty that Scripture comes from God does not rest on human reasoning alone. The Spirit illumines the mind so that we see God's majesty in Scripture, and that inward testimony adds no new revelation. It enables us to see what Scripture already displays. As Calvin put it, "The testimony of the Spirit is more excellent than all reason" (Institutes 1.7.4, as cited by Piper, n.d.). He did not mean that Christianity is against reason. He held it to be above reason, in the way that a lamp is above the furniture it lights.

Catholics and Orthodox Christians say something similar in their own terms. The grace that comes before faith, which Catholics call prevenient or enabling, and the Orthodox teaching that knowing God is a matter of the whole person being healed, both treat the capacity to believe as a gift. Arminians and Wesleyans, who differ from Calvin on how that grace is applied, still say it comes first and that no one can believe by native effort. The disagreements are real and I don't want to hide them. On this one point, that sight is a gift, the great traditions are closer than their arguments with each other sound.

C. S. Lewis, who spent his life defending the reasonableness of Christianity, knew that reasonableness is not the same as sight. Of his own faith he wrote that he believed in Christianity "as I believe that the Sun has risen", and gave his reason in a second clause, that it was "not only because I see it, but because by it I see everything else" (Lewis, 1949). Christianity, in his picture, isn't one more object lit by a torch. It is the light the other objects are seen by. Augustine had reached toward the same idea long before. Describing the turning point when he looked beyond the material and the mind itself, he said that he "with the eye of my soul (such as it was) saw above the same eye of my soul, above my mind, the Unchangeable Light" (Augustine, n.d., Book 7, chapter 10, Pilkington translation). Augustine was an African, writing from Hippo in what is now Algeria, and the whole of his search was a story of a man whose clever eyes could not see until another light shone on them. Neither Lewis nor Augustine denied the value of evidence. Both insisted that evidence needs an eye that can receive it, and that the eye is not made by effort. It is opened by grace.

## The Sceptic's Fair Complaint

Now the objection deserves a hearing, because a sceptic would be right to be annoyed by everything above.

Put in its strongest form it goes like this. If I disagree with you, you say my eyes are closed. If I produce an argument, you say arguments cannot open eyes. Whatever I say, you have a story that explains it away, and the story can never be tested. The philosopher Antony Flew made the charge famous in his 1950 article "Theology and Falsification", with a parable of two explorers who find a clearing in a jungle and disagree about whether a gardener tends it. They keep watch, they electrify a fence, they bring bloodhounds, and nothing detects the gardener. The believer keeps qualifying his claim until the gardener is invisible, intangible and silent, and the sceptic asks what is left of the original claim, and how his gardener differs from an imaginary one or from no gardener at all. Flew's challenge was to ask what would have to occur to count as a disproof of the claim (Flew, 1950 to 1951, as reprinted in Feinberg, 1968).

I'd want to say that the complaint is fair against some believers. A faith that explains every outcome in advance, so that a healing proves God's love and an unanswered prayer proves it too, has stopped saying anything. Anyone who has watched a certain kind of television preacher knows how it sounds, and the sceptic is right to refuse it.

But three things can be said about the claim in this essay. The first is that it is not true that nothing could count against the Christian claim. Paul stakes everything on a public event, saying that if Christ has not been raised, the faith is futile (1 Corinthians 15:14 to 17), and he cites five hundred witnesses, most of whom were still alive, as people who could be asked (1 Corinthians 15:6). Luke says he set out to write an orderly account after careful investigation, so that Theophilus could know the certainty of what he had been taught (Luke 1:1 to 4). Christianity has a falsification clause, written by the first apostle, and it is a historical one. Nothing in the doctrine of spiritual sight cancels that. A man who is given sight still has to look at the evidence.

The second is that Scripture does not treat blindness as an excuse. Paul says that what can be known about God is plain to people, and that they are without excuse (Romans 1:19 to 20), and the Bible's whole concern with unbelief is that it is something a person is responsible for, not a medical condition. So the doctrine does not say that sceptics are victims. It says that the human heart is entangled in the problem and that grace is needed to untangle it. That is a claim about people, the sceptic's own included, and it can be tested against what we know about how motives colour judgement. I would add, humbly, that it applies to believers as well. We also look at what suits us.

The third is that the Bible invites inspection. Nobody in the Gospels is told to close their eyes. When Nathanael scoffs that nothing good can come out of Nazareth, Philip does not argue. He says, come and see (John 1:46). The psalmist says to taste and see that the LORD is good (Psalm 34:8). Jesus tells Thomas to put his finger in the wounds, and then says that those who have not seen and yet believe are blessed (John 20:27 to 29). The gospel starts with a statement about what the apostles saw and heard and touched (1 John 1:1 to 3), and then asks the hearer to come and look.

So a believer's honest answer to the sceptic goes something like this. I am not asking you to stop looking. I am asking you to keep looking, and I am telling you what I believe about the looking, which is that more than the retina is involved. You are free to say you do not believe me. But a scientist who believed that everything about perception could be read off the lens would have misunderstood perception.

## What God Sees Before It Exists

There is one more layer, and it belongs to how God himself speaks. Paul says of the God of Abraham that he gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist (Romans 4:17). Nobody should hear in that a licence to manifest wealth by repeating a sentence. The positive confession teachers have borrowed the phrase for that purpose, and they have the logic backwards. It is God who calls into being, and he does it by a word that is his and not ours. The verse describes a God whose sight already reaches the end of the story. He called Abraham the father of many nations while Abraham was still childless. He greeted Gideon, hiding in a winepress to thresh wheat for fear of raiders, as a mighty man of valour (Judges 6:11 to 12). Gideon, looking at his own situation, saw a coward in a hole. God, looking at the same man, saw the one he would send.

God's declarations are not wishful thinking in religious dress. They are the ordinary speech of someone who can already see how it ends. And that gives an odd comfort to anyone who has looked at himself and concluded that he is merely what the file says. The Lord looks on the heart, and he sees there something he intends to make.

## An Invitation to Ask for Sight

Elisha's servant gives us the pattern for what to do with all of this. He woke one morning to find an enemy army with horses and chariots surrounding the city, and he saw only doom.

> *"Then Elisha prayed and said, 'O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see.' So the LORD opened the eyes of the young man, and he saw, and behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha."*
> — 2 Kings 6:17 (ESV)

The horses and chariots had been there all along. What changed was what the servant could perceive. Elisha, notice, did not ask for the army to arrive. He asked for the servant to see the one that was already there, and the difference between those two requests is most of the Christian life.

This changes what apologetics is for. When your cousin in Ibadan shrugs at the best argument you have, or a colleague at work listens politely and goes home unmoved, your work isn't finished at the argument. It continues as intercession. You stand beside them as Elisha stood beside the frightened young man, and ask God to do what you cannot. The Pharisees in John 9 teach the manner of it. Don't tell anyone they are blind and you can see. Say, with the man healed at the pool, that you were blind and now you see (John 9:25), and let that be as much of an argument as you make.

So here is the question God has been asking since a young man stood before an almond branch he didn't yet understand. He has never asked what you are looking at. He asks what you see.

When you look at your weakness, do you see only weakness, or the strength Paul says is made perfect there (2 Corinthians 12:9)? When you look at the diagnosis, the delayed visa, the failed interview and the empty account, do you see only absence? Faith doesn't ask you to lie about the X ray. It asks you not to hand the X ray the final word. The world looks at a grave and calls it the full stop. The church looks at the same grave and reads a comma, because on the third day that grave became the loudest witness in history to the difference between looking and seeing.

Go back for a moment to the woman in the corridor. I can't tell you what she saw in that film, and I can't tell you what she decided. I can tell you what the Bible would ask her, gently, as the Lord asked Jeremiah. What has the doctor said, which she must take seriously, and also who is watching over her while she holds it. Faith won't change the shadow on the plastic. It may change who is standing beside her in the corridor, and Hagar's name for God says that someone already is.

I'd like to suggest something small. Take the psalmist's prayer and make it yours for a week, in the words you would actually use. Open my eyes. Not the ones in my face, the ones behind them. Pray it over the one situation you have already decided you understand. And then pray it for one particular person who has heard every argument and still can't see. You may find that the answer comes more slowly than you would like. The servant at Dothan didn't see the chariots until somebody asked.
