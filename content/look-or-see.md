A woman stands in a hospital corridor in Lagos holding an X ray up to the fluorescent light. She has been standing there for eleven minutes. The radiographer left ten minutes ago. Her sister has called twice. She has not moved. She is not reading the film. She has read it already; the shadow on the lung is not ambiguous. She is doing something else entirely, something the film cannot capture and the light cannot illuminate. She is deciding what she believes is actually true about her own life. Everyone in that hospital that morning looked at the same grey rectangle of plastic. Only she had to decide what she saw in it.

This is the oldest argument in Scripture, and it is not really about eyesight.

From the garden where a woman looked at a piece of fruit and did not see the death hidden inside it, to a hill outside Jerusalem where soldiers looked at a dying man and did not see the Judge of the earth, the Bible keeps circling back to a single, uncomfortable claim: two people can stand in the exact same spot, receive the exact same information through the exact same organs, and walk away having encountered two entirely different realities. The difference was never in what was placed before them. The difference was in what they were equipped, or willing, to perceive.

Looking belongs to biology. Seeing belongs to revelation. And the entire architecture of the Christian faith rests on the conviction that God is far more interested in the second than the first.

## Two Verbs, One Chasm

Looking is cheap. It costs nothing but a functioning retina. Looking notices the surface of things: the shape, the color, the immediate circumstance. A man can look at a bank statement, a diagnosis, a wedding ring, a burning bush, a crucifix, and register only data. Looking answers one question and one question only: what is physically in front of me?

Seeing costs everything, because seeing asks what the physical thing in front of you actually means, and meaning is never neutral. Meaning implicates the one who receives it. This is why the Bible so rarely asks its characters what they are looking at. Instead, again and again, a strange and deliberate question interrupts the narrative.

**"What do you see?"**

It is not a question about optics. It is an examination of the heart's capacity to receive truth that the eye alone cannot deliver.

Consider the calling of the young priest Jeremiah, still trembling under the weight of a commission he did not ask for.

> "The word of the LORD came to me, saying, 'Jeremiah, what do you see?' And I said, 'I see an almond branch.' Then the LORD said to me, 'You have seen well, for I am watching over my word to perform it.'"
> — Jeremiah 1:11 to 12 (ESV)

There was nothing prophetic about an almond branch to the untrained eye. Almond trees were common in Judah, the first to blossom each year, ordinary enough that a boy could pass a hundred of them on his way to draw water. But the Hebrew wordplay buried in that branch, the near identity between the word for almond and the word for watching, was invisible to sight and available only to revelation. God was not testing Jeremiah's vision. He was testing whether the young man could receive meaning that the branch itself could never generate on its own.

The pattern repeats through the whole prophetic library. Amos is shown a plumb line and a basket of summer fruit and told what each one signifies for a nation drifting into judgment. Zechariah is shown lampstands, flying scrolls, and chariots of fire, and an angel supplies the interpretation his own eyes could never have produced. Not one of these prophets was praised for sharper eyesight. Every one of them was praised, or corrected, for the quality of their perception once the object had already been placed in front of them.

Revelation, in other words, is never manufactured by the human eye. It is only ever received by a heart God chooses to open.

## The Long Catalogue of Missed Reality

Scripture keeps a long, almost embarrassing catalogue of people who looked directly at the truth and still managed to miss it entirely.

A prophet named Balaam sat astride a donkey that suddenly refused to move. He beat the animal three times, furious at what he assumed was simple stubbornness. He could not see what his own donkey could see: an angel of the LORD standing in the road with a sword drawn. Only when God opened the prophet's eyes did Balaam finally see what had been standing directly in front of him the entire time.

> "Then the LORD opened the eyes of Balaam, and he saw the angel of the LORD standing in the way, with his drawn sword in his hand."
> — Numbers 22:31 (ESV)

A donkey outperformed a professional prophet in spiritual discernment that day. Scripture records this without apology, because irony is one of its favorite tools for exposing the gap between religious credentials and actual sight.

Later, twelve men are sent to scout the land of Canaan. All twelve cross the same river. All twelve walk the same hills. All twelve stand beneath the same fortified walls and carry back the same enormous cluster of grapes. Ten come back and speak of grasshoppers and giants. Two, Joshua and Caleb, come back speaking of a land flowing with milk and honey, already theirs by promise. The facts were identical for all twelve men. The report was not, because ten men interpreted the evidence through fear, and two interpreted it through the character of God. Faith, as this story makes plain, is not the denial of hard facts. Faith is the refusal to let hard facts have the final word over a promise God has already spoken.

Centuries afterward, when poisonous serpents overran the Israelite camp, God instructed Moses to lift a bronze serpent on a pole. Anyone who simply looked at it lived. Jesus later claimed this strange episode had been pointing at him the entire time.

> "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life."
> — John 3:14 to 15 (ESV)

Israel in the wilderness looked at bronze and lived. The church now looks at a cross and sees something the bronze could only foreshadow: not a symbol of poison neutralized, but the Son of God lifted up to absorb what poison could never have contained.

And then there is the strangest witness of all, standing at the edge of the Jordan River while a crowd gathered to watch an ordinary Galilean wade into the water. Everyone else in that crowd looked at a carpenter's son. One man saw something no one else in the region had yet perceived.

> "The next day he saw Jesus coming toward him, and said, 'Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!'"
> — John 1:29 (ESV)

Others saw Mary's son. John saw God's Lamb. Nothing about the physical scene had changed. Only the perception had.

## The Judge Who Sees Beneath the Surface

This principle does not originate with fallible human beings straining to interpret ambiguous signs. It originates with God himself, whose entire character is defined by seeing past appearance into substance.

When the prophet Samuel arrived to anoint a new king for Israel, he took one look at Eliab, the eldest and most physically impressive of Jesse's sons, and assumed the search was over. God corrected him immediately, and the correction has become one of the most quoted verses in the entire Hebrew Bible.

> "But the LORD said to Samuel, 'Do not look on his appearance or on the height of his stature, because I have rejected him. For the LORD sees not as man sees: man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart.'"
> — 1 Samuel 16:7 (ESV)

That single verse could serve as the thesis statement for the entire Bible. Man looks. God sees. Everything else is commentary.

The same contrast plays out in the opening scene of the book of Job, where Satan appears before God with a report built entirely on observation. Job was prosperous. Job was protected. Job had walls of blessing around his household that Satan himself could not breach. That was the extent of what Satan's eyes could register: possessions, hedges, external circumstance. God's own testimony about Job was built on something Satan's eyes had never been able to access at all: a blameless and upright man who feared God and turned away from evil. Satan saw wealth. God saw character. The difference between them was not information. It was the organ of perception.

Nigerian believers do not need an imported illustration for this principle. Anyone raised inside a Redeemed Christian Church of God vigil or a Living Faith crusade has heard some version of this warning from the pulpit a hundred times: that a testimony of prosperity is not automatically a testimony of God's favor, and that appearances of blessing can sit comfortably beside a heart that has quietly wandered. The church that has learned to distrust a well-decorated exterior is closer, in this narrow sense, to Samuel's God than it may realize.

## The World Is Like a Mask Dancing

There is a moment in Chinua Achebe's novel *Arrow of God* where the chief priest Ezeulu, watching the arrival of British colonial power in his region, sends his own son to learn the white man's religion and the white man's knowledge. His reasoning is not surrender. It is strategy, and he explains it with an image every Nigerian reader instantly recognizes.

> "The world is like a Mask dancing. If you want to see it well, you do not stand in one place."
> — Achebe, *Arrow of God* (1964)

Ezeulu understood something the ten frightened spies at Canaan never grasped: a single vantage point produces a partial picture, and a partial picture mistaken for the whole picture becomes a kind of blindness dressed up as certainty. This is not a foreign concept smuggled into African thought from a Western classroom. It is African wisdom in its own right, arrived at independently, and it maps with startling precision onto the biblical claim that looking from one fixed angle is never the same as truly seeing.

The Igbo world Achebe describes, and the wider precolonial worldview that stretched across Yorubaland, Hausaland, and beyond it into most of the continent, never treated the unseen as a fringe category the way much of post-Enlightenment Western Christianity eventually did. The ancestors were near. The spirit world pressed up against the visible one. A harvest was never merely agricultural; it was also a verdict on one's standing with the unseen powers that governed the community.

> "The land of the living was not far removed from the domain of the ancestors."
> — Achebe, *Things Fall Apart* (1958)

A Western missionary arriving in the nineteenth century, trained in a cessationist theology that had already quietly evicted the supernatural from ordinary Christian life, frequently mistook this African comfort with the unseen for mere superstition, a problem to be corrected rather than a worldview that, in this one respect, actually sat closer to the pages of Scripture than his own did. The book of Job has a heavenly council. The book of Daniel has princes contending in unseen realms over the fate of nations. The book of Ephesians describes a war fought not against flesh and blood but against rulers and authorities in the heavenly places. The African instinct that the visible world is porous, that harvests and sicknesses and dreams can carry spiritual weight, is not embarrassing next to that Scripture. It is, if anything, closer kin to it than the tidy rationalism that came ashore alongside the missionary's Bible.

This is where the story deserves its long memory rather than a short one.

In 1821, a Yoruba boy named Ajayi was captured in a raid on his village and sold into the transatlantic slave trade. A British naval patrol intercepted the Portuguese vessel carrying him and released its human cargo at Freetown, Sierra Leone, where the boy was educated, baptized under the name Samuel, and eventually became the first African to be consecrated a bishop in the Anglican Church, overseeing the mission along the Niger River that he himself had helped establish (Slavery and Remembrance, n.d.). Samuel Ajayi Crowther translated the Bible into Yoruba with his own hand. He built an indigenous African mission staffed by Africans, only to watch European colleagues within his own denomination gradually strip that mission of its African leadership and hand it back to European control before his death.

Here is the turn worth sitting with. The same colonial gaze that could not see the depth in Ezeulu's cosmology also, eventually, could not bring itself to see a Nigerian bishop as a full equal in Christ, despite his own bishop's collar and his own translated Scriptures. The problem was never a shortage of evidence. Crowther's competence was not in question; his color was. Looking, once again, proved cheaper than seeing, and prejudice functioned exactly the way spiritual blindness always functions in the biblical record: it allowed observers to stand directly in front of overwhelming evidence and still walk away unconvinced.

But here the turn must go one step further, because a Nigerian apologist owes honesty to both sides of this history, not merely to the side that suffered. The same communal comfort with the unseen that gives African Christianity its vitality has also, in the wrong hands, been twisted into something the ancestors themselves would scarcely recognize. Prayer, once a costly wrestling before a holy God, gets repackaged for a fee. A handkerchief soaked in oil is sold as though anointing were a subscription service. A pastor's private jet becomes, in certain circles, indistinguishable from a prophetic sign. The prosperity gospel is not entirely without an audience willing to mistake a platform shoe for a mountain.

None of that is African wisdom. It is a counterfeit wearing African clothing, and the church that produced men like Crowther, and produces vigils where thousands still cry out honestly for God in the middle of the night, deserves better than a theology that has confused looking wealthy with seeing God.

## The Disciple Who Looked at a Cross and Missed the Kingdom

Nowhere does this distinction cut closer to the bone than in the life of Simon Peter, the disciple who loved Jesus with more visible enthusiasm than any of the others and still, on one catastrophic afternoon, got the difference between looking and seeing exactly backwards.

Jesus had just told his disciples plainly that he was going to Jerusalem to suffer and die. Peter pulled him aside and rebuked him for it.

> "And Peter took him aside and began to rebuke him, saying, 'Far be it from you, Lord! This shall never happen to you.'"
> — Matthew 16:22 (ESV)

Peter looked at a cross and saw only defeat. Jesus, standing in the same conversation, saw redemption for the entire human race folded inside that same instrument of execution. The response Jesus gave was severe precisely because the stakes of misperception were so high.

> "But he turned and said to Peter, 'Get behind me, Satan! You are a hindrance to me. For you are not setting your mind on the things of God, but on the things of man.'"
> — Matthew 16:23 (ESV)

Peter's failure was not a failure of devotion. It was a failure of sight, the same failure that would later leave two disciples walking the road to Emmaus in complete despair, describing recent events to a stranger who happened to be the risen Christ himself, without recognizing him at all, until their eyes were finally opened at the breaking of bread. It was the same failure that left Mary Magdalene standing outside an empty tomb, weeping, mistaking the resurrected Lord for a gardener.

Ask yourself honestly: how many of your own closed doors have you mistaken for endings, when God intended them as the exact hinge on which your deliverance was about to turn?

## The Cross Was Public. The Perception Was Not.

We must return, as promised earlier, to that hill outside Jerusalem, because it is the sharpest test case Scripture offers for this entire argument.

Thousands of people looked directly at the crucifixion of Jesus Christ. Roman soldiers looked. Religious leaders looked. Passersby looked. His own mother looked. And yet the conclusions drawn from that single, publicly available scene splintered in every direction imaginable. Some saw a criminal receiving overdue justice. Some saw a failed revolutionary. One of the two thieves crucified alongside him saw, somehow, in the middle of his own agony, a King entering his kingdom, and asked to be remembered when that kingdom finally arrived. A Roman centurion, a man paid to execute Jewish rebels without sentiment, looked at the same dying man and made the confession that still echoes through every Gentile church on earth.

> "Now when the centurion, who stood facing him, saw that in this way he breathed his last, he said, 'Truly this man was the Son of God!'"
> — Mark 15:39 (ESV)

We did not first understand the cross and then believe it. We believed, and only then did the cross become comprehensible to us. And even that formula turns itself inside out under close inspection, because the ones who truly believed were the ones who had already been given sight before belief ever had a chance to form. Sight was never the reward for faith. Sight was the gift that made faith possible in the first place.

The evidence at Calvary was identical for every observer standing at that hill. The perceptions were not. This is the single most important fact any honest apologetic must reckon with, and it leads directly to the deepest question in Christian evangelism.

## Why Evidence Alone Has Never Been Enough

A great deal of modern apologetics proceeds as though unbelief were primarily an intellectual shortage, as though one more argument, one more archaeological discovery, one more philosophical proof would finally tip a skeptical mind into faith. Scripture offers a more sobering diagnosis. Jesus raised Lazarus from the dead in full public view, and the same religious leaders who witnessed it walked away plotting his execution rather than falling at his feet.

Paul names the actual obstacle with startling directness.

> "In their case the god of this world has blinded the minds of the unbelievers, to keep them from seeing the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God."
> — 2 Corinthians 4:4 (ESV)

The problem was never insufficient evidence. The problem was a blindness that no quantity of evidence, by itself, has ever been sufficient to cure. This is why C.S. Lewis, defending the reasonableness of the Christian faith without pretending reason alone could deliver conviction, wrote that he believed in Christianity the way he believed the sun had risen.

> "I believe in Christianity as I believe that the sun has risen: not only because I see it, but because by it I see everything else."
> — Lewis, "Is Theology Poetry?" in *The Weight of Glory* (2001)

Christianity, in Lewis's image, is not one more object examined under an external light. It is the light itself, the lens through which every other object in the universe finally comes into focus. Augustine reached toward the same idea centuries earlier, confessing that the deepest beauty he had spent his early adulthood chasing through pleasure and ambition could never be perceived by the physical senses at all, because true beauty is seen by the inner eye of the soul rather than the eye of the flesh (Augustine, trans. 2004). Neither man was denying the value of evidence. Both were insisting that evidence needs an organ capable of receiving it, and that organ is not manufactured by human effort. It is opened by grace.

This is why Christian apologetics, rightly understood, is never merely defensive argument. It is intercession. It is standing beside a friend, a colleague, a skeptical relative, and asking God to do for them exactly what he did for Elisha's terrified servant, who looked out at an army surrounding the city and saw only doom, until the prophet prayed a single request on his behalf.

> "Then Elisha prayed and said, 'O LORD, please open his eyes that he may see.' So the LORD opened the eyes of the young man, and he saw, and behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha."
> — 2 Kings 6:17 (ESV)

The chariots of fire had always been there. The only thing that changed was the servant's capacity to perceive what had surrounded him the entire time.

## What God Calls Before Anyone Else Can See It

There is a final layer to this argument, and it belongs entirely to God's own way of speaking. Paul describes the God of Abraham as one who calls into being things that do not yet exist as though they already did.

> "In the presence of the God in whom he believed, who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist."
> — Romans 4:17 (ESV)

This is not the positive confession movement's borrowed language of manifesting wealth through repetition. It is something far weightier: the declaration of a God who sees the finished product before the raw material has even been assembled, who called a childless old man Abraham, father of many nations, decades before Isaac drew his first breath, who called a frightened shepherd a mighty man of valor before that shepherd believed it about himself, who called violent persecutors of the church apostles and called dead sinners saints. God's declarations are never wishful thinking dressed up in religious language. They are the natural speech of a Being whose sight already extends to the end of the story.

## What Do You See?

So we return, finally, to the only question that has ever mattered in this entire discussion, the question God has been asking his people since a young priest stood before an almond branch he did not yet understand.

Not, what are you looking at.

**What do you see?**

Do you look at your own weakness, or do you see the strength that is made perfect precisely inside that weakness? Do you look at a diagnosis in a hospital corridor, or do you see a God who has already numbered every day written in your book before one of them came to be? Do you look at closed doors, delayed visas, failed interviews, empty accounts, and see only absence, or do you see the deliberate hand of a God who has never once been late, however many times he has been accused of it?

Faith does not require you to lie about the X ray. Faith requires you to refuse to let the X ray have the final and only word.

The world looks at problems. Faith sees promises hiding inside them. The world looks at a grave and calls it the end of the sentence. The church looks at the very same grave and reads it as a comma, because on the third day the grave itself became the loudest witness in history to the fact that looking and seeing were never the same thing at all.

The centurion looked at a dying man and saw the Son of God. The thief looked at a criminal's death sentence and saw a King entering his kingdom. Balaam's donkey looked at an empty road and saw an angel with a drawn sword before the prophet riding it saw anything at all.

What door is standing open in front of you right now that your eyes have already dismissed as nothing?

The invitation of the gospel has never been an invitation to close your eyes and hope harder. It is an invitation to ask, honestly, humbly, the way the psalmist asked, the way Elisha's servant needed someone else to ask on his behalf, for the one thing your own effort could never manufacture. Open my eyes, Lord. Not the eyes in my skull. The eyes behind them. Let me stop merely looking at the life you have set in front of me, and finally, for the first time, see it.
