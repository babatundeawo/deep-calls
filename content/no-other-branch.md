## A Perfectly Reasonable Mistake

He was in a hurry, which is the condition under which most of our worst decisions are made.

He needed money. He had directions. There was a bank right where they said it would be — or so it appeared — and so he walked in, handed over his papers, and waited for the transaction to be completed. The teller was smiling. The logo looked familiar. The building was on the right street.

And then: *"I'm sorry, sir. This is not our bank. The one you want is across the street."*

He had been standing in the wrong institution the entire time. The difference between the bank that could help him and the bank that could not was nothing more than which side of the street he was standing on.

He corrected his mistake. He walked across the street, finished his transaction, and went home.

That is where the grace of the analogy ends.

Because there are mistakes you can walk back from, and there are mistakes that follow you into eternity. Getting the bank wrong costs you fifteen minutes and a measure of embarrassment. Getting the God wrong costs you everything. There is no correcting that transaction once the window has closed.

## The Most Comforting Lie in the World

Let us be honest about the appeal of religious pluralism.

The idea that all roads lead to the same destination — that all gods are different names for the same ineffable reality, that sincerity of devotion matters more than the object of devotion, that every spiritual address is equally valid — is not a stupid idea. It is, in fact, a deeply attractive one. It is generous. It is tolerant. It allows a person to be spiritual without the discomfort of claiming that anyone else might be wrong. In a world weary of religious violence, sectarian slaughter, and the long ugly history of people killing each other over doctrine, this position sounds like wisdom.

It is not wisdom. It is comfort. And there is a difference.

Comfort tells you what you want to hear. Wisdom tells you what you need to hear. Comfort lets you sleep. Wisdom sometimes keeps you awake because it has handed you a truth too important to set aside until morning.

The comfortable version of religion says: Go to any bank. They are all banks. They all handle money. The specific address is incidental.

But banks are not interchangeable institutions. They hold different accounts. They operate under different rules. A cheque drawn on one account cannot be cashed at a different institution. And when you are standing at the wrong counter, it does not matter how sincerely you believe you are in the right place. It does not matter how loudly you insist. It does not matter how long you have been a loyal customer of *some* bank *somewhere* — the teller cannot process what the institution does not hold.

The question has never been whether a person is religious. The question has always been whether the God they are religious toward is actually there.

## The Verse Everyone Quotes Up to the Comma

There is a sentence in the Gospel of John that may be the most selectively read clause in the history of religious literature. People quote the first half and quietly set down the second as though it were a stone too heavy to carry in polite company.

Here it is, in full:

> *"I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me."*
> — John 14:6 (NIV)

Jesus did not say he was *a* way. He did not say he was a highly recommended route among several viable options. He did not say he was the preferred path for people raised in a certain cultural context, while other paths served others equally well. He said he was *the* way. Not a branch. The institution itself.

This is precisely what makes the statement impossible to domesticate. C.S. Lewis understood this. Writing in *Mere Christianity*, he pointed out that a man who said the kinds of things Jesus said would not leave us the option of calling him simply a great moral teacher. As Lewis put it, a man who says what Jesus said is "either a lunatic on a level with the man who says he is a poached egg or else he is the Devil of Hell" or he is exactly who he claims to be (Lewis, 1952, p. 52). The exclusive claim is not incidental to the person of Christ. It is inseparable from it. Remove the second half of John 14:6 and you do not have a gentler Christianity. You have a different religion entirely.

The Apostle Peter, standing before the very council that had conspired to execute his Lord, said it again in case anyone had missed it:

> *"Salvation is found in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given to mankind by which we must be saved."*
> — Acts 4:12 (NIV)

Notice that Peter did not hedge. He had knelt in the garden while Jesus prayed. He had stood in the courtyard and denied him three times. He had run to the empty tomb and found it precisely that. He had received the Holy Spirit at Pentecost and watched a room of terrified disciples become something else entirely. Peter was not speaking from theory. He was speaking from encounter. And the man who speaks from encounter does not hedge.

The question, then, is not whether it is socially comfortable to say that Jesus is the only way to the Father. The question is whether it is true. And if it is true, then saying anything else is not kindness. It is malpractice.

## When the Shrine Is Not Metaphorical

There is a category of reader for whom everything above is abstract — a doctrinal skirmish between Christians and secular pluralists, conducted in universities and comment sections by people who have never felt anything spiritual in their lives and are debating its logistics from a safe distance.

And then there is the reader who knows exactly what a babalawo is. Who has heard the family story told in fragments, in lowered voices, usually at a funeral of a relative who visited one before some major decision, and what came of it. Who grew up in a home where certain objects occupied certain corners for reasons nobody fully explained. Who has been in a prayer meeting where a deliverance minister was enumerating the sources of generational affliction with an authority that came not from theology alone but from having seen enough to know that something is genuinely there.

For that reader, this is not abstract. This is Tuesday.

Western Christianity has a habit of misdiagnosing the African spiritual situation. It assumes the problem is superstition — a credulous people projecting spiritual agency onto what is merely ignorance and coincidence. This misunderstands the terrain entirely. The issue in much of African traditional spirituality is not that nothing is there. The issue is *who* is there. The Apostle Paul wrote to a church in Corinth surrounded by temples, shrines, and sacrificial altars, and he did not dismiss those shrines as empty theatrics. He wrote:

> *"The sacrifices of pagans are offered to demons, not to God, and I do not want you to be participants with demons."*
> — 1 Corinthians 10:20 (NIV)

There is spiritual reality behind the shrine. The error is not in recognising that reality. The error is in mistaking its identity.

Many Nigerians carry, in their bones, an understanding that the supernatural is not a fringe category — that the world is more populated and more contested than the secular imagination permits. This is not primitive thinking. It is, in fact, a more biblically consistent posture than the polite rationalism that has stripped large portions of Western Christianity of any live expectation that God actually shows up. The problem is not too much awareness of the spiritual world. The problem is running to the wrong address within it.

And the testimony of Scripture, consistent from Genesis to Revelation, is that power is not proof of rightness. Pharaoh's magicians replicated the first two plagues of Egypt — turned water to blood, brought up frogs — before they met the limits of their authority (Exodus 8:18). They could perform signs. They could not undo what God was doing. Power at the wrong address does not become salvation. It becomes evidence.

## The Prophet Who Was Not Afraid to Laugh

There is a scene in 1 Kings 18 that the church needs to read more regularly, and not only for the theology.

Elijah has summoned the prophets of Baal — 450 of them — to Mount Carmel for what is, without question, the most audacious theological duel in the Hebrew scriptures. The terms are simple: both sides will prepare a sacrifice. Neither will light it. Whichever God answers with fire is the real one.

The prophets of Baal go first. They pray. They dance. They shout. They call on the name of Baal from morning until noon. Nothing happens.

And then Elijah begins to taunt them:

> *"Shout louder! Surely he is a god! Perhaps he is deep in thought, or busy, or traveling. Maybe he is sleeping and must be awakened."*
> — 1 Kings 18:27 (NIV)

This is prophetic sarcasm at full throttle. This is not meanness. This is accuracy delivered with wit. A god who cannot hear is not a god who can save. A deity who requires volume adjustments, persistent reminders, and the right emotional atmosphere has already failed the most fundamental requirement of divinity. Elijah was not mocking sincere worshippers. He was exposing an empty institution.

The prophets of Baal cut themselves. They bled. They kept shouting. The text records, with devastating understatement: *"But there was no response, no one answered, no one paid attention"* (1 Kings 18:29, NIV).

Then Elijah repaired the altar of the Lord, arranged the sacrifice, and drenched the whole thing in water not once, not twice, but three times, until the trench dug around the altar was full. He then prayed a single, measured prayer. No theatrics. No volume. No cutting. Just a man at the right address, speaking to the right God.

> *"Then the fire of the Lord fell and burned up the sacrifice, the wood, the stones and the soil, and also licked up the water in the trench."*
> — 1 Kings 18:38 (NIV)

Not because Elijah was louder. Not because he had been more devoted. Not because he had performed more impressively in the build-up. But because he was at the right address. And the God at that address was actually there.

The God of Baal was not at any address. He was nowhere. Because he was nothing — or rather, because what occupied his shrine was something that was not nothing, but was also very definitely not God.

## What Recovery Looks Like When There Is None

The brother in the bank story walked across the street. His transaction was delayed but not derailed. He got his money. He went on with his life. The story has a happy ending because the story is about banking.

The story the bank is illustrating is not about banking.

Eternity does not offer a second window. There is no across-the-street option once the window of this life has closed. This is not said to provoke fear — or not only that. It is said because it is true, and because the people we love are standing in queues at the wrong counter right now, and the most unkind thing in the world would be to let them stand there because we were too polite to say: *You are in the wrong place.*

The Gospel is not a superior version of what every religion is attempting. It is categorically different. Not because Christians are morally better people — one glance at church history will cure you of that idea faster than you might like. But because the Gospel is the only religious claim in the world that is not primarily about human effort toward the divine. Every other system is a map with directions. The Gospel is a rescue. It is about a God who, rather than waiting at a counter for people to navigate to the right branch, crossed the street himself. Became flesh. Entered the mess. Died on a Roman cross. Rose on the third day, in history, in a body, in front of witnesses.

N.T. Wright, who has spent more scholarly energy on the historical evidence for the resurrection than almost any living New Testament scholar, argues that the empty tomb and the post-resurrection appearances together constitute historical data that the standard alternatives — theft of the body, collective hallucination, legendary development — cannot adequately explain (Wright, 2003, pp. 686-696). The resurrection is not a metaphor the early church invented to cope with grief. It is the ground on which every claim in the Gospel stands. It is the transaction receipt.

> *"For God so loved the world that he gave his one and only Son, that whoever believes in him shall not perish but have eternal life."*
> — John 3:16 (NIV)

Not whoever strives. Not whoever is sufficiently devout. Not whoever was raised in the right tradition or performs the right rituals with enough consistency. Whoever believes. The transaction requires only that you come to the right address and trust the institution to do what it has always promised.

Augustine of Hippo spent years running to every address the late Roman world had to offer — Manichaeism, Neoplatonism, pleasure, ambition, rhetoric, fame — before he arrived, exhausted and disarmed, at the God he had been circling the entire time. He wrote, in the opening pages of his *Confessions*: "You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Augustine, circa 397-400 CE/1991, Book I, Chapter 1).

The restlessness is not a malfunction. The restlessness is the evidence. Something in the human soul knows that the transaction has not been completed, that the queue it has been standing in is the wrong one, that there is another counter on the other side of the street where an account is open and the balance is sufficient and the teller is waiting. That restlessness is grace — the same grace that made a teller smile and say, *"I'm sorry, sir. The one you want is across the street."*

Do not waste it.

## The Door Is Still Open

Walk across the street.

Not because the Christian religion is culturally superior to what you were raised with. Not because the church is a perfect institution — it is visibly, sometimes catastrophically, not. Not because you have resolved every intellectual objection and tied off every philosophical loose end. Those things may come with time, and they are worth pursuing, but they are not the threshold.

Walk across because Jesus Christ said he is the way, the truth, and the life, and the evidence — the resurrection, above all else, but also the changed lives, the transformed communities, the testimony of two thousand years of encounter — points stubbornly in the same direction. Walk across because the transaction he offers is the only one in existence in which the cost has already been paid and the account is already in your name. Walk across because the window is open now, and you do not know how long that will last.

Not because you deserve it. None of us deserve it.

Not because you are ready. Nobody ever is.

Not because you have everything figured out. The disciples walked with him for three years and still managed to get remarkable quantities of it wrong.

But because the alternative — banking on the wrong god, standing in the wrong queue, insisting at the wrong counter that this must be the right place — is a mistake from which there is no recovery.

Come to the right bank.

The Father is waiting.
