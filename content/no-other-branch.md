## Two banks on one street

Here is a scene I am making up, though you may know someone it has happened to.

A man is in a hurry, which is the condition under which most bad decisions get made. He has a cheque to cash before the end of the working day. Two banks face each other across a busy road, and he walks into the nearer one. The signboard has the right colours. The cashier smiles. The queue feels familiar, and somewhere at the back a generator is humming the way generators do. He hands over his cheque and waits a long while. Then the cashier looks at it kindly and says, "Sir, this isn't our account. The bank you want is across the road."

He crosses. He is embarrassed and fifteen minutes poorer, and then he is paid, and he goes home.

That is where the comparison stops working. A wrong bank costs an afternoon. If Jesus was telling the truth about himself, a wrong god costs a great deal more, and the New Testament speaks as though this life is where the question gets answered (Hebrews 9:27). Nobody is promised a second window across the road.

I want to argue that when Jesus claimed to be the only way to the Father, he was saying the most serious thing anyone has ever said to our neighbours, and that it was never meant as an insult. But the objection deserves a fair hearing first, because it is held by decent people and it has a lot going for it. And the claim itself needs careful handling, because Christians who agree that Jesus is the only Saviour disagree, sometimes sharply, about what follows for people who never heard his name. I will try to set out those disagreements honestly rather than pretend there is only one Christian answer to every question.

## Why pluralism is so easy to love

The best version of religious pluralism goes something like this. Nobody has seen the whole of God. Every tradition holds a piece of the truth, shaped by the soil it grew in. To insist that your piece is the whole thing is arrogance, and arrogance has filled graveyards. Better to hold your own faith warmly, honour everyone else's, and let God sort out the rest.

The philosopher John Hick gave this view its most careful modern form. He argued that the great religions are different human responses to one ultimate reality that no human concept can fully hold. He wrote that God cannot be defined or encompassed by human thought, so that each religious idea of God is a limited image of a reality beyond it, and he used the old story of blind men touching an elephant to show how each could describe something true and none could describe the whole (Hettinger, n.d., summarising Hick). A pluralist would accept that as a fair statement of the view, and I don't think it is a stupid one. It is generous. It is tolerant. It lets a person be spiritual without the discomfort of saying that anyone else might be wrong.

In Nigeria it is hardly even an abstraction. By Pew's estimate, about 56 per cent of Nigerians were Muslim and about 43 per cent Christian in 2020 (Pew Research Center, 2025). Those aren't two camps on opposite sides of a river. They are cousins on the same family WhatsApp group, and they are neighbours who share a gate. When people have buried their own because of a religious riot, "all roads lead to the same place" sounds less like a theory and more like a prayer for peace. And pluralism has spread well beyond Nigeria, and well into the church. Plenty of people who sing about Jesus on Sunday say quietly on Monday that it all comes to the same thing.

So what is wrong with it? Two things, I think, and a third that I'll come to later. The first is that the elephant picture quietly assumes that the person telling the story can see the whole elephant. That is a very large claim to make from the sidelines, and it is far more exclusive than it looks, because it tells every religion that its own account of itself is mistaken. Hick himself did not treat all religions as equally valid, and he rejected the view that one faith alone provides salvation; he was an exclusivist about exclusivism. The second is that the religions don't say the same things. A faith that says God became a man and died for sinners and a faith that says God has no son cannot both be true descriptions of the same fact. Pluralism can only be kept by treating every religion's central claims as secondary, and that is a quiet insult to those who hold them. A devout Muslim doesn't wish to be told that tawhid is one of several ways of saying the Trinity.

Tolerance, meanwhile, has never required agreement. You can believe your neighbour is mistaken about God and still defend his right to worship, his shop, and his life. Jesus told Peter to put his sword away (John 18:11), and the church has never been more faithful to him than when it has remembered that.

## What Jesus said to Thomas

Jesus did not volunteer his most famous sentence to a crowd. He said it to a frightened friend. He had just told the disciples he was going away to prepare a place for them, and Thomas, who has a reputation for honesty, said they had no idea where he was going, so how could they know the way (John 14:5). This is the answer he got.

> *"I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me."*
>
> — John 14:6 (ESV)

Notice what he did not do. He didn't hand Thomas a map. He didn't say he knew a very good route. The way, in that sentence, is a person, and the person is the one speaking.

C. S. Lewis saw that this leaves very little room for the comfortable option. In *Mere Christianity* he argued that a mere man who said the things Jesus said could not be a great moral teacher. He would be either a lunatic or, in Lewis's phrase, "the Devil of Hell" (Lewis, 1952, Book II, chapter 3). Philosophers still argue about whether those are the only choices, and Lewis is not the last word. But he put his finger on something real. You can't keep the kindness of Jesus and quietly drop the part where he says who he is. Take that part out and what's left is a different religion.

Peter took the same line when it cost him something. He had denied knowing Jesus three times in a courtyard (Luke 22:54 to 62). Not long afterwards he stood before the same ruling council that had handed Jesus over, and said this.

> *"And there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved."*
>
> — Acts 4:12 (ESV)

That is not the voice of a man enjoying his own cleverness. It is a man on trial, saying the thing most likely to get him killed.

The letters say the same thing in their own words. Paul wrote to Timothy that there is "one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV), and in the same breath that God "desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4, ESV). Hold those two verses together, because the rest of this essay lives between them. God's desire reaches every person on earth. The mediator is one.

## Three positions and what each one claims

Theologians have sorted the possible answers into three families, and the labels are worth knowing so that you can tell which one you are being offered.

Pluralism, as above, holds that the religions are many paths to one reality and that none has a special claim. Exclusivism holds that salvation is found only through Jesus Christ, and usually adds that people must hear of him and respond in faith in this life. Inclusivism holds, with the exclusivist, that Christ is the only Saviour, and then adds that his saving work can reach people who have never heard of him, when they respond in faith to the light that they do have.

Each of the last two has serious people on its side. The Westminster Confession, the great statement of Reformed faith from the seventeenth century, says that elect infants who die and "all other elect persons, who are incapable of being outwardly called by the ministry of the word" are saved by Christ through the Spirit, who works "when, and where, and how he pleaseth." But it goes on to say that "much less can men not professing the Christian religion be saved in any other way whatsoever, be they ever so diligent to frame their lives according to the light of nature, and the law of that religion they do profess" (Westminster Confession of Faith, 10.3 and 10.4, as posted by the Bible Presbyterian Church, n.d.). That is exclusivism in its classic Protestant form, and notice that even there a door is left open for some who cannot hear.

The Second Vatican Council, speaking for the Catholic Church, took a wider road. Its constitution *Lumen Gentium* says that Muslims "profess to hold the faith of Abraham" and with Christians "adore the one, merciful God," and that "those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church" but "seek God with a sincere heart, and, moved by grace, try in their actions to do his will as they know it" may also "attain eternal salvation" (Second Vatican Council, 1964, paragraph 16, as posted by the Council on Christian and Jewish relations, n.d.). That is inclusivism in a Catholic form. Notice what it keeps. Salvation is still through Christ and by grace, and it does not say the religions as such are saving.

There are Protestant inclusivists too. Clark Pinnock argued for it in *A Wideness in God's Mercy*, and John Sanders surveyed the whole discussion in *No Other Name* (1992), while others, such as Millard Erickson in *How Shall They Be Saved?* (1996) and Ronald Nash, defended the more restrictive view (Sanders, n.d.). Some have proposed that those who never heard may get an opportunity after death, and a few have hoped, with Hans Urs von Balthasar, that all might finally be saved. I'm listing these so that you know the shape of the map. A Christian who tells you that only one view is possible has not read the literature.

The Lausanne Covenant of 1974, which was written by evangelicals from around the world including Africans, takes a careful line. It says there is "only one Saviour and only one gospel," and that general revelation in nature gives some knowledge of God but cannot save. It rejects "every kind of syncretism and dialogue which implies that Christ speaks equally through all religions and ideologies," and it says that proclaiming Jesus as the Saviour of the world "is not to affirm that all people are either automatically or ultimately saved" (Lausanne Movement, 1974, article 3). Notice how little it settles. It says who saves, and it says that the gospel must be preached, and it leaves open the question of exactly how God deals with each person.

My own view is that the claim of Jesus and the question about the unevangelized are different questions. The first is about who the Saviour is, and the answer is in the verses above. The second is about the extent of God's mercy toward individuals, and there the New Testament says less than we would like. I'll say more in a moment.

## What creation and conscience can tell us

Before we get to the people who never heard, there is something to say about what people do know. Paul says that every human being receives some light. "For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them" (Romans 1:19, ESV). The invisible things of God, he adds, "have been clearly perceived, ever since the creation of the world" (Romans 1:20, ESV). That is general revelation, the knowledge of God that comes through creation and conscience, and it is why a farmer who has never heard of Jesus still knows that somebody is behind the rain.

Paul uses it himself. In Lystra he told a crowd that God "did not leave himself without witness, for he did good by giving you rains from heaven and fruitful seasons, satisfying your hearts with food and gladness" (Acts 14:17, ESV). In Athens he began with an altar to an unknown god. "What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you" (Acts 17:23, ESV). He quoted the Greek poets, "In him we live and move and have our being," and "we are indeed his offspring" (Acts 17:28, ESV). He told the Athenians that God made the nations "that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him" (Acts 17:27, ESV). And then he turned the corner. The times of ignorance God overlooked, "but now he commands all people everywhere to repent," because he has fixed a day of judgement by a man whom he raised from the dead (Acts 17:30 to 31, ESV).

That is a pattern worth copying. Paul affirms what is true in the people's own religious life, because there is something there to affirm. And he doesn't stop at affirming it. The altar to an unknown god was a sign that people were reaching, and he told them whom they had been reaching for.

But general revelation does not rescue anyone. Paul says in the same letter that none is righteous, "no, not one" (Romans 3:10, ESV), and that "all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23, ESV). He writes that even Gentiles who do not have the law "by nature do what the law requires" and show that the work of the law "is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness" (Romans 2:14 to 15, ESV). People know enough to be responsible, and not enough to be saved, and that is exactly why Paul asks the question that every missionary has had to face: "How then will they call on him in whom they have not believed?" (Romans 10:14, ESV). He concludes that "faith comes from hearing, and hearing through the word of Christ" (Romans 10:17, ESV).

## The people who never heard

So what about them? This is the question that every honest African Christian has asked at a graveside, because our grandparents lived and died before the missionaries came, or in places the missionaries never reached. I won't answer it with more confidence than Scripture gives. But here is what the Bible does say.

First, God is just. Abraham asked, when Sodom was to be destroyed, whether the Judge of all the earth would do right (Genesis 18:25). The answer of the whole Bible is yes. Nobody will stand before God and discover that he has been unfair.

Second, people are judged by the light they have. Paul says in Romans 2 that God will judge "the secrets of men by Christ Jesus" (Romans 2:16, ESV), and Jesus taught that to whom much is given, much will be required (Luke 12:48). That is a warning to those of us who have heard, and a comfort about those who have not.

Third, the Bible never says that anybody is saved by a route other than Christ. If some who have never heard are saved, then on the New Testament's own terms they are saved by his death and not by their religion. This is the inclusivist's claim at its most modest, and it is entirely compatible with Acts 4:12. It would mean that the name is exclusive but that the knowing of the name isn't a condition of benefiting from it. The Old Testament saints who trusted God's promise before Jesus was born are the clearest example. They did not know his name, and the New Testament says that Abraham's faith was counted to him as righteousness.

Fourth, the same Bible insists that this is no reason to stay at home. The reason Paul asks who will preach is that preaching is the means God has chosen (Romans 10:14 to 17). If I think that some who never hear may be saved, I have not thereby earned the right to stop telling people. I have no way of knowing which of my neighbours are in that group, and Jesus told us to go to every nation. Hope for the unevangelized is no excuse for a church that has gone quiet, and the Lausanne Covenant rightly keeps the two together.

Fifth, God desires all people to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). The fact that Christians cannot agree about the edges should humble us. It should not make us cruel in either direction, either by consigning whole peoples to hell with a confidence Scripture does not give, or by announcing that nobody is in any danger. Both are claims to know more than we do.

If you press me for my own conviction, I hold that the only Saviour is Jesus Christ, that nobody is saved by any religion as such, that the gospel must be preached to every person, and that on the question of the unevangelized the right posture is trust in a just and merciful God and urgency about the task he has given us. Good Christians, whom I respect, hold both more restrictive and more generous views, and I would not break fellowship over it.

## When the shrine is not a metaphor

Some readers will feel all of this as a debate in a lecture hall. Many Nigerian readers won't.

Anyone who has sat through a family meeting after a death knows how the conversation goes. Somebody says in a low voice that an uncle consulted a diviner before a big decision, and the room goes quiet while everyone does the arithmetic. Certain objects sit in certain corners of certain houses for reasons nobody will fully explain. And most of us have heard a deliverance minister list the sources of a family's trouble with a confidence that didn't come from theology alone.

The numbers agree with the living rooms. In a report published in November 2025, Pew said about seven in ten adults in Nigeria believe that spells, curses or other magic can influence people's lives (Pew Research Center, 2025). The Kenyan scholar John Mbiti wrote in the introduction to *African Religions and Philosophy* in 1969 that "in traditional society there are no irreligious people" (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.). Nobody is going to talk a Nigerian reader out of the spiritual world by calling it superstition. Missionaries who tried that mostly discovered that their converts kept visiting grandmother's diviner at night, because the daytime sermon had no answer for the funeral.

And grandmother was not stupid. She went because she was afraid for her children and nobody else had offered her anything. That should make us gentle. It shouldn't make us silent.

Paul wrote to a church in a city full of temples, and he did not call the temples empty.

> *"No, I imply that what pagans sacrifice they offer to demons and not to God. I do not want you to be participants with demons."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 10:20 (ESV)

His point is that something is there. The mistake is in the identity of what is there. Power and truth are different things, and Scripture keeps them apart from Genesis to Revelation. Pharaoh's magicians could turn water to blood and bring up frogs, which is where Moses must have felt rather outnumbered (Exodus 7:22; 8:7). Then Aaron struck the dust and the gnats came, and the magicians tried and could not, and said to Pharaoh that this was the finger of God (Exodus 8:18 to 19). They could produce signs. They could not undo what God was doing. Power at the wrong address doesn't turn into salvation. It turns into evidence.

## What African scholars have made of it

Two views have run through African Christian thought about the traditional religions, and both come from people who loved the gospel.

Byang Kato was born in 1936 in Kwoi in what is now Kaduna State, to a family whose father had dedicated him to be a priest of the old religion. He became General Secretary of the Evangelical Church of West Africa, spoke at the 1974 Lausanne Congress, and drowned in the sea off Mombasa in December 1975 in circumstances that were never explained (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.). His book *Theological Pitfalls in Africa* (1975) warned against syncretism, the idea that the traditional religions are close enough to Christianity that people could be saved by following their traditions and living a good life. Kato affirmed that God reveals himself in nature, conscience and history, and insisted that Africa needs the clean cut gospel (The Aquila Report, n.d.). His emphasis was on discontinuity.

Kwame Bediako, the Ghanaian theologian who became the first rector of the Akrofi Christaller Institute and died in 2008, took a different emphasis (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). According to a Christianity Today account, he rejected radical continuity between African religion and Christianity, but argued that African animism "had a closer relationship with it than any other" religion, and he saw Christ as speaking through people's cultural heritage. In Hebrews he read Christ as an "elder brother" who fulfils the mediating role that African traditional religion gave to ancestors. Critics have said that this risks syncretism, and the same account notes that Bediako taught that conversion means turning to Christ with one's whole being and not abandoning one's heritage (Christianity Today, 2024).

John Mbiti stood nearer to Bediako. He held that the God of the Bible is the same God worshipped in traditional communities as Creator before Christianity and Islam arrived, and he pointed to the African names for God as evidence. He did not equate the two religions, since the covenant, the Trinity and the Holy Spirit were new, and he saw traditional religion as a preparation for the Christian message (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.).

I find both of them useful, and I would not want to lose either. Kato is right that a sincere devotion to the wrong address does not become saving, and that the shrine is not neutral ground. Bediako is right that Paul stood in Athens and found something to build on, and that a gospel that makes a convert despise his grandmother has not been well preached. The thing to avoid is the choice between contempt and flattery. The African church needs to learn to say, as Paul did, "what you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you," while knowing that Paul went on to call for repentance.

## Elijah on Mount Carmel at noon

There is a scene in 1 Kings 18 that the church ought to read more often, and not only for the theology.

Israel had been limping, in Elijah's word, between two opinions. The king had married into the worship of Baal, a drought had gone on for years, and Elijah summoned 450 prophets of Baal to Mount Carmel with a crowd of undecided people watching (1 Kings 18:19 to 21). The terms were simple. Each side would prepare a bull and put no fire under it. The God who answered with fire was the real one.

The prophets of Baal went first. They called on him from morning until noon. They danced round the altar. Nothing happened. Then Elijah, who had waited long enough, joined in.

> *"And at noon Elijah mocked them, saying, "Cry aloud, for he is a god. Either he is musing, or he is relieving himself, or he is on a journey, or perhaps he is asleep and must be awakened.""*
>
> — 1 Kings 18:27 (ESV)

You can hear the crowd shifting on its feet. This is sarcasm, and it is accurate sarcasm. A god who needs volume, repeated reminders and the right atmosphere has failed the first requirement of being a god. His target was an arrangement that depended on how loudly people could cry, and the people were secondary. The prophets answered by cutting themselves until they bled, and kept it up through the afternoon, and the text records that there was no voice, no answer, and no attention (1 Kings 18:26 to 29).

Then Elijah rebuilt a broken altar with twelve stones. He had water poured over the offering three times, until the trench around it was full, which in a drought is a deliberate extravagance. He prayed a short prayer. There was no dancing and no cutting.

> *"Then the fire of the LORD fell and consumed the burnt offering and the wood and the stones and the dust, and licked up the water that was in the trench."*
>
> — 1 Kings 18:38 (ESV)

Elijah had not been louder, or more devout in his performance, or better prepared. He was at the right address, and the God at that address was there. What was or wasn't behind Baal's altar I will leave to the apostle Paul. On Carmel, nothing answered. Notice too that Elijah's mockery was aimed at a system and a performance, and that the people who were watching were invited to choose, not to be shamed. Sarcasm is a tool of the prophets, and it is dangerous in the hands of the rest of us. Use it on ideas, and never on the grandmother.

## The God who crossed the street

Everything I have said so far is diagnosis. If that were the whole of it, the gospel would be a stern signboard that says you are standing in the wrong queue, good luck. That would be accurate, and it would be cruel.

The bank story needs one more turn. The man in the scene had to find the right branch for himself. He had to read the signs, ask the question and cross the road, and he was a competent adult who could do all of that. The people we love are not in that position. Most religious systems, including some very noble ones, describe a road a person must climb, and I won't flatten the many ways other faiths speak of mercy. But the Christian claim is peculiar in what it says about who does the crossing. God did, in person. He became flesh and walked into the mess, died on a Roman cross, and rose on the third day in a body, in history, in front of witnesses. If the bank is the right bank, the manager came out and took the cheque himself.

That is why the resurrection carries so much weight. N. T. Wright has argued as a historian that the empty tomb and the appearances together are best explained by the claim that Jesus was raised, and that the shape the early Christian movement took makes little sense without it (Wright, 2003). Reviewers have noted that he spends more of his energy on how historians should approach the question than on answering each sceptical theory in turn (Neff, 2003). So his case is best read next to the writers who take the sceptics on one by one. But the resurrection is the receipt. It is the thing that tells you the account is open.

> *"For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life."*
>
> — John 3:16 (ESV)

Whoever believes. The verse doesn't say whoever has been devout enough, or was raised in the right family, or performs the ritual with enough consistency. It says whoever believes, and it says the Father gave the Son first.

Augustine spent years running to every address the late Roman world offered. He tried the Manichaeans, then philosophy, then ambition and the polish of a good career, before he arrived at the God he had been circling the whole time. He begins his *Confessions* by saying that God made us for himself and that our hearts are restless until they rest in him (Augustine, 1991, Book 1, chapter 1). I read that restlessness as a sign that the transaction hasn't been completed. It is also a mercy, the same one the cashier shows when she looks up kindly and says the account you want is across the road.

## Walking across

If you are reading this from inside a family that has both banks on one street, here is something small and specific to try.

Don't begin with the argument. Open John 14 and read it in one sitting, from the beginning, until you get to Thomas. Thomas asked the question you are probably asking, and he asked it out loud in front of the others, and nobody laughed at him. Jesus did not scold him for asking, and he didn't hand him a map. Read what he says, and then say the sentence back to yourself in your own words.

After that, tell one person. A pastor will do, or a friend who has crossed the street ahead of you. You don't need to be ready, because nobody ever is, and you don't need every intellectual objection tied off. The disciples walked with him for three years and still got remarkable quantities of it wrong.

And if you have a Muslim neighbour, or a grandmother whose shrine still sits in a corner of the compound, or a cousin who says all roads lead to the same place, you do not need to win. Ask them what they hope for when they die, and listen all the way to the end of the answer. Then tell them whom you hope in, and why.

The window is open today. He has not moved from the door, and he is expecting you.
