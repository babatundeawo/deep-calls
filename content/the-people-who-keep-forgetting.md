## Twelve Stones and a Chronic Condition

The prayer meeting has been going for three hours. The room smells of candles and the particular kind of desperation that makes people honest with God. A woman in the third row is weeping not quietly, but with the full weight of someone who has run out of alternatives. She is asking God to show up. She is asking God to prove that He is real, that He is near, that He has not forgotten her.

And God — who healed her father's kidney disease in 2007, who provided her school fees through a stranger's phone transfer when there was no money and no plan, who parted the waters for her grandmother's generation, and has not once in the history of the universe broken a promise — that God is listening to her ask whether He is capable of doing anything at all.

This is not a story about a faithless woman. It is a story about a very human one. Because the catastrophic spiritual failure of God's people across all of recorded history has not been idolatry, though idolatry is bad enough. It has not been immorality, though the consequences of that have been spectacular and well-documented. The foundational failure, the one from which all the others seem to flow like tributaries from a poisoned source, is simpler and more humiliating than either: they forgot.

They forgot what God did. When they forgot what He did, they stopped believing He would do it again. When they stopped believing, they built golden calves and consulted mediums and made peace treaties with empires that should have been beneath their dignity. One descent. One source. They forgot.

The Israelites are, in this regard, almost heroically consistent. Forty years in the wilderness with a pillar of fire by night and a cloud by day — supernatural GPS, unambiguous and free of charge — and still finding time to complain that the manna is repetitive. Three months out of Egypt, where they witnessed ten plagues dismantle the greatest military superpower of the ancient world entirely on their behalf, and they are already at the foot of Sinai constructing a replacement god because Moses is taking longer than expected on the mountain. You want to shake them. You want to say: were you not there? Did you not see it? Have you already forgotten?

And then you look at your own prayer journal or the absence of one and you go quiet.

Moses understood the problem early. Which is why, before the Israelites had crossed a single river into the Promised Land, he sat them down and said:

> *"Do not be afraid of them; remember well what the LORD your God did to Pharaoh and to all Egypt."*
> — Deuteronomy 7:18 (NIV)

The command is not passive. Remember *well*. In Hebrew, the word is *zakar* and it does not mean the idle, ambient kind of memory that keeps your childhood phone number somewhere in an unused corner of your brain. *Zakar* is active. It is intentional. It is the kind of remembering that changes your posture, that reaches into the past and pulls something forward into the present tense and makes it load-bearing. Remember as an act of spiritual defiance against the anxiety eating you alive. Remember as a weapon, not a comfort. Remember as though your life depends on it, because it does.

Joshua, who understood that his predecessor was not given to unnecessary speeches, took the lesson to heart. When the Jordan parted and the nation crossed on dry ground — which was, by any reasonable measure, an impressive morning's work — he did not trust the moment to collective memory. He stopped twelve men, assigned one to each tribe, sent them back into the riverbed, and made them carry out a stone each. Twelve stones. Stacked into a monument on the western bank of the Jordan, in plain sight.

Why? Because Joshua knew what Moses knew. They would forget. Give them two good harvests and a measure of peace and they would forget. Give them prosperity and the monument would become the only thing standing between the nation and the amnesia that always preceded the disaster. He put the reason on record:

> *"In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it crossed the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever."*
> — Joshua 4:6-7 (NIV)

The stones are not decorative. They are theological. They are the ancient equivalent of writing it down, of building a system that forces the memory to survive the seasons of ease and the seductions of comfort. They are saying, in rock, what every generation must learn again: something happened here that the next generation needs to know about. Stack the stones. Because the water will rise again, and the next person to stand at this bank will need to know that it has moved before.

## The Passage Everyone Quotes and Almost Nobody Finishes Reading

Hebrews 11 is one of the most preached chapters in the New Testament, and with good reason. It is the faith hall of fame — Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Rahab, Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, the prophets. The chapter moves like a flooding river, sweeping name after name into a great roaring declaration that faith works, that faith always has worked, that faith is the evidence of things not yet seen and the substance of things still only hoped for.

What gets preached from this chapter, almost invariably, is the faith. What gets skipped, almost as invariably, is Hebrews 12:1. The chapter break is a modern editorial decision. The writer of Hebrews is not pausing. He turns the corner into chapter 12 and says something that changes the entire meaning of the list that came before:

> *"Therefore, since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles. And let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us, fixing our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith."*
> — Hebrews 12:1-2 (NIV)

A cloud of witnesses. Not spectators who have settled comfortably into their heavenly seats to watch the race from a safe distance. These are the people from chapter 11. Abel, who died at his brother's hand without seeing the promise. Abraham, who walked into a country he did not know toward a city he could not see. Moses, who chose the shame of Egypt's slaves over the pleasures of Egypt's palace when Egypt's palace was, by any worldly standard, a very attractive alternative. They are witnesses not merely in the sense of watching but in the sense of testifying. Their lives are the testimony. Their endurance is the evidence. Their stories are the cloud that presses in on every side as we run.

This means something that most preaching on Hebrews 11 fails to land: when you remember what God did for Abraham, you are not indulging in historical nostalgia. You are pulling a still-active testimony into your present moment and making it bear weight. The cloud is not behind you. It is around you. The faces in it belong to people who ran this same race under harder conditions, with less Scripture, less revelation, less understanding of the full scope of what Christ was going to accomplish — and they finished. Memory does not create faith; but faith rarely survives long without memory.

Forget the cloud and you run alone. Remember it and you run in company that the writer of Hebrews says constitutes a surrounding. Not a distant audience. A surrounding.

## What the Griot Knew Before the Seminary Did

Long before seminaries existed in West Africa — long before the missionary arrived at the coast carrying his Bible and a thorough conviction that African people needed to be saved from their own culture as much as from their sin — there was already a tradition in this part of the world that had grasped something the Western academy is still, in its better moments, trying to articulate: memory is not merely cognitive. It is moral. It is communal. It is, at its deepest register, sacred.

The griot — the oral historian, genealogist, and keeper of communal memory in West African societies — was not entertainment. To reduce the griot to entertainment is like reducing the Levitical priests to caterers. The griot held the genealogies, the origin stories, the records of victories and the honest accounting of defeats, the names of ancestors who had already walked the paths the living were only beginning to travel. Amadou Hampâté Bâ, the Malian historian and diplomat, made the point before the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization in 1960 with a precision that has outlived most of what else was said in that building that year: "In Africa, when an elder dies, it is a library that burns." Not a person who has passed. A library. Because without the memory, the community loses the thread of who it is, where it came from, and therefore what it is capable of becoming.

There is a concept running through this tradition that carries the same moral weight as the Hebrew *zakar*: the understanding that to remember your origins is not an act of sentimentality but an act of identity formation. You remember where you came from so you know who you are so you can know what to do next. The sequence matters. Memory is not the end of the chain — it is the beginning. It is the foundation on which identity is built, and identity is the foundation on which courage rests.

The Hebrew Bible understood this long before West African culture encoded it, and West African culture encoded it independently of the Hebrew Bible — which ought to tell us something about the God who stands behind both. Some truths are woven into the fabric of human experience because they were placed there by the One who designed human beings for memory. The testimony culture of African Christianity — the practice of standing before the congregation and declaring what God has done this week, this season, this year — is not a primitive ritual. It is *zakar* made communal. It is twelve stones stacked by twelve men from twelve tribes so that every child in the next generation inherits the evidence. The call-and-response of "God is good!" answered by "All the time!" is a confession of faith dressed in the clothes of celebration, and it is doing more theological work than it gets credit for.

The griot understood that a community which cannot remember its own story is a community vulnerable to anyone willing to write a new story for it. The same is true of the believer. The Christian who cannot recall the specific, particular, datable moments of God's faithfulness in their own life is a Christian who will be perpetually starting from scratch — perpetually asking God to prove Himself when He has already done so, perpetually anxious at the edge of a Jordan that has been parted before.

## The Notebook God Is Already Keeping

In the years after Jerusalem fell to Babylon — when the temple was ash and the walls were rubble and the promised prosperity of the covenant seemed to have followed the kingdom of David into the dust — a group of returning exiles was arguing about whether serving God was worth the effort. The arrogant ones seemed to prosper. The faithful seemed to suffer. The evidence on the ground did not obviously support the theology.

But there was another group. They talked to each other. And while they talked, God was listening. Malachi records what happened next with an economy of language that belies the enormity of what it describes:

> *"Then those who feared the LORD talked with each other, and the LORD listened and heard. A scroll of remembrance was written in his presence concerning those who feared the LORD and honoured his name."*
> — Malachi 3:16 (NIV)

A scroll of remembrance, written in God's presence. Not by God as a passive record-keeper filing away data, but as an active covenant response — the One who never forgets inscribing the names of the ones who chose to remember Him in a season when forgetting would have been entirely understandable. As if to say: I see what you are doing in the rubble. I see that you are choosing faithfulness when faithlessness would have cost you nothing. I am writing it down.

Nothing is lost. Not one prayer. Not one act of trust in a season of evidence to the contrary. Not one stone stacked by shaking hands in the ruins of everything that was supposed to be permanent.

This is the theology beneath the spiritual discipline of journaling — not the therapeutic exercise the wellness industry has appropriated and repackaged with pastel covers, but the covenantal record-keeping of someone who believes that what God has done in their life is worth the effort of documentation. When you write down what God did — the healing that arrived too late by every clinical measure, the provision that came through the wrong person at the wrong time in the right amount, the open door that appeared in a wall that looked solid — you are not keeping a diary. You are building your own scroll of remembrance. You are adding your twelve stones to the pile. You are making sure that the next time the Jordan rises, you do not have to find your faith from scratch.

The Lord's Supper is, in this light, the institutional form of the same practice. On the night of His arrest, hours before the cross, Jesus took bread and broke it and said words that the church has been repeating ever since, week after week, in languages He never spoke and in countries that did not exist when He said them:

> *"This is my body, which is for you; do this in remembrance of me."*
> — 1 Corinthians 11:24 (NIV)

And after supper:

> *"This cup is the new covenant in my blood; do this, whenever you drink it, in remembrance of me."*
> — 1 Corinthians 11:25 (NIV)

Do this in remembrance of me. Not in analysis of me. Not in celebration of me, though celebration is right. Not in debate about the precise metaphysics of what the bread and cup contain. In *remembrance*. Every time the church gathers at the table, it is performing a *zakar*. It is forcing itself structurally, communally, physically to call to mind what God did at Calvary before the week swallows it. Jesus, who understood what humans are capable of forgetting, built the antidote into the weekly rhythm of the church's life. The Lord's Supper is not a ritual. It is a prescription.

## Remembrance Is Not Nostalgia. It Is Warfare.

Here is the turn.

Everything said so far might give the impression that remembrance is a comfort — a warm mug of spiritual nostalgia, a browse through an album of divine miracles to soothe a restless heart in a difficult season. That impression is incomplete in the way that calling a surgeon's scalpel a nice piece of cutlery is technically accurate and catastrophically inadequate.

Remembrance is warfare. It is one of the most aggressive things a believer can do.

Consider Jeremiah in Lamentations 3. This is not a man writing from a position of comfort or theological leisure. Jerusalem has fallen. The temple is ash. His people are in chains on a road to Babylon. He is sitting in the ruins of everything he was trained to believe was permanent, and in verses 17 through 20 he is drowning. He has forgotten what prosperity looks like. His hope, he says, is gone. He is bent low with wormwood and gall, and his soul is bowed down within him. And then — and this is the turn that has kept the church breathing through three thousand years of ruin — he does something that looks small on the page and is, in reality, enormous:

> *"But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope: The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness."*
> — Lamentations 3:21-23 (ESV)

"But this I call to mind." Not: but this occurred to me in a pleasant moment. Not: but this came to me when the grief eased. I *call* to mind an active, volitional, deliberate act of memory executed in the middle of devastation. Jeremiah reaches past the evidence of ruin and retrieves the character of God and holds it in the room, against everything the room is saying, until it grows stronger than the despair. He does not deny the ruins. He does not pretend the grief is not real. He does not perform a happiness he does not feel. He simply introduces something else into the equation — the love that does not cease, the mercy that does not run out, the faithfulness that has never had a bad morning — and the equation changes.

This is what Psalm 77 looks like from the inside. The psalmist opens in genuine darkness:

> *"I cried out to God for help; I cried out to God to hear me. When I was in distress, I sought the Lord; at night I stretched out untiring hands, and I would not be comforted."*
> — Psalm 77:1-2 (NIV)

By verse 7, he is asking the questions that every believer has asked in the dark: Has God forgotten to be merciful? Has His anger shut off His compassion? These are not rhetorical questions dressed up to look sincere. They are the real questions of a real person lying on the floor of a real crisis. And then the psalmist makes a decision. Not a feeling. A decision. Verse 11:

> *"I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your miracles of long ago. I will consider all your works and meditate on all your mighty deeds."*
> — Psalm 77:11-12 (NIV)

"I will remember." The emotion has not changed. The darkness has not lifted on a convenient theological switch. The circumstances have not been resolved. But the psalmist plants a flag in the ground and says: I am going to pull God's record into this room and set it next to my despair, and I am going to see which one is stronger.

Charles Spurgeon, preaching through the Psalms in the 19th century, wrote of this passage: "The remembrance of former mercies is a powerful weapon against present doubts." He was right, and he understood what the Nigerian Christian has always understood by instinct if not always by articulation: the testimony is not a celebration. The testimony is an arsenal. The believer who has cultivated the discipline of remembrance — who has written down what God has done, who has spoken it before the congregation, who has stacked the stones — that believer does not face a crisis empty-handed. They face it with evidence. The evidence does not cancel the pain. It out-argues the despair.

God Himself commands the practice. In Isaiah 46, addressed to a people who were being tempted to look at the impressive gods of Babylon and reconsider their allegiances, He does not make a sentimental appeal. He makes a rational one:

> *"Remember the former things, those of long ago; I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me."*
> — Isaiah 46:9 (NIV)

The command to remember is a command to reject the competition by consulting the record. You cannot genuinely believe that God is incomparable and simultaneously panic as though He has run out of options. The memory of His former deeds is not emotional support — it is theology in the most practical form theology can take. It is the accumulated evidence of a God who has never once been unable.

Augustine, reflecting on the restlessness that drives human beings to seek satisfaction in every direction except the one where it can actually be found, wrote at the very opening of his *Confessions*: "Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." The restlessness Augustine names is, in part, the restlessness of a soul that has lost the thread of its own story with God — a soul that has forgotten the evidence and is wandering through alternatives looking for what was never lost. Remembrance reorients. It does not merely comfort; it corrects. It pulls the wandering soul back to the evidence and says: look at what has already been done. Now look at what you are afraid of. Now look again.

## The Door Being Held Open

There is a woman in a prayer meeting somewhere — Lagos, London, Lusaka, it doesn't matter — weeping on a Wednesday night and asking God to show up. God has shown up before. For her. For her mother. For Abraham and for Jeremiah and for the psalmist and for the twelve men who carried twelve stones out of a riverbed on a day the Jordan stood still. The question has never been whether God is faithful. That question was answered before she was born, before Moses, before Abraham — before the creation of the world that He spoke into being and called good without pausing to check whether it was good.

The question is whether she will remember that He is.

Not vaguely. Not as background noise. Not in the soft, untested way that keeps the idea available without letting it cost anything. Remember well. Call it to mind. Stack the stones before the next flood comes and you are standing at the bank wondering whether the water has ever moved. Write in your scroll. Take the bread and the cup and let your body perform the theology your emotions cannot currently access. Stand in the congregation and say what God did — not because the telling benefits God, but because someone in the room is still waiting for their Jordan to part and they need to hear that the water has moved before. Your testimony is not about you. It is the cloud. It is the twelve stones. It is the griot's library standing intact while everything else burns.

The psalmist closes this argument with a simplicity that great theology often achieves at the end of the fight:

> *"I remember the days of long ago; I meditate on all your works and consider what your hands have done."*
> — Psalm 143:5 (NIV)

He is not remembering for sentiment. He is remembering for survival. He is building a case against despair from the evidence of history, and he is winning.

So can you. Not because you are strong enough to manufacture faith from nothing, and not because your circumstances are about to improve on your preferred timeline, but because the God who never forgets has a record without a single failure in it. Every covenant He has ever made, He has kept. Every promise He has ever spoken is already complete in the mind of the One for whom time is a creation, not a constraint.

Remember that. Then write it down. Then tell someone who is standing at the edge of a river they have never seen move.
