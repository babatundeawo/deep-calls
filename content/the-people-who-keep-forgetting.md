## Three Hours Into the Prayer Meeting

Picture a prayer meeting that has been going for three hours. This is a scene and not a report, but anyone who has sat through a Nigerian vigil will know how the room smells by then, of candles and sweat and the particular honesty that comes when people have run out of plans. A woman in the third row is crying with her whole body. She wants God to show up. She wants proof that he is near and has not forgotten her.

Now suppose her history, because the scene needs it. Her father's kidney trouble eased years ago, against what the doctors had told the family. Her school fees were once paid by a stranger's transfer on the very morning she was due to be sent home. Her grandmother's generation lived through things that should have flattened a family and didn't. None of it was small. And tonight she is praying as if God has never done a thing in her life and she must open the case again from the beginning.

I don't tell this to scold her. Nearly everyone who prays has stood where she stands. The point is how ordinary it is. A great deal of the trouble in the Bible begins at exactly this place, with people who forget. They forget what God did, so they stop expecting him to do it again, and then they start looking about for something else to lean on. Gold calves, mediums, treaties with empires that had no business being trusted. Different symptoms, one condition.

I want to take that condition seriously for the length of an essay. The claim is simple. Forgetting what God has done is among the oldest dangers in the Bible, and Scripture does not answer it with a lecture. It answers by building memory into things you can touch and repeat: stones, a meal, a feast, a written record, a song, a decision made at night. Along the way I want to look at what the Hebrew word for remembering actually asks of us, why African ways of keeping the past help us read the Bible honestly, and what to do with the harder cases, when your own memory is full of prayers that were not answered the way you asked.

## How Quickly Israel Forgot

Israel is almost heroically consistent here. Within weeks of leaving Egypt, where ten plagues had taken apart the greatest military power of the age on their behalf, the people stand on the far side of the sea and sing. Three days later they are in the desert and the water is bitter, and the singing has turned to complaint (Exodus 15:22 to 24). Soon they are looking back at Egypt with something like fondness. They had a pillar of cloud by day and fire by night, which is guidance with no network problems and no fee, and they still found time to say the manna was monotonous. By the time Moses has been on Sinai for forty days they are melting their jewellery to make a calf, because the leader is late and a god you can see is easier to wait for than one you have to trust (Exodus 32:1 to 4).

You want to shake them. Were you not there? Then you look at your own prayer journal, or the absence of one, and you go quiet.

It would be comforting to think that only the wilderness generation was so forgetful. The book of Judges says otherwise. When Joshua's generation died, "there arose another generation after them who did not know the LORD or the work that he had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10, ESV). Read that again. These were not atheists. They lived inside the land, under the story, in sight of the stones. They simply had not been told, or had not been told in a way that stuck, and the next several centuries of Israel's history are what follows from that one failure of transmission. Psalm 106, which rehearses the whole sorry record in one breath, puts it in six words about the generation at the sea: "But they soon forgot his works" (Psalm 106:13, ESV).

The disciples are no better, and the Gospels do not flatter them. In Mark 8 Jesus has just fed four thousand people from seven loaves, and not long before that five thousand from five. Then the Twelve climb into a boat and fret because they have only one loaf between them. Jesus turns on them with a question that stings because it is so reasonable. He asks if they have eyes and do not see, ears and do not hear, and "do you not remember?" (Mark 8:18, ESV). Then he makes them count the baskets of leftovers, twelve in the one case and seven in the other, as if to say that the evidence is still in your hands. Twelve baskets and twelve men, and still they forgot. If that is how forgetful the people were who had just eaten the miracle, nobody reading this has grounds to feel superior.

So the problem is not a shortage of divine activity. Israel, and the church after her, have had more than enough of that. The problem is that memory does not look after itself.

## What the Word Remember Asks

The old preachers said that in Hebrew the verb is *zakar*, and the Old Testament scholar Brevard Childs devoted a short and influential book, *Memory and Tradition in Israel* (1962), to arguing that it rarely means a quiet inner recollection. As I understand his case, the word often carries the sense of attending, acting and keeping faith, so that to remember God is already to be doing something about it, and to forget him is close to abandoning him. You can see why this fits the text. In Deuteronomy, the great book of remembering, "remember" and "do not forget" sit beside commands about obedience in a way that would be odd if the verbs only described a mental event.

I would add one thing that is easy to miss, and it is the bedrock under everything else in this essay. The first thing to say about remembering in the Bible is that God does it. He remembers Noah in the ark and the waters begin to recede. He hears Israel groaning in Egypt and remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and the exodus begins (Genesis 8:1; Exodus 2:24). Mary, pregnant with Jesus, sings that God has helped his servant Israel "in remembrance of his mercy" (Luke 1:54, ESV). When Scripture says God remembers, nobody supposes he had mislaid something. It means he acts on what he promised. Our remembering is an answer to his. We are not manufacturing a faithfulness that would otherwise be missing. We are trying to stay in step with one that never was.

Moses knew the danger before Israel crossed a single river. Facing a land full of people who looked stronger than they were, he did not tell them to be brave. He told them to remember well what the LORD their God did to Pharaoh and to all Egypt (Deuteronomy 7:18). Courage, on his account, is something you build out of evidence. A frightened people with a good memory can stand, and a confident people with a bad one cannot. The command is also a refusal of a cheap idea of faith, in which belief floats free of anything that has happened.

That has a pastoral edge. When a believer is in the dark and cannot summon any faith, the mercy is that the argument does not rest on her recall. It rests on his. But Scripture still commands us to remember, and it does so precisely because forgetting is how a person drifts from the One who holds her.

## Why God Told Joshua to Carry Stones

When the nation crossed the Jordan on dry ground, nobody could have been in doubt about what had happened. That was the morning to hold a long meeting about remembering, and Israel didn't. Instead God told Joshua to choose twelve men, one from each tribe, and have each carry a stone out of the riverbed to the place where the people would camp that night (Joshua 4:2 to 3). The stones were God's idea before they were Joshua's. Joshua also set up twelve more in the middle of the river itself, where the priests had stood, and the writer says they were still there when he wrote (Joshua 4:9).

What were they for? The text is clear.

> *"that this may be a sign among you. When your children ask in time to come, 'What do those stones mean to you?' then you shall tell them that the waters of the Jordan were cut off before the ark of the covenant of the LORD. When it passed over the Jordan, the waters of the Jordan were cut off. So these stones shall be to the people of Israel a memorial forever."*
> — Joshua 4:6 to 7 (ESV)

Notice who the stones are built for. They are built for a child who wasn't there. The people who walked across never needed a monument. They had wet feet to remind them. The pile exists so that a ten year old, years later, will point and ask, and so that someone will be obliged to answer. A stone with no question put to it is only a stone. And look at how the answer is expected to be framed. Elsewhere in Deuteronomy a child asks what the commands mean, and the parent does not say "they were slaves in Egypt". The parent says "we" (Deuteronomy 6:20 to 21). Moses presses the point to a generation that was mostly born in the wilderness and had never seen Egypt: the covenant was made "not with our fathers" only but "with us, who are all of us here alive today" (Deuteronomy 5:3, ESV). Memory in Israel is a way of making the past belong to people who weren't there.

The word the writer uses for these stones is the same word used of Passover. The feast was to be "a memorial day" for all generations (Exodus 12:14, ESV), and the instruction for the night was that the father should tell his son what the LORD did for him when he left Egypt (Exodus 13:8). One word, two devices, a heap of rocks and a roast lamb. And the high priest himself wore two stones on his shoulders, engraved with the names of the sons of Israel, as "stones of remembrance" (Exodus 28:12). Wherever the priest went, the people went with him in stone. Israel's religion was soaked in the practice of making the past hard to lose.

The stones also had a second audience. Joshua says they were there "that all the peoples of the earth may know that the hand of the LORD is mighty" (Joshua 4:24, ESV). The memory was meant to travel past the family, and, as we will see, that is exactly what the church has tried to do with it.

Later in Israel's history the habit went on. When Samuel set up a stone after a hard won victory he named it Ebenezer, saying, "Till now the LORD has helped us" (1 Samuel 7:12, ESV). Anyone who has driven past church signs in Ibadan or Enugu or Port Harcourt will have seen that name often enough to forget it means a stone. It was a marker set at a place where something happened, so that the next person who stood there would not have to guess.

## How Comfort Wears Memory Thin

You might expect people to forget in a crisis. The Bible says they forget in comfort. Before Israel entered the land, Moses warned them about the very thing that lay ahead, which was success. He told them to take care that when they entered a land of cities they had not built and cisterns they had not dug, they should not forget the LORD who brought them out of Egypt (Deuteronomy 6:10 to 12). And in the next sermon:

> *"lest, when you have eaten and are full and have built good houses and live in them, and when your herds and flocks multiply and your silver and gold is multiplied and all that you have is multiplied, then your heart be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God."*
> — Deuteronomy 8:12 to 14 (ESV)

A few verses later he adds a warning against a sentence that sounds harmless: "My power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth" (Deuteronomy 8:17, ESV). Anyone who has watched a church member get a promotion, then a car, then a Sunday job that takes him out of the pew will know the pattern. He didn't decide to leave God. He just stopped telling the story of how he got there. Moses' remedy for this in the same chapter is a command to remember "the whole way that the LORD your God has led you these forty years in the wilderness" (Deuteronomy 8:2, ESV), and that is a striking choice of memory. He does not tell them to remember the good harvests. He tells them to remember the hunger and the manna, the years when they had nothing but him.

So the stones are not a decoration for hard days. They are insurance against good ones.

There is a counterfeit of remembering that deserves a mention, because it fools sincere people. In Numbers 11 the Israelites say, "We remember the fish we ate in Egypt that cost nothing, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, and the garlic" (Numbers 11:5, ESV). Look at what they remember and what they do not. Egypt had free fish, apparently. It also had slave drivers and drowned infants and the lash. Nostalgia edits. It keeps the cucumbers and loses the whip. Every Nigerian who has heard an uncle say that nothing was ever as good as the old days, and has watched the same uncle forget the queues and the shortages he complained about at the time, knows how it works. Biblical remembering is the opposite discipline. It insists on the whole record, the slavery along with the rescue, the hunger along with the manna, and it asks you to stay honest about both. A church that remembers only the triumphs is as forgetful as one that remembers nothing.

## The Crowd Around the Track

Hebrews 11 is the long roll of people who trusted God without seeing the end, and it is easy to preach it as a list of heroes: Abel, Enoch, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, Moses, Rahab, Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, David, Samuel, the prophets. The chapter moves like a river in flood, name after name, and the writer insists that this faith has always worked. But he doesn't stop at the list. He turns straight into the next sentence, though the chapter break hides it.

> *"Therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us."*
> — Hebrews 12:1 (ESV)

Readers have long disagreed about what the witnesses are doing. Some picture them in the stands watching us run. I lean to the other reading, that they are witnesses in the sense of testifying, since the whole of chapter 11 is about what their lives attest. The argument doesn't depend on settling it. Christians also differ on a larger question that this verse touches, which is whether the departed saints pray for us. Catholic and Orthodox Christians say they do, and Protestants in the main say Scripture does not teach it, and the verse itself does not decide the matter either way. What every tradition can say is the part the text certainly says. These people ran, they finished, and their record is meant to press in on the runner.

Either way, the effect is the same. When you recall what God did for Abraham, who set off for a country he had never seen, or for Moses, who preferred the company of God's people to the comforts of a palace, you are not wandering into history for entertainment. You are putting people around you. The cloud isn't behind you. It is on every side. And it has this advantage over the memory of your own best moments, that it contains people who ran the race with less Scripture and less light than you have, and finished.

But Hebrews 11 does not allow a cheap reading, and I should not give one. The chapter's second half turns darker. Some "were tortured", others suffered mocking, flogging, chains and imprisonment, and some "were killed with the sword" (Hebrews 11:35 to 37, ESV). Of these the writer says that although they were commended for their faith, they did not receive what was promised (Hebrews 11:39). Not every name in the cloud was rescued. If the only memories we allow are the ones that end in a parting river, we have misread the chapter. The cloud is a witness to God's faithfulness, and that includes people for whom faithfulness looked like endurance to the end and a better country beyond it. I'll come back to this, because it is where the hard objection lives.

## What Oral Memory Has Always Known

The Bible is not the only place to learn that a people keeps its past by deliberate effort. Amadou Hampâté Bâ, the Malian writer and scholar, spent a lifetime recording and defending the oral traditions of West Africa. He was Fulbe by birth, lived from 1900 to 1991, and served on the executive council of UNESCO. In a chapter he wrote for UNESCO's *General History of Africa*, titled "The Living Tradition", he said that "where writing does not exist, man is bound to the word he utters", and that "the world's earliest archives or libraries were the brains of men." He said that "generally speaking every old man in Africa is a master of knowledge in some historical or traditional field." He also observed that those who do not write have the most highly developed memory, and he said that African tradition conceives of speech as a gift of God.

Hear that last claim as a theologian. The word is not a throwaway sound in this view. It binds the speaker. It is received, and it is to be kept. That is a long way from a culture in which memory is a leisure activity, and it sits easily with Moses' demand that a father tell his son.

You will often hear Bâ's thought compressed into a saying, that when an old man dies in Africa, a library burns. The wording shifts from one retelling to the next, and I could not trace those exact words to a first text, so I won't put them in his mouth. His own written words are enough. They also correct a common mistake. Bâ was careful to say that the person the French call a griot, the hereditary keeper of a people's history in many of the societies of the Sahel and the western savanna, is far from the only qualified guardian of the tradition. The memory of a community was kept by many hands, elders and lineage heads and specialists, and the griot is one among them. To reduce the griot to a praise singer at weddings would be like reducing the Levites to caterers. To make him the whole of African memory would be an overstatement too.

I should be careful about what I claim. Bâ's examples come mainly from Mali and its neighbours, and Nigeria is a large country with many peoples who keep their memories in different ways, through royal courts, family lineages, naming customs and the telling of the town's founding. I am not claiming that West Africans worked out the Hebrew insight on their own, or that the Hebrews borrowed it, or that African traditions are the Bible in disguise. I don't know, and I distrust anyone who says they do. The modest claim is better, and I think it holds. In places where people knew that a community can lose itself in one generation, they set up structures to prevent it. In Israel the structures were stones, festivals and a family table. In many African societies they were elders whose job was to remember. If a God designed human beings for memory, we should expect to find evidence of the design in more than one place.

The church inherits both. Think of the testimony time in a Nigerian service, when a woman stands to say what God did for her this week. It's easy to treat that as a warm interval before the sermon. I would put it closer to Joshua's pile of stones, a public record of the kind the child is meant to ask about. Somebody in that room is at the edge of a river of their own, and they need to hear that it has been crossed before. The old call and response, where a leader says that God is good and the room answers that he is so all the time, is a confession of faith dressed as celebration, and it has been teaching theology to children who could not yet read a catechism.

The Ghanaian theologian Kwame Bediako pressed a related point in *Theology and Identity* (1992), which compares the identity problems of Christians in the second century with those of African Christians today. As his reviewers describe it, his argument rests on the belief that church history is organically one, so that younger churches meet in their own time questions earlier churches met elsewhere. For our subject, the lesson is that an African believer who knows only the last fifty years of her own church is short of memory. The Christian past is hers. Tertullian wrote from Carthage and Augustine from Hippo, and neither of them is a European import. Bediako also saw that the question of how much of one's inherited culture to keep is answered differently by different African thinkers. The reviewer notes that Byang Kato sat with the critics of building on pre Christian religion and Tertullian's warnings about compromise, while Bediako was more sympathetic to those who looked for traces of God in African religious life. I'd say the church does well to hold both warnings together. Memory that is not tested against Scripture drifts into syncretism, and memory that is cut off from a people's actual story drifts into foreign borrowing.

Samuel Ajayi Crowther is the nearest example in our own history. Born in Osogun around 1809, he was seized by slave raiders in 1821 at about twelve, sold, freed by the Royal Navy, and set down in Freetown, Sierra Leone. He became the first African Anglican bishop of West Africa, consecrated at Canterbury in 1864, and the translation of the Bible into Yoruba, finished in the mid 1880s, was part of his life's work, along with primers in Igbo and Nupe. A boy who lost his home to forgetting's cousin, violence, spent his life putting God's record into the mother tongues of the people it would reach. That is Joshua's stones carried by a bishop. A people who can read what God has done in their own language have a library that does not burn.

## A Book Written While Nobody Watched

Malachi writes to people who are back in the land, with the temple standing and the offerings going on, and the fire gone out of it all. Some say that serving God is pointless, because the arrogant seem to do well. In the middle of that grumbling, the prophet notices a smaller conversation.

> *"Then those who feared the LORD spoke with one another. The LORD paid attention and heard them, and a book of remembrance was written before him of those who feared the LORD and esteemed his name."*
> — Malachi 3:16 (ESV)

Look at what this describes. A handful of people keep talking about God when talking about him costs them something, and God keeps a record of it. I don't want to stretch the verse into a promise that every private prayer is filed somewhere. It doesn't say that. It says that faithfulness in a dry season is noticed and written down by the one who has never needed reminding. It is also communal. They "spoke with one another". Whatever memory this is, it is not a private act in a locked room, and the ones in the record are the people who kept each other going.

For a believer, that is the courage to keep a notebook of your own. Writing down the healing that came late, the money that arrived through the wrong person in the right amount, the door that opened in what looked like a wall, is not therapy with a pastel cover. It is your own small pile of stones, kept where you can find it when the water rises. The practice is plain. Write the date and the thing in a sentence, and when you can, write who prayed. Someone in your house, someone in your church, can read it later, and a child who finds it may ask the question the stones are for.

## The Table Where We Rehearse It

On the night he was betrayed, Jesus took bread and said words the church has repeated in languages he never spoke and countries that didn't exist.

> *"This is my body, which is for you. Do this in remembrance of me. ... This cup is the new covenant in my blood. Do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of me."*
> — 1 Corinthians 11:24 to 25 (ESV)

He didn't ask for analysis, and he didn't ask for a debate about exactly how bread and cup relate to his body. He asked for remembrance, and he put it into a meal that would come round again and again, because he knew what happens to a memory that has to survive a busy week. The meal was set inside Passover, the same feast Israel kept with a lamb. The Greek word for remembrance, *anamnesis*, is the one the church has argued over for two thousand years. A Catholic reads it with the Council of Trent, which in the sixteenth century taught that the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ in substance. The Orthodox speak of a holy mystery and decline to define it, and of a remembering that makes the saving event present to the worshipper. Lutherans teach that Christ's body and blood are truly present with the bread and wine. Calvin held that Christ is truly present to the believer by the Spirit, though not in the bread itself, and Zwingli, in the stricter memorial view that many Baptist and Pentecostal churches share, saw the meal chiefly as a remembrance and a confession. These are serious differences and I won't flatten them.

But look at what all of them keep. Nobody in that room has ever said the point of the meal is to forget. Every tradition that takes the words seriously agrees that Jesus built the remembering into a physical act and put it on a calendar. The Lord's Supper is the same kind of thing as the stones. Something happened once, and a physical act has been put in place so that nobody has to depend on a good mood to recall it. Paul adds a line that shows how public it is meant to be: "For as often as you eat this bread and drink the cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26, ESV). It is testimony, and it faces both ways. It looks back to Calvary and forward to the return.

And it carries the same ethic as the stones. The church is to say what happened, to children who were not there, again and again, until the Lord comes back.

## Which Memory Wins in the Dark

Here the argument turns, and I should say plainly that I have made remembering sound easier than it is.

Not every memory is a comfort. Take the poet of Lamentations, whom tradition names as Jeremiah, though the book itself doesn't say. Jerusalem has fallen, and he is writing out of the ruins. In the middle of the third chapter, he first asks God to remember, and then admits what his own memory is doing.

> *"Remember my affliction and my wanderings, the wormwood and the gall! My soul continually remembers it and is bowed down within me."*
> — Lamentations 3:19 to 20 (ESV)

Read that slowly. He has not failed to remember. His trouble is that he remembers too well, and the memory of the bad days follows him everywhere and drags him down. He has said already that his hope has perished. Then comes the sentence that the church has held on to for centuries.

> *"But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope: The steadfast love of the LORD never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness."*
> — Lamentations 3:21 to 23 (ESV)

He calls something to mind. He does not receive it as a pleasant idea on a calm afternoon. He pulls it forward on purpose, and he sets it down beside the ruins without pretending the ruins aren't there. Two memories are in the room, and he picks the one he will feed. He is not denying the grief. He is adding something to it, and the sum changes.

Psalm 77 works the same way from the inside. The psalmist can't sleep, and his soul "refuses to be comforted" (Psalm 77:2, ESV). By the middle of the psalm he is asking whether God's love has ended for good (Psalm 77:8, ESV). Even remembering God at first only makes him groan. Then he makes a decision, and it is a decision, not a mood.

> *"Then I said, 'I will appeal to this, to the years of the right hand of the Most High.' I will remember the deeds of the LORD; yes, I will remember your wonders of old. I will ponder all your work, and meditate on your mighty deeds."*
> — Psalm 77:10 to 12 (ESV)

Nothing in the psalm says the darkness lifted that night. What changes is that the record of what God has done has been brought into the argument. The same move is behind God's own words to Israel in exile, when they were tempted by the impressive gods of Babylon.

> *"remember the former things of old; for I am God, and there is no other; I am God, and there is none like me."*
> — Isaiah 46:9 (ESV)

That is a case made from evidence. You can't hold that no one is like God and also panic as though he has run out of options, and the record exists to stop the panic. Psalm 143 closes the pattern with the plainest sentence in the Psalter on the subject. The writer is hunted and spent, and he says, "I remember the days of old; I meditate on all that you have done; I ponder the work of your hands" (Psalm 143:5, ESV). He is not remembering for sentiment. He is remembering for survival.

## Why Remembering Is a Kind of Fighting

Some people say remembrance is warfare, and others find the language overblown. I think the biblical material supports a measured form of it. The one place the New Testament speaks of conquering an accuser, in Revelation 12, says that believers "have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony" (Revelation 12:11, ESV). The setting is martyrdom, so I would not make the verse mean more than it says, but notice that spoken testimony sits beside the cross as a weapon. A testimony is not a decoration for the evening service. It answers a charge. And Paul, writing to a young pastor under pressure, gives him an instruction that is almost a whole strategy in four words: "Remember Jesus Christ, risen from the dead" (2 Timothy 2:8, ESV).

Why does it work? Because despair is an argument. It says that God has gone quiet, that he is not who you thought, that the evidence is against him. The believer's reply is also an argument, and it uses evidence. This is the point I most want the sceptic to hear, because it is where the Christian case for remembering differs from a self help technique. The stones at the Jordan stood in public, where anyone could go and ask. The apostles' memory is also anchored in public events. Paul tells the Corinthians that the risen Christ appeared to more than five hundred people at one time, "most of whom are still alive" (1 Corinthians 15:6, ESV), which is an invitation to go and check. Luke says he set out to write an orderly account after investigating everything carefully from the start (Luke 1:3 to 4). Christian remembering is not a mood we conjure to feel better. It points at things said to have happened and invites examination. If those things did not happen, the faith is empty, and Paul says exactly that. If they did, then the evidence against despair is already on the table.

None of this is a trick for talking yourself into cheerfulness. The spiritual discipline of recalling what God has done has a precise function. When Jeremiah wrote that his hope had perished and then called something to mind, the something was not an opinion about the future. It was a settled thing about the character of the LORD. Augustine, who was an African and wrote from the coast of North Africa, opened his *Confessions* with the words, in E. B. Pusey's nineteenth century English, "Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." The restlessness he names is in part the restlessness of a soul that has lost the thread of its story with God and is trying one alternative after another. Remembrance reorients. It pulls the wandering soul back to the evidence and says: look at what has already been done, then look at what you are afraid of.

## What About the Prayers That Were Not Answered

Now to the hard objection, which is the strongest one and deserves its full weight. Someone will say that this is all very well for the woman whose father recovered. What about the woman whose father did not? What do you tell a family who prayed all night, believed God for a healing, and buried the patient on Friday? If your memory is full of prayers that were not answered, a call to remember what God has done sounds like an insult.

I would not answer that quickly, and I would not answer it with a formula. Four things seem to me to be true.

First, the Bible does not hide from it. Psalm 44 is the classic case. It begins by recalling what God did "in the days of old", how he drove out nations and planted his people (Psalm 44:1 to 3). Then it turns on God. "You have rejected us and disgraced us", it says, and then, in a sentence that no one invented to comfort anyone, "All this has come upon us, though we have not forgotten you, and we have not been false to your covenant" (Psalm 44:9 and 17, ESV). Remembering is the ground on which the complaint stands. The psalmist is allowed to bring God's own record to God and say that the present does not match it. Psalm 88, the one psalm that ends without a ray of light, is in the canon too. The Bible makes room for those who remember and still grieve.

Second, the memory the Bible prescribes is of the whole record, and that includes the people in the second half of Hebrews 11. They did not receive what was promised in their lifetime, and the writer calls them people "of whom the world was not worthy" (Hebrews 11:38, ESV). If your own history includes a funeral that God did not prevent, that is not evidence that you have nothing to remember. It means the cloud around you has more in it than rescued people. Some of what God did for his people was to keep them to the end, and the church has always counted its martyrs and its faithful dead as witnesses, not as failures.

Third, remembering is not forced cheerfulness. It sits beside lament, as Lamentations 3 does. The writer says his hope has perished and does not pretend otherwise. If a pastor tells a grieving family to simply remember how good God is, and says it as though grief were forgetfulness, the pastor has misused the text. Grief is not the opposite of faith. In the Bible it is one of the ways faith talks.

Fourth, and here I will put my own conviction on the table, the central thing the believer is told to remember is not a list of answered requests. It is the cross and the empty tomb. The memory that does not fail when the hospital bed is empty is that God did not spare his own Son and has raised him. Paul builds on exactly this: the God who did not spare his Son will give us all things with him (Romans 8:32). The anchor is that fact, and the rescued father is a sign that points toward it. When the sign is missing, the fact still holds. I realise this will not feel like much to a person in the first week of grief. It isn't meant to be said to her in that week. It is meant to be there, waiting, in the stones she or someone else has already stacked.

## The God Who Chooses to Forget

One more difficulty is worth a paragraph, because anyone who reads the Bible with care will raise it. If God never forgets, what do we make of the promise in the new covenant, "I will remember their sin no more" (Jeremiah 31:34, ESV)? Does God forget after all?

The answer, I think, is that Scripture uses remembering and forgetting as covenant words, and not as mere reports on what a mind retains. To remember is to act on something. To remember sin no more is to refuse to bring it against the person, to decline to prosecute. Isaiah has God say, "I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake, and I will not remember your sins" (Isaiah 43:25, ESV). This is not amnesia in the divine mind. The cross is a record kept in full, and the case is closed on it. So the same God who never forgets his people's good, and who remembers his covenant for a thousand generations, has also chosen to be the one who will not hold their sins against them. It is the best news in the book. And it explains why the stones can sit beside the confession of sin. Israel remembered their rebellion in their liturgy and still sang of mercy, because what God has declared forgotten at the level of charge is not the same as what the community has forgotten at the level of lesson.

## Back to the Third Row

Go back to the woman in the third row, who may be in Lagos or Lusaka or a church hall in London. God has shown up for her before, and for her mother, and for Abraham, and for the poet who sat in the ruins and chose which memory to follow. The question was never whether he is faithful. The question is whether she will call to mind what she already knows, and whether somebody will make that easy for her.

I would ask her to do something small and specific. Before the next meeting, write down one thing God has done in your life, with the date, the way you'd label a stone. Don't polish it. Then find a person who is waiting at their own river, and tell them. If your history also holds something God did not do, write that down too, and bring it to him honestly, in the manner of Psalm 44. He can take it. And when the bread and the cup come round on Sunday, take them as the thing they were meant to be, a memorial with a child in the room who has not yet asked what it means.

If you are the woman in the third row tonight, you needn't stop praying or pretend you aren't desperate. Only say, between the tears, what you remember. He has never needed reminding. You do, and so do the rest of us.
