## A Story About an Empty Pulpit

The story goes like this. Henry Ward Beecher was due to preach at Plymouth Church in Brooklyn, and he did not appear. A substitute stood up, and word went round the pews, as it does, and people began to leave without much effort to hide it. The substitute waited a moment, called for quiet, and said something close to this: "All who came this morning to worship Henry W. Beecher may now leave. The rest will remain and worship God."

It turns up in devotional pieces and sermon illustrations, and it is a good one. In the version I found most often the substitute is Beecher's brother, and it is credited to a church bulletin, with no date and no further source (Clifton, n.d.). Another page, dated 2019, gives the line as "All who came to worship Henry Ward Beecher may now leave. All who came to worship God, keep your seats!" and cites nothing at all (Noland, n.d.). The two versions don't agree on the wording, and in other tellings Beecher is ill and the substitute is an unnamed minister. I could not trace the story to a first source.

So I won't tell you that it happened. It might have, in some form. Beecher was a real man, born in 1813 and dead in 1887, and in 1847 he became the first pastor of Plymouth Church, where the new sanctuary seated 2,800 (Henry Ward Beecher, n.d.; Plymouth Church, n.d.). After his father died in 1863 he was described as the most famous preacher in the nation. He did have a real brother, Thomas, who was also a minister and who preached in Brooklyn before moving to Elmira, New York, in 1854 (Thomas K. Beecher, n.d.). But nothing I could open puts Thomas in the Plymouth pulpit on a morning like that, and I won't pretend otherwise.

A story with no author and no date, passed from one sermon to the next, is a story, and I will treat it as one. Stories like this survive because they are true to something, even when they are not true to a particular Sunday in Brooklyn. What this one is true to is what the rest of this essay is about. And I will argue that the story is a little too easy on us, because the real question it raises is not about the crowd that left.

## The Athenians Loved Anything New

Imagine, first, that the people who walked out that morning did not know what they were doing. That is the usual way. No one files into a church with the intention of committing idolatry before the first hymn. It happens by drift, dressed in the language of preference. *I find him more anointed. I connect better with her teaching. The singing at the other church is simply stronger.* Some of that may even be true. None of it is the point.

Paul met an old version of this in Athens. The city was crowded with shrines, and he said so to its people.

> *“For as I walked around and looked carefully at your objects of worship, I even found an altar with this inscription: TO AN UNKNOWN GOD.”*
> — Acts 17:23 (NIV)

The Athenians were not irreligious. They were connoisseurs. Luke says that "all the Athenians and the foreigners who lived there spent their time doing nothing but talking about and listening to the latest ideas" (Acts 17:21, NIV). They collected gods the way some of us collect programmes and conference badges. When Paul reached the resurrection, the part that required something of them, a number of them sneered and some said they would hear him again another time (Acts 17:32). Curiosity was welcome. A claim on their lives was not.

I don't think Brooklyn in the 1870s and Athens in the first century are the same crowd, and I won't flatten the difference. The Athenians had never heard the gospel. Beecher's hearers had heard it all their lives. But the appetite is recognisable. Religion as a source of interesting experiences, and the preacher as the one who supplies them.

## The Corinthians Had the Same Habit

You don't need Brooklyn or Athens to find it. It is inside the church, in the New Testament, and Paul meets it head on. Writing to the church at Corinth, he reports what Chloe's people had told him, which was that the congregation had split into camps. One said they followed Paul, another Apollos, another Cephas, and another, rather piously, Christ. Then Paul asks, with some irritation, whether Christ has been divided up and whether Paul was crucified for them (1 Corinthians 1:11 to 13).

Notice that nobody in Corinth had been caught in a scandal over this. They liked their teachers. They had favourites, as people do, and the favourites had become badges. A church can be full of gifts and gratitude and still have quietly changed the object of its loyalty. Paul's remedy is to shrink himself and Apollos to servants through whom people believed (1 Corinthians 3:5), which is a harsh thing to say to a church that is proud of its stars.

## What the Father Is Looking For

Jesus meets the same trouble in another form at a well in Samaria. A woman raises what sounds like a theological question and is really a question about addresses. Her people worship on this mountain, she says, and the Jews say that the right place is Jerusalem. She wants to know which building is the proper one.

His answer closes the argument.

> *“Yet a time is coming and has now come when the true worshipers will worship the Father in the Spirit and in truth, for they are the kind of worshipers the Father seeks. God is spirit, and his worshipers must worship in the Spirit and in truth.”*
> — John 4:23 to 24 (NIV)

Look at the verb. The Father seeks. It is the language of someone looking through a crowd for a particular person. God is not hunting for an audience, or for people who admire the choir. He is looking for people who have come for him. And the reason that mountain and city no longer matter is given in the next sentence: God is spirit, which means he cannot be pinned to an address, a building or a famous voice. Worship follows him wherever he is, and he is not more present where the better preacher stands.

C. S. Lewis, who thought more honestly about worship than most, saw how plain this is. Praise, he wrote in *Reflections on the Psalms*, "completes the enjoyment; it is its appointed consummation" (Lewis, as cited in Taylor, n.d.). We praise what we have been enjoying, and the enjoyment is unfinished until we say so. Elsewhere he gave his picture of the best church service. "The perfect church service would be one we were almost unaware of; our attention would have been on God" (Lewis, as cited in Taylor, n.d.). Picture a service where the sermon is good and the singing is fine and you find afterwards that you forgot to notice either. The service has done what it was for.

## What Church Is For

Here I want to head off a wrong turn. Someone will say that if God is spirit and the point is God, then I can worship at home, alone, with my phone and my Bible, and spare myself all this trouble with preachers. Hebrews anticipates that move.

> *“And let us consider how we may spur one another on toward love and good deeds, not giving up meeting together, as some are in the habit of doing, but encouraging one another.”*
> — Hebrews 10:24 to 25 (NIV)

The writer does not say that gathering is a performance to be rated. He says it is a mutual work. We meet so that we can stir one another up, and the habit of staying away is treated as a danger to the people who stay away and to the people who are missing them. Church is a place where other people need you to turn up, and where you are known well enough to be corrected. That is a thing no sermon on a screen can do.

The picture in Acts 2 is of the same kind. After Pentecost the believers "devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching and to fellowship, to the breaking of bread and to prayer" (Acts 2:42, NIV). Luke adds that "every day they continued to meet together in the temple courts" and "broke bread in their homes and ate together with glad and sincere hearts" (Acts 2:46, NIV). Look at what is on that list. Teaching, yes, and the apostles were gifted. But also food, houses, money held loosely, and prayer. A visiting celebrity is not in it.

Read the first scene of Acts 2 with unhurried eyes, too. There is no famous preacher on the programme. There is a room, a group of about one hundred and twenty people (Acts 1:15), most of them unremarkable by the world's accounting, who had been told to wait. They had done the one thing that turned out to matter, which was to stay. What the Spirit did next did not depend on a name on a poster.

The word the New Testament uses for church simply means an assembly, a gathering of the called. Nothing in the word suggests a show. And Hebrews 10 closes the door on the opposite error as firmly as John 4 closes it on this one. If the argument of this essay were only that you should stop caring about preachers, you would be left with a Christianity that floats free of any actual congregation. That would be just as much a mistake.

## When the Gifted Person Falls or Leaves

The trouble with a church built around a face is that faces get tired, fall ill and sometimes fail. Anyone who has attended a large Nigerian convention knows the small change in the hall when the announcer says the guest minister's flight has been delayed. Nobody is angry, but the mood shifts, and you can feel that a good many people had come to meet the man and were not planning to meet anyone else.

I am not describing a particular church, and I'd be unfair to any pastor if I did. I am describing a temptation every large gathering faces. Often the poster for a programme carries the speaker's face larger than anything else on it. We wouldn't dream of a banner that said, "Come, because God is here", because that would not fill the hall, and halls do have to be filled. So little by little, the person who was meant to point at the door becomes the door. When the pastor lands at the airport and a crowd has assembled to meet him, there is real love in it. There is something else beside the love, and if we are honest we know it.

The Psalms saw this long ago, and they were not gentle about it.

> *“Do not put your trust in princes, in human beings, who cannot save. When their spirit departs, they return to the ground; on that very day their plans come to nothing.”*
> — Psalm 146:3 to 4 (NIV)

Beecher himself is a sober warning of what happens to a congregation that rests on one reputation. Theodore Tilton brought a civil case against him in 1874 over an alleged affair with Tilton's wife. The trial began in January 1875, and after six days of deliberation in July the jury could not reach a verdict (Henry Ward Beecher, n.d.). I am not saying he was guilty, and the jury was not able to say so either. My point is smaller. A church that rests on one famous name has put a great deal on the outcome of a case like that. When your worship depends on someone's name, you have handed a stranger the keys to it.

The Christians of North Africa met this question long before the rest of us. In the Donatist dispute that divided Augustine's day, some held that a minister's sin could spoil what he handed on. Augustine, preaching on John's Gospel, would not have it. "And that which was given by Paul, and that which was given by Peter, is Christ's; and if baptism was given by Judas it was Christ's" (Augustine, n.d., Tractate 5). That is a freeing sentence. What we receive in the church belongs to Christ and not to the person who hands it over, so the failure of the person need not be the failure of the faith. It also means we have no business making the person the point.

## Performance in Nigerian Church Culture

This needs saying carefully, because there is a lazy way of saying it that insults people who love God. Nigerian worship is bodily, loud, communal and joyful, and I would not trade a minute of it for a quiet hall where nobody moves. Dancing, shouting and long singing are not performance in the bad sense. They are people using the whole of themselves for the God who made all of them.

But a scholar who studies Nigerian Pentecostalism has used the word performance on purpose. Abimbola Adelakun's *Performing Power in Nigeria* examines how Pentecostal piety is staged in public life, in identity, politics and spectacle (Adelakun, 2021). A study in the journal *HTS Teologiese Studies* by Solomon Enobong, Ezichi Ituma and Favour Uroko goes further and names what it calls a religious celebrity syndrome in the Nigerian church, in which fame, material gain and sensationalism are put ahead of faithful proclamation. They report that it contributes to "spiritual shallowness, moral compromise and a distorted view of Christianity", and they set it against Paul's picture of ministry in 2 Corinthians 4 as a sacrificial service in which the message, and not the messenger, is lifted up (Enobong et al., 2024).

I am not able to say how common this is in any given congregation, and I won't invent a statistic. Most Nigerian churches are small and led by pastors nobody will ever put on a poster. But the pattern is easy to recognise. The master of ceremonies announces the guest with the reverence reserved in other places for royalty. A programme is judged by the size of the crowd. A ministry is built around a personality so completely that the work cannot survive the worker. And when the pastor falls, which some do, being human beings carrying weights no one was built to carry alone, the congregation does not seek God more carefully. It scatters, because the God it was attached to lived at that address and the address is now closed.

There is a further, quieter form of the same trouble that sits in the pew beside real devotion, and it is not unique to Nigeria. You arrive composing your verdict. The worship was too long and the sermon too short. The illustration didn't land. The choir was flat in the bridge. The welcome team looked bored and the generator kicked in during the prayer. Some of it may be fair. But you are sitting in the house of God functioning as a critic of a performance.

## Audience or Actors

The Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard saw this problem more sharply than most, and he used a picture from the theatre. We normally imagine the preacher as the actor and the congregation as the audience. Kierkegaard turned it round. In his *Purity of Heart*, the speaker is the prompter, the listener "is the actor, who in all truth acts before God", and "in the most earnest sense, God is the critical theatergoer" (Kierkegaard, as quoted in Reformed Worship, n.d.). The one who is being watched, in other words, is you. The preacher is there to whisper the lines. God is the one in the seats.

That is a striking reversal, and it is worth carrying into a service. If it is right, then rating the sermon is not just rude. It is a mistake about who is in the audience. A writer in *Reformed Worship* has pushed back on the picture by asking who the host is, since God in worship is not only a spectator but the one who invites us to his table (Reformed Worship, n.d.). I think that is a fair correction. The picture has to be held loosely. But the core of it stands.

John Stott, in *The Living Church*, argued that the church is a community of participants, not a gathering of spectators, and Dallas Willard in *The Divine Conspiracy* argued that worship is something we do for God and not primarily for our own inspiration, though inspiration may follow. I am giving you the substance of their arguments, not their wording. If either is right, the moment the service becomes primarily about our experience of it, we have turned the altar into a mirror.

## A Fair Defence of Wanting a Good Preacher

I should give the other side its due, because it is not a foolish one. Someone will say that wanting a gifted preacher is not idolatry. People who are hungry go where the food is good. Scripture itself honours teaching. If you leave a church where the sermons are thin and go to one where the Bible is opened with care, you have done nothing wrong, and a pastor who says otherwise has probably not noticed how little he has been feeding people. Beecher's crowd, if the story is true, was not wicked. They were hungry, and they knew who fed them.

I agree with all of that. There is nothing wrong with preferring a better meal. Still, there is a difference between choosing a church because its teaching is faithful and moving every few months to wherever the sound and the atmosphere are best, the way a phone user hops between networks for a stronger signal. The first is care for your soul. The second is shopping, and it leaves you nowhere long enough to be known, corrected or needed.

The question the story raises is a different one, and it is about what happens when the meal is taken away. Would you stay? If you did, who would you be staying for? That is the test, and you can run it on yourself without anybody watching. Think of the last time the person you most enjoy hearing was not there. A visiting speaker, a youth leader, a man you didn't know. What did you do with that Sunday? Did you sit through it with folded arms, composing your review for the drive home, or did you find that God was still in the room?

## What Peter Said and What Mary Chose

John 6 gives us a crowd that did leave. Jesus had said something so hard that many of his followers turned back, and he asked the twelve whether they wished to go too. Peter's answer is one of the plainest in the Bible.

> *“Simon Peter answered him, ‘Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life. We have come to believe and to know that you are the Holy One of God.’”*
> — John 6:68 to 69 (NIV)

Peter does not say that the teaching was easy or that the atmosphere was good that week. He had thought about where else he might go and found nowhere. That is what staying for God looks like. It doesn't depend on how the morning went. It is a decision made again after the other options have been weighed.

And there is Mary, in Luke's account, at the feet of Jesus while her sister Martha rushes about with the arrangements. Martha is doing what capable, devoted people do, which is making sure everything works. When she asks Jesus to tell Mary to help, his answer does not scold her service. It redirects her attention. He says that few things are needed, indeed only one, and then: "Mary has chosen what is better, and it will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:42, NIV). A sound system can fail and a preacher can be absent, and the portion is still there. What Mary knew, and what the crowd at Plymouth forgot, if the story is true, is that the preaching, the music and the community are there to bring us to him. When we mistake the scaffolding for the building, we have swapped the means for the end.

## Staying in the Room

If I could speak to one reader, it would be somebody who has begun, quietly, to feel that church is something they judge. You walk in with a scorecard in your head. Some of the marks may even be fair. But a critic and a worshipper can sit in the same chair and sing the same songs, and only one of them goes home having met anyone.

So here is an invitation, and it is a small one. This Sunday, before the service begins, ask yourself one question. If the person I most like to hear were not here, would I still have come, and for whom? Then go in, and see what happens when you aren't keeping score. Greet the person next to you whose name you have never learned. Stay a few minutes after the benediction. If the answer to the question turns out to be uncomfortable, you have learned something useful, and it is better to learn it in a pew than in a crisis.

I have told you a story I cannot prove. What I can say is that the Father is still looking through the crowd, as he was at the well in Samaria, for the people who have come for him. Whether or not anyone ever said it from a pulpit in Brooklyn, the sentence is worth hearing as though it were said to us. Anyone who came for something else may go. The rest of us will stay and worship God.
