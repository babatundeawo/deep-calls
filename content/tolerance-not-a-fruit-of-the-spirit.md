## A Thumbs Up Nobody Quite Meant

Picture a family WhatsApp group on a Sunday afternoon. Somebody's cousin posts a graphic with a sunset on it and a line underneath saying that all religions lead to the same God, and within a minute the thumbs have come in. The aunty who runs the group sends three. A nephew just back from his master's degree abroad adds a clapping emoji. And one man, who goes to church every week and has been taught that Jesus is the only way to the Father, reads the line twice and puts his phone face down on the table.

He doesn't reply. He's been called intolerant before, at a dinner and once on a bus, and he has noticed that the word works like a verdict. You can't appeal it. It arrives sounding kind, and the only answer anyone expects is an apology. So he does what many of us have done. He decides the safest opinion to hold is none at all. He will smile, nod, and keep what he actually believes inside his own head, where it can't offend anybody.

I'd like to speak to that man, because I think he's been handed a false choice. He has been told the Christian options are to approve of everything or to be a bigot, and he has quietly agreed to an arrangement that neither Scripture nor common sense supports. The word at the centre of it is *tolerance*. It deserves a better hearing than it usually gets, from both sides, and the argument has more than two ends.

## What the Word Used to Mean

Words don't announce when they change. They shift the way a river shifts course over decades, and one morning you look up and the water is somewhere else.

The Bible scholar D. A. Carson, in *The Intolerance of Tolerance* (2012), argued that this is exactly what has happened here. In its older sense, tolerance meant putting up with views you thought were wrong. You let someone hold a belief, say it aloud and argue for it, and you kept your own right to say you thought they were mistaken. That was the whole point. There is nothing to tolerate if you already agree. In the newer sense, on Carson's account, the weight has moved from allowing contrary opinions to accepting all of them. In a line quoted by Daniel Snape in his review for *The Pneuma Review*, Carson describes the shift as one "from allowing the free expression of contrary opinions to the acceptance of all opinions" (Carson, 2012, pp. 3 to 4, as quoted in Snape, 2016).

The older word was a serious virtue because it cost something. It meant sitting across from a person whose ideas you thought dangerous or false, and choosing not to crush him. Nobody pays a price for approving of what they already like. The newer word has been promoted from referee to cheerleader, and a cheerleader has no way of saying that anyone is wrong.

You can see the difference in public documents. The UNESCO Declaration of Principles on Tolerance, adopted in Paris on 16 November 1995, begins by saying that "Tolerance is respect, acceptance and appreciation of the rich diversity of our world's cultures," and the sentence goes on from there. In the next clause it adds something I'd like Christians and their critics to read together: "Tolerance is not concession, condescension or indulgence." So even the most official definition of the word keeps a line between respecting a person and agreeing with everything he says.

Nigerians are better placed than most to see why the older meaning matters. Section 38 of our constitution gives every person freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including the freedom to change his religion or belief, and to "manifest and propagate" it in worship, teaching, practice and observance. Look at what that sentence protects. It protects a Muslim's right to call people to Islam and a Christian's right to call people to Christ, and it protects a person's right to say no to both. Nobody in it is required to agree with anybody. They are required to let each other speak, and to live in the same country afterwards. That is the old tolerance written into law, and I would defend it against all comers.

## Where Tolerance Came From

It helps to know that the old tolerance was not invented by people who had no convictions. The most famous defence of it in English is John Locke's *A Letter Concerning Toleration*. Locke wrote it in Latin while in exile in the Dutch Republic in 1685 and 1686, and William Popple published an English translation in 1689 without Locke's knowledge (Wikipedia, n.d.). Locke was a believing Christian, and his argument was in good part a Christian one. Faith, he held, cannot be produced by force, because belief that is only outward obedience is worth nothing to God. The magistrate's sword can move a man's body into a church, and it cannot move his mind to believe. So the state has no business with the saving of souls, and a man who is wrong about God should not lose his property or his life for it.

The secular case for tolerance has a different foundation and it is a strong one. It says that none of us can prove our final beliefs to the satisfaction of everyone else, that history is crowded with people who burned their neighbours because they were sure, and that a society of many faiths and none can only stay at peace if each person agrees to let the others live by conscience. You don't need to believe anything about God to accept that argument. You only need to have read the history of Europe in the century before Locke, or the headlines from our own northern and middle belt communities in the last twenty years. I think it is a good argument, and Christians have good reason to say thank you for it. We have been on the receiving end of persecution far more often than we have been the ones holding the matches.

There is a small irony worth noticing, and I mention it without any satisfaction. Locke himself drew lines. He did not think the magistrate should tolerate a church whose members owed their obedience to a foreign prince, which readers have generally taken to mean the Roman Catholic Church, although scholars still argue about how absolute he meant it to be. And he excluded atheists, on the ground that "Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist" (Locke, 1689, as quoted in Wikipedia, n.d.). The Toleration Act of 1689 likewise left out Catholics and atheists (Wikipedia, n.d.). I don't raise this to score a point against Locke, who was a man of his time and ahead of it. I raise it because it shows something that every honest person eventually admits. Nobody tolerates everything. Every society, and every person, draws a line somewhere, and the only real question is where, and whether we are willing to say so out loud.

That is the question the new meaning of the word hides. A person who says "I tolerate everything" has either not thought it through or has drawn a line she is not telling you about.

## The Best Case for Calling Us Intolerant

Before I say what I think is wrong with the new meaning, I owe it a fair hearing, because the person who holds it is not stupid and often not unkind.

Here is how I think such a person would put it, and I hope she'd say yes, that is my view. You tell me every human being is made in God's image, and I believe you mean it. But then you tell my brother that his relationship is a sin, or my friend that her religion is false, and what I hear underneath the kindness is a ranking. In my experience, "I'm only telling you the truth" has too often been the sentence that comes before a door closing. People have been sent out of family gatherings with it, and out of churches, and in some places out of their lives. And in this country we have buried far too many neighbours who were killed by people perfectly certain that they were right. When I ask you to tolerate everything, I am asking for peace. I'm not trying to destroy your faith. I am trying to get everybody home alive.

I think that is a serious position, and parts of it are simply correct. Christians have used the truth as a weapon, and some of us have enjoyed it. The fear of religious violence in Nigeria is not an invention, and the people who carried it out have not all belonged to one faith. The numbers suggest that the fear sits alongside a good deal of ordinary goodwill and some real suspicion. An Afrobarometer dispatch published in November 2024 found that 83 percent of Nigerians surveyed would like, or would not mind, having a neighbour of a different religion, and 63 percent said freedom of religion should be absolute. Yet only 51 percent said they trusted people of other faiths, and the gap was wide, with 70 percent of Muslims saying they trusted people of other faiths against 37 percent of Christians saying they trusted people of other religions (Afrobarometer, 2024). That last number should make a Christian uncomfortable. A church that answers it with a lecture on logic deserves the contempt it gets.

But the argument has a weak joint in it, and it's the place where peace and agreement are treated as the same thing. They aren't. Peace comes from restraint, and Nigerians have learned over a long and bloody history that it can survive a very great deal of disagreement. It cannot survive one side deciding that the other has no right to hold its view. The new tolerance, by asking for applause, makes the same mistake from the other direction. And it is worth noticing how quickly it turns hard on anyone who won't clap, so that the loudest calls for tolerance sometimes arrive attached to the least patience for a person who says *I disagree*.

## Patience Is a Fruit and Approval Is Not

Paul gives a list in his letter to the Galatians, and it is a good place to check what the Spirit actually produces.

> *"But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness,"*
>
> — Galatians 5:22 (ESV)

The verse runs on into gentleness and self control, and then Paul adds that against such things there is no law (Galatians 5:23). Read down the list, and tolerance isn't on it in either meaning. Patience is. That is a different word, and I think it is closer to what the old tolerance was reaching for. Patience is the capacity to bear with a difficult person for a long time without giving up on him. Paul uses the same idea again when he tells the Colossians what to do with each other.

> *"bearing with one another and, if one has a complaint against another, forgiving each other;"*
>
> — Colossians 3:13 (ESV)

Notice that the verse takes the complaint for granted. Bearing with someone presupposes that something about him is hard to bear, and the apostle doesn't ask us to pretend otherwise. We are asked to stay in the room, to keep forgiving, and to stay patient with the person while going on, when the time comes, to say that something is wrong. Ephesians 4:2 puts patience and bearing with one another in love at the front of the life worthy of the calling, and in 2 Timothy 2:24 to 25 Paul tells Timothy that a servant of the Lord must be kind to everyone, able to teach, and gentle when he corrects opponents. Gentle correction is still correction. The Spirit's fruit doesn't remove the second word from that sentence.

This is more costly than applause. It's easy to affirm a stranger. It's far harder to put up with a difficult uncle for thirty years.

So when a friend tells me that a Christian ought to be tolerant, I'm happy to agree, as long as we're clear which tolerance we mean. If you mean bearing with people, listening to them, defending their right to speak and to worship, sitting at the same table and refusing to treat them as enemies, then yes, and Jesus was more tolerant than most of us. He was accused of eating with sinners, which is exactly what the Pharisees said of him in Luke 15. If you mean approving of every belief and calling every road the same road, then no. That isn't on Paul's list and it isn't love. Love doesn't need me to believe everything you believe. It needs me to stay.

A woman caught in adultery is the clearest case I know. When her accusers had drifted away, Jesus told her that he did not condemn her, and then added that she should go and sin no more (John 8:10 to 11). The first sentence is the patience, and the second is the truth. Take either one out and you no longer have the story.

## Where Jesus Was Not Gentle

It would be dishonest to stop there, because the gospels show Jesus saying things that no public relations adviser would have signed off on. In Matthew 15 the Pharisees complain that his disciples don't wash their hands in the way the elders required, and Jesus answers that they have set aside the commandment of God for the sake of their tradition. The disciples are worried. They come to him afterwards with the kind of nervous question that Nigerian families know very well, the one that begins *Sir, do you realise that they are offended?*

> *"Do you know that the Pharisees were offended when they heard this saying?"*
>
> — Matthew 15:12 (ESV)

He doesn't apologise. He says this.

> *"Let them alone; they are blind guides. And if the blind lead the blind, both will fall into a pit."*
>
> — Matthew 15:14 (ESV)

There's a physician's directness in that, and I don't think it is cruelty. It's what a doctor does when he has read the scan and decides not to talk about the weather. But the offence was never the aim. Jesus did not wake up looking for people to upset, and he spent far more of his time with the broken than with the proud. When his own townsmen could not get past the carpenter's son, he did not water down what he said. In Matthew 11:6 he calls blessed the person who does not stumble over him, which is an honest admission that following him would cost something and that some would not pay. And when he spoke of the towns that had seen his miracles and still refused to repent, he said it would be more bearable for Sodom on the day of judgment than for them (Matthew 11:24). That is the same man who wept over Jerusalem. The offence was what happened when the truth entered a room furnished with comfortable stories.

John the Baptist lost his life for telling a king the truth about his marriage (Mark 6:17 to 18). James was put to the sword by Herod (Acts 12:2). Neither man was killed for rudeness.

## What Paul and John Say About Tolerating

The one place in the New Testament where *tolerate* is used as a charge is worth reading with care, because it's often stretched further than it goes. In the letter to the church at Thyatira, the risen Christ opens with praise. He knows their love, faith, service and patient endurance, and he says their latest works are more than their first (Revelation 2:19). Then comes the complaint.

> *"But I have this against you, that you tolerate that woman Jezebel, who calls herself a prophetess and is teaching and seducing my servants to practice sexual immorality and to eat food sacrificed to idols."*
>
> — Revelation 2:20 (ESV)

That is a rebuke of a church for what it allowed *inside itself*. A teacher within the congregation was leading Christians into immorality and into the idol feasts that went with the trade guilds of a commercial town, and the elders had let her carry on. It isn't a verse about how we should treat our unbelieving neighbours, and nobody should use it as one. What it does show is that the risen Christ can hold a church responsible for its silence, even a church he is praising for its love. A congregation can be so anxious not to give offence that it stops teaching altogether, and that is also a failure of love.

Paul draws the same boundary in 1 Corinthians 5, and he draws it more carefully than his readers often do. The church in Corinth had a member living in a sexual relationship that even the pagans around them found shocking, and the church was proud of its broadmindedness about it. Paul tells them to remove him from their fellowship. But he stops them from taking it further than that. He had earlier told them not to associate with sexually immoral people, and he explains that he did not mean the immoral of this world, for then they would have to leave the world altogether. He meant a professing brother who goes on in open sin. And then comes the sentence that keeps the discipline in its place.

> *"For what have I to do with judging outsiders? Is it not those inside the church whom you are to judge? God judges those outside. “Purge the evil person from among you.”"*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 5:12 to 13 (ESV)

Put the two passages together and a pattern comes up. Toward the outsider, the neighbour, the sceptic, the member of another faith, the church's posture is witness, patience and hospitality, and the judging is left to God. Toward the insider who teaches or practises what the gospel forbids, the church has a duty to say so and, if need be, to act. It is a fierce standard applied inside the house and a gentle one at the gate. Most of the shame in church history comes from reversing them, which is what happens when a church is harsh on the people outside its walls and soft on the people who run it.

Paul's own later letter to Corinth suggests that the discipline was meant to heal. Many commentators think the offender of 1 Corinthians 5 is the person Paul urges them to forgive and comfort in 2 Corinthians 2:5 to 8, lest he be overwhelmed by sorrow, though the identification is debated. If they are right, then even the sharpest word in the New Testament about tolerating sin ends with an instruction to reaffirm love to the sinner. The church was not told to approve of what he had done, and it was not told to throw him away.

## The God Who Is Patient and Holy

Here the argument gets careful, because the truth requires holding two things at once, and the modern mind, trained on clean binaries, finds it hard to carry them in the same hand.

God is opposed to sin. That is not a minor footnote in the biblical story. It is the reason the story exists. The covenants, the law, the prophets, the cross, the resurrection and the coming judgment are the story of a holy God dealing with a creation that has broken his design. His holiness is not a cold abstraction. It is a burning that cannot sit alongside what corrupts.

And yet the same God desires all people to be saved and to come to a knowledge of the truth (1 Timothy 2:3 to 4). Peter explains his apparent slowness in the same spirit. The Lord is not slow in keeping his promise as some count slowness, he says, but is patient toward you, not wishing that any should perish but that all should reach repentance (2 Peter 3:9). The delay of judgment is a gift. It is time given, because he knows what judgment costs, and he would rather save than condemn.

That is why the proper word for what God does with us is patience and not tolerance in the modern sense. He has borne with our idolatry and our pride for a very long time, and with the clever theological inventions that have the shape of worship and the content of self service. But patience has a horizon. A teacher who warns you that the road ends at a cliff is not being unkind by describing the cliff. And a God who holds back his judgment so that people can turn is the least tolerant thing in the universe about sin and the most patient with the sinner.

## Whether Conversion Is Itself Intolerant

Someone will say that I have been making an argument for polite exclusion. Take the converts. If a man turns from the faith of his fathers to Christ, hasn't he declared that his fathers were wrong? Isn't conversion the most intolerant act there is?

I think the honest answer begins by admitting how true that feels from the other side. The earliest Christian converts in Yorubaland did not step away from the ancestral shrines quietly or without cost. To confess Christ in a compound where every elder had poured out offerings to the household gods was a rupture. Families fractured. Inheritances were withheld. The converts knew what they were walking into, and they walked in anyway, and nobody who has watched that happen in a family would call it a polite lifestyle choice.

But look at what the convert is claiming. He is judging his own past, and he is paying for it himself. He is not asking the state to punish his uncle or the market to refuse him. He is saying that he cannot, in conscience, believe what he used to believe, and that he asks his family to let him go. That is precisely the freedom that Locke argued for, and the one that section 38 of our constitution protects. A conversion that costs the convert everything and takes nothing from anyone else is not intolerance as the old word defined it. It is conscience at work.

Samuel Ajayi Crowther is the best Nigerian witness I know to how this can be done. He was taken as a boy of thirteen in 1821 by slave raiders, sold several times, rescued by a British warship, and lived to become, in 1864, the first African bishop of the Church of England (Christian History Institute, n.d.). He had more reason than most to resent the faith of the men who enslaved him, since they were Muslim raiders. Yet his approach to Muslims, as the missiologist Lamin Sanneh and others describe it, was not confrontational. He avoided attacking Islam, he let Scripture speak for itself when Muslims asked him questions, and he gave much of his life to translating the Bible into Yoruba (Fuller Studio, n.d.). He was no relativist. When a chief demanded two hundred slaves for his release, he said he would rather die than see more Africans enslaved, and he called slavery a great abomination in the sight of God (Christian History Institute, n.d.). Here is a man who held the truth tightly and his neighbours gently, and he did both for decades. Missionary politics treated him badly in the end, and that too is part of the record.

## Two Anglican Answers in 2026

If this all sounds like a tidy argument for the classroom, look at what happened in the Anglican Communion this year, because it shows how hard the question is for people who love both truth and one another.

In March 2026, the Global Anglican Future Conference (GAFCON) met in Abuja from the third to the sixth of the month. According to a report by Joseph Maina for Religion Unplugged, 436 delegates from 48 countries attended, and they dissolved the group's Primates' Council and set up a new Global Anglican Council, with Archbishop Laurent Mbanda of Rwanda as its first chair. The movement had announced in October 2025 that it no longer recognised the traditional structures of the Communion as authoritative. Mbanda criticised the appointment of Sarah Mullally as Archbishop of Canterbury, saying that Canterbury had "chosen a leader who will further divide an already split Communion." The wider dispute goes back to the Church of England's decision in 2023 to allow blessings for same sex couples (Maina, 2026).

A few weeks later, on 25 March, Mullally was installed at Canterbury Cathedral, with twenty six Anglican primates in attendance, according to the Episcopal News Service. She said she would work to "break down the walls that divide us" (Episcopal News Service, 2026).

I'm not using this to settle the dispute, and a short essay can't. What I notice is that both sides believe they are acting from love. As I understand the Abuja position, a communion that cannot say what Scripture teaches is not united in anything worth the name, and walking apart is the honest alternative to pretending. As I understand the Canterbury position, walking apart is itself a failure to bear with one another, and a family that leaves the table has given up on the one thing it was called to do. Both can quote Paul. Both have been hurt by the other. And a slogan about tolerance helps neither, because each side is using the word to mean something the other would not recognise.

What helps, I think, is a question that comes before the doctrine, and it is the question the rest of this essay is about. Whatever you believe is true, how do you say it to a person you actually love?

## What Truth Costs the One Who Tells It

This is where the usual argument turns. We have been asking whether Christians are allowed to say hard things, and the honest answer is yes, they must. The better question is whether we've earned the right to be believed.

Think about what we trust in ordinary life. If a doctor knows the growth is malignant and says nothing, we call it negligence. If a friend knows the bridge is out and waves you cheerfully towards it, we call it betrayal. Nobody calls either of them tolerant. And yet if a stranger leans out of a passing car to shout that you are going to hell, we don't call him loving either. We call him loud. What separates the doctor from the shouter is not the content of the message. It's that one of them has been in the room with you, has read your file, and will still be there tomorrow.

The proverb has it exactly.

> *"Faithful are the wounds of a friend; profuse are the kisses of an enemy."*
>
> — Proverbs 27:6 (ESV)

The wound is faithful because it comes from a friend. Take the friendship away and the same words are just a wound. Take the wound away and what remains is a very large number of kisses, and they come from an enemy, the writer says, because someone who will only ever flatter you has stopped caring where you end up. The new tolerance is a great deal of kissing. The verdict shouted from a passing car is a great deal of wounding. Neither is what Paul had in mind when he told the Ephesians what a grown up church does.

> *"Rather, speaking the truth in love, we are to grow up in every way into him who is the head, into Christ,"*
>
> — Ephesians 4:15 (ESV)

The phrase is not *speaking truth, and also being nice*. It is truth spoken in love, and for that you have to be there. You have to know the person's name and their children's names and where the pain is. You have to be willing to drive to the hospital. That is why I distrust both the church that will say nothing to anyone and the Christian who treats every conversation as a chance to score.

Stories of a certain kind get told in churches, and I'd treat them as scenes and not as records, because I cannot trace this one to a source. Picture a young man whose car breaks down at night. A teenager stops, gives him a lift, and says within the first minute that if he isn't a Christian he is going to hell. The young man nearly asks to be dropped at the roadside, but it is a long walk in the dark, so he stays, and the teenager keeps talking about Jesus until they arrive. Five years later he comes to faith, and he says that the blunt question was what stayed with him, because he knew in some corner of himself that it was true.

If that happened, I'm glad for him. But I would be careful what I learn from it. For every young man who is eventually grateful to the stranger in the car, there must be many more who were simply driven away and never came back, and we never hear their side. Truth does sometimes arrive without manners and still find its mark, and Paul does say that the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing (1 Corinthians 1:18). The cross will always offend somebody. That is no reason to add offence of our own. It would be a strange policy to plan for the lucky case.

## The Sentence Nobody Can Soften

None of this lets us off the hook about the thing that makes the world most uncomfortable with us. In the upper room, the night before his death, Jesus said this.

> *"I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me."*
>
> — John 14:6 (ESV)

There is no softer reading of this that does justice to the words. It doesn't say that Jesus is one good teacher among several, or that every road leads up the same mountain. It says that he is the way. I'd rather say that plainly than offer a gentler version I'd have to defend later to my own conscience. The popular picture of many paths up one mountain is a fair picture of how many people hope the world works. But the person who offers it is describing the view from the summit, which no one on any path has reached. Jesus claims to have come down from there.

But look at what sort of claim it is. It is a claim about a person. It isn't a ranking of cultures or a verdict on anybody's grandmother. A Muslim neighbour who holds that the final revelation came through the Prophet is making an exclusive claim as well, and I would never ask him to stop, because that is what it is to hold a faith and not a hobby. What I'd ask of both of us is what the constitution asks, that we let each other make the claim aloud, listen to the reasons, and go on being neighbours. The Christian who believes John 14:6 has no need to hate anyone, or to hurry anyone. If anything, the claim tells him he is in no position to take the credit, because he didn't find the door. He was shown it, and shown it by someone who sat with him.

The church, Paul tells Timothy, is the pillar and foundation of the truth (1 Timothy 3:15). A pillar holds something up. It doesn't throw it at passers by.

## Love and Truth Do Not Compete

Here is where the floor shifts. We have spent a long time on whether Christians should be tolerant or intolerant, and I think the question was framed badly from the beginning. The real question is what it means to love a person.

You can love a person warmly, sacrificially, and for decades, while believing that what she believes is wrong. You can treat every human being as made in the image of God, and mean it with your whole heart, while refusing to pretend that every belief system leads to the same place. These are not contradictions. They are the two hands of an honest witness, held together in the same person, at the same time, toward the same people.

What we can't do is choose between love and truth as though they were rivals. Separate them and you get, on one side, a loveless truth that beats people with a Bible, and on the other, a truthless love that holds people's hands as they walk towards a cliff. The first has lost its Saviour and the second has lost its message. Locke was right that no one can be forced to believe. He was right, too, that a man who is not forced to believe is entitled to be told what we think is true, kindly and without a threat behind it. That is the one weapon the church was given, and it works by persuasion.

## A Word to the Man With His Phone Face Down

Go back to the family group chat. The thumbs are still coming in, and the man at the table has been staring at the screen for a while.

I'd like to suggest that he has more options than a thumbs up or a sermon. He could write something short and warm, the way you'd talk to a person you've known all your life. He might say that he doesn't agree that all religions say the same thing, that he'd love to talk about it over a meal, and that he isn't going anywhere. Or he might say nothing in the group and ring the cousin afterwards. What he shouldn't do is bury the conviction for the sake of a quiet Sunday, because the people in that chat are the very ones to whom he owes it.

You may be this man. If you've been called intolerant one time too many, I hope you can hear that the discomfort you feel isn't a defect in your faith. Some of it is the ordinary friction of holding a belief in company that doesn't hold it. Don't go looking for offence, and don't be afraid of it either. Be the kind of person who is patient with people for decades, who can be trusted with their secrets and their grief, and who can therefore be heard on the day you finally say the hard thing.

Say it slowly. Say it to someone you'd be willing to sit up with all night. And if you can't think of a person you'd do that for, then start there, because you have found the first thing the truth needs from you.
