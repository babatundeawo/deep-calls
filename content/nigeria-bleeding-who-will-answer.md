## What Happened at Yawota on 15 May

Picture a Friday morning in Yawota, in Oriire Local Government Area of Oyo State. Exercise books are open on the desks. A teacher is in the middle of a sentence. Then comes the sound of motorcycles, and gunfire, and men walking into classrooms as if they had the timetable.

That was 15 May 2026. Gunmen struck schools in Yawota and in a neighbouring community, among them Baptist Nursery and Primary School, Community Grammar School and L.A. Primary School. Forty six people were taken into the forest. The figure reported most often is thirty nine pupils and seven teachers, one of them a principal, and the Christian Association of Nigeria in Oyo described the captives as mostly children between two and sixteen years old. Early counts from the state shifted for days, which is its own kind of sorrow, because a parent standing outside a school gate does not need a revised number. She needs a name.

Two teachers did not come home. They were Michael Oyedokun and Deacon John Olaleye. A prosecution witness has told the Federal High Court in Abuja that both were beheaded by their captors, and that other captives later recovered their bodies. I will say no more about how they died. They were somebody's colleague, somebody's husband, somebody's deacon on Sunday. There is a way of writing about Nigeria's violence that turns people into evidence, and I would like to resist it.

It is now nearly six months since that morning and almost four since the children were freed. The headlines have moved on. That is exactly the point at which a country usually looks away, and it's the point at which this essay wants to look harder. The argument comes in three parts. First, what the facts show, as far as they can be checked. Second, what Scripture says to a people in this condition, which is more than most of us have been told. Third, what the church in particular owes the bleeding, beyond a vigil and a hashtag.

## Fifty Six Days of Not Knowing

For fifty six days nobody outside the forest could say for certain who was alive. Government said little, and some of what it said was contradicted by families. A federal delegation brought rice and cash to Yawota in the last days of May, and, as reported at the time, the women there turned it down. They had not asked for relief packages. They had asked for their children.

The Nigeria Union of Teachers in Oyo shut public schools from 1 June, and teachers marched in Lagos. On 4 June the United Nations humanitarian coordinator in Nigeria publicly called for the captives' release. The union suspended its strike on 1 July, with the state promising a joint security task force for vulnerable schools, counselling for the victims and payment of entitlements to the families of the dead teachers. I had forgotten, until I went back to the dates, that the strike ended while the children were still missing. Teachers went back to their classrooms for nine more days with their colleagues in captivity.

On 10 July the presidency announced that all the pupils and teachers had been rescued. Spokesman Bayo Onanuga said that eight members of the gang were in the custody of the Department of State Services and that some attackers had been "neutralised". He added, "There was no quid pro quo in the rescue," which was the government's way of saying nobody had been exchanged for the children. A fuller account was promised. As far as I could find, it has still not been published in any detail, so how the children came out remains largely a story told in the government's own words.

I'm not accusing the government of lying. I am saying that nearly four months on, the public knows who has been charged but still has no independent account of the last days. A mother is entitled to that account, and so is a country that is expected to believe its own security services.

## What the Courts Have Heard Since

By 20 July the federal government had named three suspects, all from Suleja in Niger State, and charged them at the Federal High Court in Abuja under the Terrorism (Prevention and Prohibition) Act, 2022. Reporting on 23 July said that Justice Salim Ibrahim had convicted the three and sentenced them to life imprisonment on the counts to which they had pleaded guilty, with the remaining counts adjourned. They continued to deny others.

In September the Department of State Services brought an amended charge against five men, again alleged to be members of Ansaru, a group the Nigerian state has proscribed as terrorist. On 17 September all five pleaded not guilty. Two of them are named in court reporting as Mahmud Usman Muhammad, described as a self proclaimed emir of the group, and Abubakar Abbas, described as his deputy. At the hearing of 12 October a prosecution witness, identified only as BBB, told the court that the attack was meant to pressure the federal government into freeing detained Ansaru leaders, and that the group had submitted a list of thirty names. He said the kidnappers threatened to kill their captives if the demand was not met, and that two were killed before the rescue.

These are allegations until a judge rules, and the men are owed the presumption of innocence that any of us would demand for our own sons. But notice what the testimony describes. It doesn't describe a ransom raid that got out of hand. It describes a hostage taking by an armed organisation to force a state to release its commanders. The state that was being bargained with had let a small armed group hold forty six citizens in a forest within a state's own territory for eight weeks.

## An Industry That Prices Children

Oriire felt like an exception because it happened in the south west, within reach of Ibadan and Lagos. The figures say it was nothing of the kind.

In August 2026 SBM Intelligence, a Lagos based research consultancy, published its latest count of kidnapping in Nigeria in a report called *The Capture of Nigeria's Kidnap Economy*, covering July 2025 to June 2026. It recorded 7,825 people kidnapped, up from 4,722 the year before, a rise of about 66 percent. At least 1,142 people were killed, against 762 a year earlier. Kidnappers demanded 22.86 billion naira, well below the 48 billion of the previous period, but at least 7.78 billion was paid, compared with about 2.56 billion before. In plain terms, the gangs asked for less and collected three times as much. The report says one in three naira demanded was recovered, where in the earlier year it was one in twenty. It also found that in at least six incidents, victims were killed after the ransom had been paid.

I read that as a market that works. People pay because the alternative is a funeral, whatever the law says, and each payment funds the next raid. A 2022 law forbids paying ransom, and the people who wrote it never had to sit through a night when their own child was in a forest. No argument from principle survives that night, and I wouldn't ask any parent to try. But the arithmetic is cruel. The payments that families scrape together are the working capital of the next abduction.

The prophet Micah described a certain kind of wickedness that will be familiar to anyone who reads those figures. He spoke of people who plan harm on their beds and carry it out when the morning dawns, because it is in the power of their hand, and who seize fields and houses and oppress a man and his household (Micah 2:1 to 2). He was writing about landowners in eighth century Judah, but his eye for the premeditated, the calculated, the done because it can be done, fits a camp with a burner phone and a spreadsheet of what each family can pay.

Augustine saw the same thing in a very different empire. He asked what kingdoms are once justice is taken away, and answered that they are great robberies, which is to say bands of robbers with a larger territory. He did not mean it as a joke. He meant that a state that cannot or will not do justice has lost the one thing that distinguished it from the gang.

## Who Is Bleeding, and How It Is Counted

The most common thing I hear when I raise this subject among Christians is that this is a war on us, and anyone who says otherwise is being polite at the expense of our children. I want to state that view in its strongest form, because it is not a stupid view and it is not held only by the uninformed.

It runs like this. Open Doors, which publishes the World Watch List each year, reported in January 2026 that Nigeria accounted for 3,490 of the 4,849 Christians it counted as killed for their faith worldwide in the year to September 2025. Its Nigeria lead, speaking to Catholic News Agency, said the government is reluctant to name the religious dimension because it fears being designated a country of particular concern by the United States. A separate study by the Observatory for Religious Freedom in Africa found Christians 2.7 times more likely than Muslims to be targeted and killed in attacks attributed to Fulani militants. Add to that the church attacks, the abducted worshippers in Kaduna State, the priests taken for ransom, and a demand reported at the time of Oriire that Sharia be imposed on a southern state, and the pattern looks to a Christian parent like exactly what he says it is.

Part of this is true and must not be waved away. Christians have been killed, in numbers that should have shamed the government into a different posture years ago. Churches have been attacked and worshippers have been taken from them. Open Doors counts persecution and not total violence, and its figures cannot be checked against a public register, but nobody who has buried a pastor needs a spreadsheet to know that something is being done to the church.

Now the rest of the picture. SBM counted 7,334 of its 7,825 kidnap victims in the north, which is 93.7 percent, and 491 in the south. Its largest totals were in Zamfara with 1,921, then Sokoto with 1,157 and Kaduna with 814. The report does not record religion, so I will not pretend it does. But Zamfara and Sokoto are states where most people are Muslim, and they have bled more than anywhere. On 21 August 2026 gunmen entered mosques in Borgu, Niger State, during Friday prayers and took worshippers. Reports of how many were taken ranged from a little above sixty, according to the local government chairman, to an estimate of six hundred in Reuters reporting; the chairman said about thirty were killed, and the president ordered a rescue. I could not find a confirmed account of how it ended. In February, at Woro and Nuku in Kwara State, mostly Muslim farming villages, officials said at least 162 people were killed, and survivors told the Associated Press that the gunmen entered the mosque, issued the call to prayer and shot those who came. The governor said the attack was probably a response to counterterrorism operations, and officials said the villages were targeted for refusing extremist ideology. Villagers said the toll was nearer two hundred.

I take two things from that. One is that the violence has no single religious address, and that anyone who tells you it is only Christians dying, or only Muslims, is selling a story. The other is that a Christian is not required to choose between those facts. The Catholic Bishops' Conference of Nigeria managed it in August. In a statement reported by The Tablet on 29 August, the bishops pledged what they called "unyielding solidarity with their Muslim compatriots" after the mosque abduction, and said that "no political, ethnic or religious cause can ever justify such cruelty." They also told the kidnappers, citing Galatians, that God cannot be mocked. That is what a church sounds like when it takes its dead seriously and everyone else's dead seriously too.

The Sharia demand deserves the same care. It was reported in early June, at the time of the abduction, that the kidnappers wanted a ransom, vehicles, the release of associates and the implementation of Sharia in Oyo State. The claim was disputed at once, and the prosecution testimony I have read describes a demand for the release of thirty named Ansaru members and says nothing of Sharia. So it is reported, it is disputed, and it has not been tested in court. If it was made, then armed men who use the language of a faith to frighten a Christian town have done something wicked, and a Christian is allowed to say so. Those charged are accused of belonging to a proscribed terrorist organisation, which is a different thing from being a Muslim programme for Oyo, and the overwhelming majority of the north's Muslims are among the victims. Justice needs precision here. Generalisation in either direction is its own kind of injustice.

## Why the Killing Keeps Going

There is a reason this crisis has spread instead of shrinking, and it is not simply that Nigeria lacks soldiers. The country has one of the larger militaries in Africa. The failure sits somewhere between capacity and will. I think it has three faces, and the honest thing is to name them without turning any of them into a slur.

The first is a pool of young men with nothing. Across the north there are boys who grow up in informal religious schools run by teachers with no state support, then reach adulthood without a trade, land or a stake in any institution. In their healthiest form such schools have produced scholars. In their most neglected form they have produced idle, humiliated young men who are easy to recruit. Most of those boys never touch a gun. I have not found a study that counts how many recruits come from that background, so I will not give a number. But the Crisis Group's 2020 account of the north west describes the same raw material in the same places, a weakly regulated gold sector, forests that became hideouts after forestry protection lapsed, porous borders and a flood of small arms, and the jihadist groups moving into the security vacuum that all of that left.

The second is the long conflict between herders and farmers across the north central belt. The Crisis Group traced it in 2017 to drought and desertification pushing herders south, to the loss of most of the grazing reserves set up in the 1960s, to easy access to automatic weapons, and to the breakdown of traditional mediation and the impunity that followed. It is a conflict with real roots in land and water, and it has been allowed to curdle into something far worse because no government has invested seriously in grazing reform, ranching, or the even handed policing that would persuade both farmers and herders that the state, not their own militias, will protect them. The same report found no solid evidence for the claim, repeated in many Christian communities, that a president deliberately held back because he was himself Fulani, though it warned that the belief deepens distrust. The Sultan of Sokoto has said herders who kill should be prosecuted as criminals. I wish more of our pastors would quote him.

The third face is the state itself. Semafor reported in August that ACLED's analyst called the second quarter of 2026 the most violent in Nigeria since its records began in 1997, with attacks on civilians by insurgents and bandits up about thirty percent. The Institute for Security Studies' researcher told the same reporter that there is no evidence insecurity is substantially easing despite military pressure. Armed men move between roles, and the line between community defender and bandit is thin. And when you ask who is held to account, the answer is often silence. The Catholic Secretariat of Nigeria called the killing, in language I find hard to improve, "a massacre allowed by silence," and asked for the prosecution of terror sponsors and enablers, not only foot soldiers.

Ezekiel would have understood the diagnosis. God accuses a whole society in the twenty second chapter of his book. Her princes are like wolves tearing the prey, shedding blood for dishonest gain. Her priests do violence to the law. Her prophets whitewash it with false visions and say "Thus says the Lord" when the Lord has said nothing. The ordinary people practise extortion and oppress the poor. Every layer is implicated, the officials, the clergy and the crowd. Then comes the line that has to be read slowly.

> *"And I sought for a man among them, that should make up the hedge, and stand in the gap before me for the land, that I should not destroy it: but I found none."*
>
> — Ezekiel 22:30 (KJV)

The gap is not a theoretical gap. It is the place where a wall has fallen and the enemy walks in. God was looking for someone to stand in it, and Ezekiel's own people had been too compromised, too frightened or too comfortable to do so. Isaiah has a parallel complaint. He says that truth is fallen in the street and equity cannot enter, and that the LORD saw it and it displeased him that there was no judgment (Isaiah 59:14 to 15, KJV). Then he says that God saw there was no one to intercede, and acted himself. A people can be so far gone that God has to do what they should have done. The Bible treats that as a rebuke and not as a comfort.

## What Lament Is For

I have read many Nigerian responses to bloodshed that skip straight from the news to the sermon. The prayer that follows a massacre is often a prayer about what God will do to the wicked, delivered in a voice that has no room for what the speaker feels. Scripture has a form for that feeling, and we have largely stopped using it.

The form is lament, and the Psalms are full of it. Psalm 13 begins with a question put to God and not about him.

> *"How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily? how long shall mine enemy be exalted over me?"*
>
> — Psalm 13:1 to 2 (KJV)

Notice what the psalmist does. He does not deny that God is good, and he does not pretend the enemy is small. He accuses God of absence, loudly, to God's face, and the book leaves it in the canon. Habakkuk does the same.

> *"O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear! even cry out unto thee of violence, and thou wilt not save!"*
>
> — Habakkuk 1:2 (KJV)

God's answer to Habakkuk is not rescue on the prophet's schedule. It is a longer, harder word about justice unfolding in God's time, and Habakkuk has to learn to wait while wrong still looks like it is winning. That isn't a sermon I'd preach fast, and I wouldn't preach it to anyone still waiting for news of a child. But it is honest. The Bible does not hand the sufferer a quick answer, and it does not tell him he was wrong to ask.

The same cry goes up from the souls under the altar in Revelation, who call out, in the King James wording, "How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge" (Revelation 6:10). It goes back further, to Genesis 4:10, where God tells Cain that his brother's blood is crying out from the ground. The Nigerian bishops cited that verse in August, and I think they were right to. Blood is not silent in Scripture. It has a voice, and God hears it.

Walter Brueggemann argued in an influential essay, "The Costly Loss of Lament", that when a worshipping community stops lamenting, it does not become more faithful, it becomes more compliant. A church that may only praise has no way to bring its grief into the open, and so no way to say to the powerful that something is wrong. Soong Chan Rah made a related case in *Prophetic Lament*, that lament is a form of protest that refuses to accept suffering as normal. And the Ugandan Catholic theologian Emmanuel Katongole, writing out of the Great Lakes region of Africa, argues in *Born from Lament* that hope amid cycles of violence is best understood through lament, as an arguing and wrestling with God that mourns, protests and appeals all at once. I find his book useful because it describes a church that mourns in public and then goes to work.

Which raises the objection I most want to take seriously. Some will say that lament is only passivity in a nicer outfit, a way for the church to feel something while changing nothing. It can be. A lament that never leaves the prayer meeting is the very thing the bishops meant by silence. But the pattern of the psalms is that the lament turns. Psalm 13 ends with a person saying that he has trusted in God's mercy and will sing, not because anything on the ground has changed, but because he has brought it to the One who can change it. Jesus himself wept at a graveside before he raised a man from it, and Matthew set Rachel's weeping beside the slaughter of Bethlehem's children (Matthew 2:18, quoting Jeremiah 31:15), which tells you that God's own people have been here before. Romans 12:15 puts the instruction in one line.

> *"Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep."*
>
> — Romans 12:15 (ESV)

Weeping is a duty before it is a mood. The Nigerian church has too often reversed it, rejoicing at the next building project while the villages bury their dead. We don't need less hope. We need hope that has first been honest.

## What the Church Owes the Bleeding

The Nigerian church has not been a passive witness to any of this. Congregations pray, raise money for the displaced, and quietly pay to get their own people back, which they rarely talk about in public. Priests have been kidnapped alongside everyone else, and Bishop Bulus Yohanna of Kontagora, whose diocese includes the Catholic school at Papiri from which 315 students and staff were reported taken in November 2025, has asked for a military base in the region. The Catholic Secretariat has demanded redeployment of security forces to the frontlines, prosecution of those who finance terror, compensation and psychological care for victims. None of this is small.

But vigils are not a strategy, and I doubt most pastors think they are. The wisest of them would say a church that wants to be believed should be doing more than praying with its eyes shut. Proverbs 31:8 to 9 is the text I would put on the door of every church office in the country.

> *"Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute. Open your mouth, judge righteously, defend the rights of the poor and needy."*
>
> — Proverbs 31:8 to 9 (ESV)

Those verses are addressed to a king, in the mouth of his mother. They assume that the person with a platform will use it for the person without one. Almost everyone reading this has a platform of some size. A pulpit, a WhatsApp group of four hundred, a seat on a parish council, a vote, a salary.

Nehemiah gives the practical picture. He rebuilt Jerusalem's wall while enemies were plotting against the builders, and the account is more practical than most of us remember. Those who carried burdens were loaded in such a way that each worked with one hand and held a weapon in the other (Nehemiah 4:17). He did not stop building to fight, and he did not stop praying to organise. His prayer in the first chapter and his building in the third are one project. I don't want to turn that into a licence for every church to arm a militia. Vigilantes without accountability become one more armed group, and the Crisis Group's account of ethnic profiling by vigilantes in the north west shows how quickly protectors turn into persecutors. The point is the pairing. Prayer without work is exposed, and work without prayer is lonely.

In practice it might look ordinary. Church leaders can press local and state governments to publish what happens after a school attack, as the Oriire families are still waiting to learn. Dioceses and denominations can share what they know about threats as readily as they share news of missions. Pastors in the south can visit pastors and imams in the north, and listen to what a mother in Zamfara says about the last two years before saying a word about their own. Congregations can fund the counselling the Oyo government promised the rescued children. And every citizen can keep asking, in the church car park and at the ballot box, why a gang on motorcycles was able to run a school abduction for fifty six days.

And we must keep the grief honest in both directions. The churches that wept for Oriire should weep for Woro and for Dekera, for the same reason. If our sorrow only has room for the victims who look like us, then it is not yet Christian sorrow, and it will not persuade a single sceptical neighbour that we believe the image of God is in every person.

## The Question Isaiah Heard

Isaiah's vision in the temple ends with a question put to no one in particular.

> *"Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?"*
>
> — Isaiah 6:8 (ESV)

Isaiah answered, "Here I am! Send me." He did not know the cost. The teachers of Yawota did not know theirs on the morning they went in to teach children of two the alphabet. Two of them paid, and almost six months after that morning a court in Abuja is still hearing about it.

Pass over the grand words, then. I have a smaller request. Find out which school near you has had a security scare, and who is supposed to protect it. Ask your own pastor what your church will do for the next family that goes through this, so the answer does not have to be invented at three in the morning. Give to a fund that supports a teacher's family or a child's counselling, and check that the money arrives. When the next headline comes, pray, and then say something, to someone who has the power to act.

Which of those could you do before Sunday?
