## Two coursemates and one long argument

Here is a scene, built to be recognisable and not reported as fact. Two final year students share a table in a library at the University of Ibadan, a week before examinations. Tolu is a Christian who has sung about the blood of Jesus at six in the morning since he was small. Zainab is a Muslim who has read the Quran through more times than Tolu has read Romans. She isn't trying to win anything. She simply asks him why Christians make God so complicated. One God is easy to understand, she says, and you people have three.

Tolu does what many of us would do. He rebukes the question. He says something about the devil confusing her mind, and the conversation, which could have been the best one of their four years, ends at the photocopier.

I want to say the plain thing first. You cannot rebuke an argument. You can only answer it, and you cannot answer it until you can state it in words that the person who holds it would nod at. That is the work of this essay. Muslims in Nigeria are our classmates, colleagues, landlords, in laws and customers, and across many northern towns they are the large majority. A faith that is only comfortable arguing with people who already agree with it is not very confident.

So I will do two things at every turn. I will give the Muslim case at its best, using the Quran and the hadith (the reported sayings and actions of Muhammad) as Muslims themselves cite them. Then I will answer it in full, and I will say where I think the Christian answer is weaker than we usually pretend.

## What a Muslim friend would put on the table

Islam presents itself less as a rival to Christianity than as the correction of it. The message, a Muslim will say, was given to every prophet, including Moses and Jesus, and then blurred by later hands. The Quran is the final and unblurred form of it.

From that starting point come the claims. God is one, undivided and unshared; the word for this is tawhid. The Quran says the earlier scriptures, the Torah and the Injil (which Muslims understand as the revelation God gave to Jesus), have been corrupted, or at least misread, and the Quran has been kept safe. Jesus was a great prophet and the Messiah, but he was not crucified, and he was not God's Son. Every believer has direct access to God, with no priest between. Each person answers for his own deeds and nobody carries another's load. Islam gives a whole way of life, from prayer to business ethics to inheritance, and five daily prayers give the day a spine. Muhammad is the last prophet, so nothing can come after him. And the worldwide Muslim community, the ummah, is a family that no border can cut.

Those are not weak claims. Nobody who has watched a whole mosque go silent and stand shoulder to shoulder, rich man next to poor man, can think they are weak.

One more thing belongs here before the answers. Nigeria does not fully know its own religious numbers. The last census to measure religion was in 1973 and its results were never published, so every figure you hear is an estimate. Pew's latest, dated 2020, puts Muslims at 56.1 per cent of the population and Christians at 43.4 per cent, with other groups at 0.6 per cent (Pew Research Center, 2025). Neither of us is going away, and neither of us is a small minority.

## One God and the love before creation

The first claim is the oldest and, to a Muslim, the simplest. The short chapter of the Quran that Muslims recite more than almost any other says it in four lines.

> *"Say, "He is Allah, [who is] One, Allah, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, Nor is there to Him any equivalent.""*
>
> — Quran 112:1 to 4 (Saheeh International)

I want to honour what is going on in those lines. They guard against a real danger, which is that people make gods in their own image and then fight over them. Christians have done exactly that. A Muslim who reads the Gospel of John for the first time and hears that God has a Son may well hear an echo of the old pagan stories of gods who fathered children. The Quran says to Christians that the Messiah was "but a messenger of Allah" and then tells them, "do not say, 'Three'" (Quran 4:171, Saheeh International). Elsewhere it says those who say Allah is "the third of three" have disbelieved (Quran 5:73), and it pictures God asking Jesus whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods (Quran 5:116). That is a protest, and the protest has a history behind it.

Now the Christian answer, stated in the form Christians have confessed since the councils of Nicaea in 325 and Constantinople in 381. The Bible agrees with the Quran on the starting line. Moses told Israel, "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4, ESV), and Jesus himself quoted it as the most important commandment (Mark 12:29). No Christian creed teaches three gods. The claim is that the one God has always existed as three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, who share one being and one will, and that the Son was not made at some point in time but is the Word who was with God from the beginning. Nobody on the Christian side means that God took a wife and had a child. A Muslim is entitled to hear us say that plainly, and the Quran's picture of the Trinity as God, Mary and Jesus (5:116) is not what Nicaea confessed. Christians can say to Muslims, with respect, that the Trinity the Quran rejects is a doctrine we reject too.

What a Christian will not do is stop there, because the New Testament says more about Jesus than that he was a messenger. John opens, "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1, ESV). Jesus said, "I and the Father are one" (John 10:30, ESV), and his hearers understood him well enough to pick up stones. He said, "Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58, ESV). And when Thomas saw him alive after the cross he said, "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28, ESV), and was not corrected. A Muslim reads those lines as later additions or as misunderstandings. I would only ask that the question be put honestly, because the first Christians were Jews who recited the oneness of God every day and still prayed to Jesus. They did not find those two things in conflict. They found they had to widen their idea of what the one God is like.

The usual argument for why God is like that runs through love. If God is love, and the apostle John says he is, then love was there before there was anyone to love.

> *"Whoever does not love does not know God, because God is love."*
>
> — 1 John 4:8 (NIV)

The Quran also speaks of God as loving; it calls him "the Forgiving, the Affectionate" (Quran 85:14, Saheeh International), and al Wadud is among the names Muslims recite. So before creation, who was God loving? Either his love had no object, which makes it love of himself alone, or he needed creation in order to love, which makes him depend on what he made. The Trinity says there was always someone to love, because God has never been alone.

I don't think that argument is a proof, and I would be a poor guide if I said it was. A thoughtful Muslim has a good reply. Love, like knowledge and power, is an attribute that belongs to God in himself whether or not any creature exists to receive it, and there is no need to make God a family to say so. A Christian can't refute that in one move. What I can say is that the doctrine did not arrive as a puzzle to be solved. It arrived because the first disciples met a man they could not describe as only a man and could not stop worshipping, and they kept believing in one God throughout. The argument from love is not the reason Christians believe in the Trinity. It is the reason we find it fitting.

The Muslim reader should also notice something in his own book. The Quran gives Jesus a position that it gives nobody else. He is born of a virgin. He is called the Messiah, a word from God, and a spirit from him. He speaks from the cradle, and the Quran has him say, "peace is on me the day I was born and the day I will die and the day I am raised alive" (Quran 19:33, Saheeh International). The hadith add that when every child is born Satan touches it and it cries, "except Mary and her child" (Sahih Bukhari 3431). Muslims will say none of this makes him God, and they are right that the Quran says so (5:72, 5:75, 19:35). I am only observing that if Jesus were merely one prophet among many, Islam's own sources treat him very oddly.

This is an old conversation, and it has been conducted well. In the year 781 the patriarch Timothy I of the Church of the East sat before the caliph al Mahdi in Baghdad and answered his questions about Christ's sonship, the Trinity and Muhammad one at a time. The account that survives describes a courteous exchange in which the caliph's questions were pointed and the patriarch's answers were patient and rooted in Scripture (Ministering to Muslims, n.d.). Eleven centuries later, a Nigerian Christian over a plate of rice has no excuse to do worse.

## What the Quran says about the Gospel

The second claim is the one many Christians first meet, and it comes with feeling. The Injil has been corrupted, says the Muslim, so you cannot test the Quran against it.

Let me state this the way a careful Muslim would. The Injil is not the four Gospels. It is the revelation God gave to Jesus, which has been lost or buried under later writings about him. The Gospels are biographies written by his followers, and they were edited and rearranged. The word that Muslim scholars use for the charge is tahrif, alteration, and they have not always agreed on what kind of alteration is meant. A Muslim research body summarises the classical positions. Al Bukhari held that no one changed the wording of any of the books of Allah, and that people distorted them by misinterpreting them. Others argued that some of the wording itself was changed, and Ibn Taymiyyah favoured a moderate version of that view, in which a few phrases were altered but most of the text remained as revealed (Dorar al Saniyyah, n.d.). So the Muslim position is itself a range of views.

The Christian answer begins inside the Quran, because the Quran is awkward for the corruption claim. It says God sent Jesus "confirming that which came before him in the Torah," and it commands, "let the People of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed therein" (Quran 5:46 to 47, Saheeh International). It tells the People of the Scripture that they are "standing on nothing" until they uphold the Torah and the Gospel (Quran 5:68). It even tells Muhammad that if he is in doubt about what was revealed to him he should ask those who have been reading the earlier scripture (Quran 10:94), and it says, "None can alter His words" (Quran 6:115, Saheeh International). Think about what those verses assume. The Quran was recited in the seventh century. It sends its hearers to a Gospel which Christians then possessed and read. If that Gospel had already been corrupted by then, the command makes no sense. If it was corrupted after the Quran, then the manuscripts we have from before Islam would look different from those we have now, and they don't. Codex Sinaiticus, which contains the earliest complete copy of the New Testament, dates from the middle of the fourth century (University of Birmingham, n.d.), about two and a half centuries before Muhammad, and it reads as the same New Testament.

Which leaves the Muslim with a hard choice. He can say the Quran was mistaken to point people to the Gospel Christians had. Or he can say the Gospel Christians had was reliable, in which case it says that Jesus claimed what the Gospels say he claimed. Most thoughtful Muslims prefer to say the Gospel in the Quran is the lost Injil of Jesus himself, and I understand why. But then there is no text for the command to point to, and the Quran's confirmation of what was "in their hands" has nothing to confirm.

I should be honest about my own side. The Gospels do not claim to have been dictated. Luke says he investigated carefully and wrote an orderly account, and Christians have never needed the Gospels to be a single block of heaven's dictation, because for us the revelation is a person and the Gospels are testimony to him. That also means textual differences do not shake the Christian faith the way they would shake a doctrine of dictation. And there are differences. We have 5,795 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament, against 1,757 for Homer's *Iliad* and 210 for Plato (Jones, 2013), and where they differ the differences are visible, and most of them are matters of spelling and word order. A few passages are really uncertain, and Christian scholars say so openly. The great number of copies is how we know. It is hard to change a book quietly when thousands of hands, in different languages, are copying it in different cities.

Now the Muslim claim about the Quran, which deserves the same fairness. The Quran says of itself, "Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur'an and indeed, We will be its guardian" (Quran 15:9, Saheeh International). Muslims believe this, and the written text is remarkably stable. The classical collection of hadith records that a companion named Hudhaifa, "afraid of their differences in the recitation of the Qur'an," warned the caliph Uthman. Uthman had committee members including Zaid bin Thabit copy the Quran from the manuscripts held by Hafsa, told them to write it "in the dialect of Quraish," and then ordered "that all the other Qur'anic materials, whether written in fragmentary manuscripts or whole copies, be burnt" (Sahih Bukhari 4987). A Muslim reads that as an act of love for the text. A caliph saw a fire starting and put it out. I think that is a fair reading, and I don't think it is a scandal. The popular Christian line that Uthman edited the Quran and the Bible was left alone is rude and wrong.

Equally, Muslim scholars do not claim that recitation has been uniform. The ten recognised readings of the Quran follow the Uthmanic written text, which was first written without the dots and marks that later readers added, and a paper by the Yaqeen Institute, a Muslim research body, says most of the differences among them are subtle, estimates that about 0.9 per cent of the Quran's words have an alternative reading, and deals openly with the older readings attributed to Ibn Masud, whose codex some reports say lacked the opening chapter and the last two, though the authors note that scholars dispute whether those reports are authentic (Khatib & Khan, 2024). Ibn Masud was a companion whom the hadith record saying, "By Allah, I learnt over seventy Suras direct from Allah's Apostle" (Sahih Bukhari 5000), and his reading was influential in Kufa. So a Christian who says "the Quran has no variants" has not read what Muslim scholars write. A Muslim who says "the Bible has no stable text" has not read what Christian scholars write either.

So I would put the difference this way. Islam holds that God promised to guard his book and then did. Christians hold that God sent his Word in a person, and that the writings about that person were carried by ordinary hands into thousands of copies. There is one more difference worth stating, and it comes from a Gambian scholar who was born Muslim and became a Christian, Lamin Sanneh. He argued that Christianity is a translated religion, because it has no sacred language and passes into each people's own tongue, while Islam treats the Quran as Arabic revelation that is not truly translated (Sanneh, 1989, as summarised in Carman, 1989). Samuel Ajayi Crowther's work on the Yoruba Bible was crucial to the way the gospel took root in this country (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.), and nobody needs to learn Hebrew or Greek to hear Jesus speak. Sanneh's thesis is debated, and a reviewer pointed out cases where Christians have resisted translation (Carman, 1989). But it does explain why a Nigerian child can hear the Gospel in her mother's tongue on a Sunday morning.

## Jesus as prophet and the cross

Muslims honour Jesus. Many Christians never hear that, and a Muslim will say it with some hurt. He is called the Messiah and a messenger, and he is born of a virgin. What Islam denies is that he is God's Son and that he died on a cross.

The Quran speaks of the crucifixion in one place. It begins with what it calls a Jewish boast.

> *"And [for] their saying, "Indeed, we have killed the Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, the messenger of Allah." And they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him; but [another] was made to resemble him to them."*
>
> — Quran 4:157 (Saheeh International)

The next verse says, "Rather, Allah raised him to Himself" (Quran 4:158, Saheeh International). Most classical Muslim commentators understood that another person was made to look like Jesus and was killed in his place, while Jesus was taken up alive. Another verse, 3:55, has God say, "indeed I will take you and raise you to Myself," and Muslims read the line in Jesus's own mouth about "the day I will die" (19:33) as pointing to a death after he returns at the end of time. I am giving the Muslim view as it is held, and I would add that it is a coherent reading that takes God's honour seriously. God would not abandon his messenger to his enemies.

My answer has three parts. The first is historical. The crucifixion of Jesus is one of the best attested events of the ancient world. The Roman historian Tacitus, writing about Nero's persecution, says that "Christus" had "suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus" (Tacitus, trans. Church, n.d., Annals 15.44). Tacitus was no friend of Christians, and the Jewish and Roman records at that time had no motive to invent the death of a man whose followers were an embarrassment. Paul, writing within a few decades of the event, passes on what he had himself received, that Christ died for our sins and was raised on the third day (1 Corinthians 15:3 to 4). The Gospels agree on the main outline. A Muslim may reply that the Quran outranks history. Then I would ask the second question, which is how this sits beside the verses I quoted a moment ago, where the Quran sends its hearers to the Gospel as a trustworthy witness. The Gospel is the primary witness to the cross.

The third part is the one I find most useful in conversation, because it begins with agreement. The Quran is worried about a boast, that "we killed the Messiah." The Gospels share that worry. In John, Jesus says of his life, "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18, NIV). Nobody defeated him. If he died, it was because he chose to, and he said so before it happened. The Christian claim is that God's messenger was not overpowered by his enemies. He went into their hands knowing what he was doing, and God raised him on the third day. That is also what the Quran's 3:55 and 4:158 say in their own way, that God took him to himself. The disagreement is over whether he died first.

I cannot prove the Quran wrong from within the Quran, and I won't try. What I can say is that two witnesses are in conflict, one separated from the event by about six centuries and the other written within living memory of it, and the one that is closer says he was crucified and raised. That is why the resurrection matters so much in this argument. If it happened, the rest follows, and if it didn't, Christians have nothing to say that is worth a Muslim's time.

## Access to God and the Day of Judgement

The next claim is about access. A Muslim stands to pray with no priest, no altar and no intermediary, and the Quran speaks of a God who says "We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein" (Quran 50:16, Saheeh International). I won't pretend that Christians have always been as good as Muslims at remembering that. Anyone who has watched a worship service turn into a queue for a man in a robe to touch your forehead knows that some Christians behave as if God lives behind a counter. The Muslim criticism of that is fair and often deserved.

I also want to correct a point I have seen made against Muslims, that they pray to Muhammad, or that he is a hidden mediator in daily prayer. That is not what Islam teaches, and orthodox Muslims regard prayer to anyone but God as the gravest sin. Muslims pray to God directly. The prayer does include a blessing on the Prophet in its final sitting, but that is a greeting and a request for his blessing, and does not turn him into a go between. So on the daily practice of prayer, I think the Muslim claim stands.

The question I would raise concerns the Last Day. The Quran asks, "Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?" (Quran 2:255, Saheeh International), and the hadith describe how that permission will be used. In the long account preserved in Sahih Bukhari 4712, people gather in distress and go to Adam, who says "Myself! Myself! Myself!" and sends them to Noah, who sends them to Abraham, then Moses, then Jesus, who also sends them on, to Muhammad. Muhammad falls in prostration beneath the Throne and is told, "Intercede and it will be accepted." Muslims call this the great intercession, and it is the high point of the Prophet's honour. In that account, interestingly, Jesus is the only prophet who mentions no sin of his own, though he too sends them away.

The Muslim reply is that this is permitted intercession and not mediation, since God alone decides who is forgiven. I accept the distinction. But it leaves a question that both religions share, which is on what ground anyone is qualified to stand before God for someone else. In Islam it is the Prophet's rank. In the New Testament it is the sinless death of one who is also the Son. The writer to the Hebrews says that Jesus "is able to save to the uttermost those who draw near to God through him, since he always lives to make intercession for them" (Hebrews 7:25, ESV). And the same letter says that he does not stand in the doorway but holds it open.

> *"Let us then approach God's throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy."*
>
> — Hebrews 4:16 (NIV)

So the Christian is not saying that Muslims lack a door and Christians have one. We say that the door is there for everyone, and that it was opened by the person who is God's own Son, and that is why we don't have to go and find the best qualified prophet on the Last Day. The first Christians saw the curtain of the Jerusalem temple torn from top to bottom at his death (Matthew 27:51), and read it as the end of the barrier.

## Whose burden, and who pays

The third claim is about personal responsibility. The Quran puts it plainly. "And no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another" (Quran 35:18, Saheeh International). This is a serious moral instinct, and Christians share it. Nobody wants a justice system where a stranger takes your sentence without your consent.

It is also true that the hadith contain a difficulty that Muslims themselves have discussed. Sahih Muslim 2767 reports, on the authority of Abu Musa, that on the Day of Resurrection God will give each Muslim a Jew or a Christian and tell him that this one is his rescue from the Fire. A reader who has met this hadith in a hostile tract will take it to mean that Muslims' sins are loaded onto Jews and Christians. That is not how classical Muslim scholars read it, and I should say so. Imam al Nawawi explains that the disbelievers go into the fire "because of their disbelief and sin," so that they become like ransoms for the Muslims, and al Qadi Iyad says that the disbeliever in effect takes the believer's place in Hell, because God has decreed that Hell will be filled (as summarised in IslamQA, n.d.). So there is no transfer of guilt, only a substitution of places. I'm bound to say that the language of ransom is odd for a religion that rejects substitution. But the tradition is at least consistent with its own rule that nobody bears another person's sin.

The Christian claim is different from both. The cross is not a forced transfer of guilt from the comfortable to the condemned. It is a voluntary self offering by one who had the power to refuse it. "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18, NIV). A burden carried by someone who was forced to carry it is exactly what both of us object to. A burden carried by someone who stepped forward, who could have refused, and who did it for people who could not pay the debt, is something else. Christians do not say that God dumped one man's guilt on another man's account. We say God went himself.

## Can anyone be sure of salvation

This is where I think the two faiths differ most, and a Muslim will not find the Christian description unfair, because Islam says it of itself. A Muslim lives between fear and hope. The Quran says the weighing of deeds on that day "will be the truth" (Quran 7:8, Saheeh International), and the one whose scales are heavy will succeed. The Prophet himself was told to say, "I am not something original among the messengers, nor do I know what will be done with me or with you" (Quran 46:9, Saheeh International), and the hadith record him saying of a companion who had died, "By Allah, I do not know what Allah will do with me though I am Allah's Apostle" (Sahih Bukhari 1243). The most striking report is in Sahih Muslim, where he says, "There is none amongst you whose deeds alone would attain salvation for him," and when his companions ask whether that includes him, he says it does, unless God covers him with his mercy (Sahih Muslim 2816).

Islam has real mercy, and I am not setting up a straw man. "My mercy encompasses all things," God says in the Quran (7:156, Saheeh International), and he calls to his servants who have sinned, "do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (Quran 39:53, Saheeh International). Sunni teaching holds that a Muslim who dies affirming God's oneness will in the end enter paradise, even if sins send him first into the fire for purification. What Islam does not offer is the thing the New Testament offers in the present tense. No Muslim, however devout, can say on the day he prays that he is certain of what will become of him.

Christians have disagreed with each other about a great deal on this subject, including whether a true believer can finally fall away. Reformed Christians hold that those whom God saves he keeps, and Arminian Christians hold that a person can walk away from salvation. But all of them agree on the ground of assurance. It rests on Christ's work and not on my performance. "There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus" (Romans 8:1, ESV). Paul asks who will bring a charge against God's elect and answers, "It is God who justifies" (Romans 8:33, ESV), and he adds that Christ is "at the right hand of God, who indeed is interceding for us" (Romans 8:34, ESV). The weighing of deeds in Islam becomes, for the Christian, the weighing of someone else's deeds in our place. I admit that many Christians live with less assurance than they should, and a prosperity sermon that tells people to sow a seed to be sure of God's favour offers a worse assurance than a Muslim has. But the gospel as the New Testament states it does offer certainty. It says "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8, ESV).

## Why forgiveness has to cost something

A Muslim will press the next question, and it is a fair one. If God is merciful and can forgive anyone he wills, why does anyone have to die? Why can't he simply forgive?

The Christian answer begins with what sin is. Islam teaches that Adam repented and was forgiven, and it does not teach inherited guilt. Each child is born without sin. The New Testament takes the same facts and says that all humans, whatever one thinks about how it spreads, fall short. "For all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and are justified by his grace as a gift, through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:23 to 24, ESV). Every honest person, in any religion, knows the gap between what he thinks he ought to be and what he is. It is rarely a gap of information. A man knows he shouldn't lie to his wife, and he does. A woman knows she should forgive her sister, and she doesn't. Give either of them a stricter timetable and a longer fast and you will get a better informed failure.

The question is whether forgiveness is free. A judge who simply waves guilt away is not merciful, he is careless, and the victims of the crime know it. A father who forgives his son's debt to a neighbour has not ignored the debt. He has paid it, or it has stayed on his own account. That is the Christian meaning of God's justice and mercy meeting. Paul puts it as God showing that he is "just and the justifier" of the one who has faith in Jesus (Romans 3:26), and the old teachers of the church, from Anselm in the eleventh century onwards, returned to this thought again and again. Mercy that costs the giver something is real mercy.

Paul wrote what he found in himself in a way that most of us recognise at once.

> *"Wretched man that I am! Who will deliver me from this body of death?"*
>
> — Romans 7:24 (ESV)

Notice what Paul asks. He doesn't ask who will tell him what to do, because he knew. He asks who will rescue him. No law answers that, because the law is the thing that showed him the problem. And the answer, in the very next line, is a person.

> *"Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!"*
>
> — Romans 7:25 (ESV)

## A whole way of life and five daily prayers

I want to give more here than most Christian writers would. Islam does offer a comprehensive system, with prayer, fasting, almsgiving, pilgrimage, dietary law, commercial ethics, family structure and governance. A faith that tells you how to pray, how to fast, how to give, how to do business and how to bury your dead builds a person who knows what Tuesday is for. Many Nigerian Christians have watched a version of our own faith shrink to a transaction. Plant a seed, claim a breakthrough, go home. Against that, a Muslim neighbour who gets up before dawn to pray is making a serious statement about who owns his time. I have no wish to talk down to it. The posture of prayer, the community facing one direction, the discipline of the fast: these train the body and not only the mind.

My question is the one the structure itself raises. What is it for? A soldier can keep perfect discipline and feel nothing for the general. A pupil can recite a poem he does not understand. Christians need to admit that a believer who prays only when he feels like it is not freer, only lazier. But Jesus pressed his own people on this point. He warned against the prayer made to be seen, and told his listeners to go into a room, shut the door, and speak to a Father who sees in secret (Matthew 6:5 to 6). Paul's instruction to "pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17) invites the whole of life into prayer, which is a heavier demand than a timetable.

The old prophets saw it coming. Jeremiah spoke of a day when God would say, in the ESV's wording, "I will put my law within them, and I will write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33). A system of rules can produce an ordered life, and I don't mean that as a small thing. What it cannot do by itself is change the person who keeps it. Paul describes the alternative as a harvest and not an enforcement. The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience and the rest, and "against such things there is no law" (Galatians 5:22 to 23). That is a claim that no religion of commands can make, including mine. It is a claim about what God does inside a person, and Christians say it happens because of what happened on the cross.

## The last prophet and who said so

The sixth claim is that Muhammad is the final prophet. The Quran says that he is "the Messenger of Allah and last of the prophets" (Quran 33:40, Saheeh International), and Muslims hold that no revelation can follow.

A Muslim has good reasons for believing it. He will point to the Quran's literary power, to the transformation of Arabia within a generation, and to passages which he reads as foretelling the Prophet. Two are common. Deuteronomy 18:18 speaks of a prophet like Moses raised up from among "their brothers," and Muslims read the brothers as the descendants of Ishmael. Christians read it as Jesus, as Peter did in Acts 3:22. And in John 14 and 16 Jesus promises the Paraclete, whom Muslims have identified with Muhammad and Christians with the Holy Spirit, who in John 14:26 is sent in Jesus's name. I would only say that the Gospel text describes the Paraclete as coming to those who were then sitting with Jesus, which fits Pentecost better than a seventh century Arabian.

My main answer is about the logic of finality. A claim to be final is a claim a person makes about himself and his message, and this does not make it false, but it does mean it needs support beyond the statement. Jesus too made claims about himself, and Christians point to the evidence for them. Which of the two is true cannot be settled by repeating either claim louder. It is settled by looking at who Jesus was, and a Muslim has already said a great deal about that.

The Christian position is also that a final word has been spoken. The question is when and by whom. The letter to the Hebrews opens by saying that God spoke "by the prophets" at many times and in many ways, but "in these last days" has spoken to us by his Son (Hebrews 1:1 to 2). A word that comes after the Son is not a completion by this account, but a revision, and it requires a burden of proof matching its claim. The patriarch Timothy made a similar point to the caliph in 781, answering that Christ himself had warned against accepting later prophets and false Christs who might come after him (Ministering to Muslims, n.d.). The caliph was not persuaded. But it was said to his face, and he did not have the patriarch thrown out.

## A brotherhood in a country with two wounds

The last claim is the most moving, because Nigerians can see it with their own eyes. The worldwide Muslim community is a family of a kind that few other religions match. A Muslim who arrives in an unfamiliar city can often find a home within an hour. The church could learn from that.

It would be dishonest to move on without noticing the history. The first great division within Islam, between Sunni and Shia, began over the question of who should lead the community after the Prophet, and Ali, the Prophet's cousin and son in law, was killed by a Kharijite in 661. The Ottoman Caliphate, which came closest to realising the ideal of one governed Muslim community, was abolished in 1924 and has not been restored. None of this disqualifies the ideal. Every human attempt to live out a noble ideal fails somewhere. Christianity has no room to throw stones. The Crusades, the Inquisition, the blessing of the transatlantic slave trade by men who called themselves Christians, and the apartheid theology that baptised racial hierarchy in biblical language are our wounds, and they are real.

So the question is not which community has a cleaner history. It is what the foundation of the brotherhood is. Paul wrote to churches in Galatia where Jewish and Gentile believers were failing, badly, to live as equals.

> *"There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus."*
>
> — Galatians 3:28 (ESV)

The basis of Christian unity is not shared practice, shared ethnicity or shared language. It is shared belonging to Christ. The early church at Antioch had among its leaders men from Africa (Acts 13:1), and an Ethiopian official was baptised on the road to Gaza (Acts 8:26 to 39). That means the person who disagrees with me about nearly everything is still, in my own faith, someone Christ died for. I haven't always lived as if that were true. Neither, I suspect, has the Muslim community always lived as if the worldwide family were true in the next compound.

And in Nigeria, we stand on each other's wounds. Pew's note on Nigeria records that both Muslims and Christians have been "attacked, kidnapped and killed by armed gangs," that Boko Haram and the Islamic State West Africa Province have burned both churches and mosques, and that in the Middle Belt a conflict between mostly Christian farmers and mostly Muslim herders has turned violent (Pew Research Center, 2025). Counting the dead is fiercely contested, and I won't enter that contest with figures I can't stand behind. This year more than a hundred Muslim worshippers were seized from a mosque in Borgu, in Niger State, and the Catholic Bishops' Conference of Nigeria answered by standing with them and demanding their release, saying that "no political, ethnic or religious cause can ever justify such cruelty" (The Tablet, 2026). I would like every pastor to find a way to say that sentence from his pulpit.

That is the first thing, and I would put it before any argument. We do not blame a whole faith for the crimes of armed men. We do not ask a Muslim in Ibadan to apologise for a camp in the forest, any more than we expect a Christian in Kano to answer for every angry sermon.

The second thing is what the peacemakers have shown us. In 1992 violence broke out between Christians and Muslims in Kaduna State. Imam Muhammad Ashafa lost two brothers and his teacher. Pastor James Wuye lost his hand. In 1995 they founded the Interfaith Mediation Centre, which has mediated conflicts across Nigeria and trains youth, women and religious leaders as peace activists (Tanenbaum, n.d.). In August 2026 the Muslim World League launched a social peace initiative in Abuja under the theme "Diversity and Social Peace: Partnership for a Promising Future," bringing Muslim and Christian leaders together, and the Abuja Declaration that came out of it set up a coordinating body for religious and civil leaders in West Africa (Africanews, 2026). Long before that, in the nineteenth century, Samuel Ajayi Crowther of the Niger Mission is described as having pioneered an early form of Christian Muslim dialogue for Africa (Dictionary of African Christian Biography, n.d.). Dialogue is an old idea in our country, and one we keep needing to learn again.

## People worth reading

If you want to go further, a few guides are worth your time. Kenneth Cragg, an Anglican bishop who spent decades in Beirut, Jerusalem and Cairo and died in 2012 aged 99, wrote *The Call of the Minaret* (1956) as an attempt to understand Islam from the inside while staying rooted in his own faith, and the people who knew him describe a man who listened first (Anglican Communion News Service, 2012; Wikipedia contributors, n.d.). Lamin Sanneh, mentioned above, was born in the Gambia, came to Christianity from Islam, taught at Yale, and wrote *Piety and Power: Muslims and Christians in West Africa* (1996) among other books (Wikipedia contributors, 2026). And Nabeel Qureshi, who grew up in a devout Muslim family in America, told the story of his path to Christianity in *Seeking Allah, Finding Jesus* (2014) and died in 2017 aged 34 (Zondervan Academic, 2017). Read them with the Quran open beside you, and don't take my summary of any of them in place of their own words.

## What the cross does that a system cannot

Here is where the answers converge. Islam is, in its most sincere form, a serious attempt to order human life around submission to God. It takes God seriously, it takes human responsibility seriously, and it takes the unity of God seriously. For that it deserves respect and not condescension.

But the gap Paul describes in Romans 7 remains. No timetable answers it, no perfectly preserved book answers it, no brotherhood answers it, though all of them are good gifts. The claim of the New Testament is that when people had tried everything they could try, God came himself. He lived the life no human being has managed to live. He gave himself as a ransom, in Paul's word to Timothy. And the weight of what was wrong with us was carried by someone who did not have to carry it.

A Muslim will hear that as a claim about a man, and a hard one. I understand why. The Quran is firm that God does not take a son, and I have no wish to pretend the difference is small. If the Christian claim is false, Zainab has every reason to say so, and I hope she will. If it is true, then it is no rival offer at all, and stands as the answer to the question that every system, ours included, was asking.

## Come and sit down with them

If you are a Christian who has read this far, here is what I would ask. The next time a Muslim colleague or neighbour asks why you believe what you believe, resist the rebuke. Ask what she finds hardest to accept, and listen long enough that she hears you taking it seriously. Learn what the Quran says about Jesus, because it says more than most Christians think. Ask what she thinks the human problem is. Then say, in your own words and without a verse used as a weapon, what you think God did about it.

And if you are a Muslim who has read this far, you are welcome at the same table. I have tried to state your case in words you would accept, and where I have got any of it wrong I would honestly like to be told, because I would rather be corrected than be comfortable.

Tolu and Zainab are still at that table in the library. It is a week before examinations, and the photocopier is free. There is a second chance in front of them, and I hope he takes it.
