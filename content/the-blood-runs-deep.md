## A Question Asked in Whispers

Picture a man of thirty in Ibadan. His father drank. His grandfather, by every family account, drank harder. Two of his uncles lost their marriages and one lost a business, and now he has noticed his own temper doing a thing he swore it would never do. He hasn't told anyone. He has, however, begun to wonder whether the whole thing is a package that was delivered to his family before he was born, with his name already written on the label.

Then a visiting minister at a Friday night programme stops the service and asks whether anyone present comes from a family with a repeating pattern. Hands go up slowly. The man's hand goes up with them.

That is a scene, not a memory, but anyone who has spent time in Nigerian church life knows how true to the room it is. The question underneath it is old and it is not silly: *is there something on my family that I did not choose, and can it hold me?* A second question sits just behind it, quieter and more frightening. *Can a demon actually get into a Christian?*

I think both deserve a plain biblical answer, and I think the answers are better news than most of the people asking them have been told. But I'm not going to get there by waving the questions away. Some of the people who dismiss generational curses are as lazy as some of the people who sell them. So the case starts with why the fear makes sense.

## Why Nigerian Churches Take It Seriously

Start with the strongest version of the belief, put the way its own holders would put it.

The Bible does say that the sins of fathers are visited on children. It says so in the Ten Commandments. Families really do pass things down: habits, wounds, ways of treating a wife, ways of handling money. Nobody who has watched a family for forty years doubts that. And the African worldview, which does not find the unseen embarrassing, has always insisted that spiritual causes lie behind material ones. Western theology often shrugs at that. Nigerian Pentecostalism looked at the New Testament, with its demons and its healings, and said that the shrug was the real departure from the text.

The South African scholar Mookgo Kgatle (2022) reports that across African Pentecostalism, "Central to the ministry of deliverance is the deliverance from generational curses." He adds that some believers hold that even a born again person can still carry such a curse. Matthew Engelke (2010) observes that both Pentecostal and African Independent churches express a desire to "break with the past," and he thinks the idea needs more debate than it usually gets. That desire is not hard to understand. Many people in these congregations carry a family history of poverty, sickness and sudden death. A clear cause with a clear remedy is a comfort when doctors are expensive, the hospital is two hours away and the generator has been off since Tuesday.

So the belief isn't a trick that appeared from nowhere. It answers something real. And in its better forms it does something the cooler churches often fail to do. It believes the person who says that their trouble has a shape. Plenty of people in the pews have been told by a relative that it is only stress, by a doctor that it is only nerves, and by a polite congregation that it is only time. A church that says, in effect, "we see this, we will pray with you, and you are not mad" has met a real need. The question is whether the explanation it offers is the one the Bible gives.

It also gets exploited, and honesty requires saying so. The Nigerian writer and sceptic Leo Igwe (2021) wrote that families who pay for "family deliverance" are being exploited, that the practice leads to accusations against elderly relatives, and that hardship is better explained by bad governance than by ancestral curses. I don't share his conclusion that the spiritual dimension should leave the conversation, because Scripture won't let me drop it. But he describes things a good many Nigerians have also heard about: a price list for prayer, a named aunt blamed for a family's trouble, a household told that the remedy needs one more fee. A teaching that can only be fixed by a specialist, and only for a payment, deserves to be tested against the text before anyone pays.

That is what follows. The text first, then the practice.

## The Family Paul Could Have Written Off

Before Exodus, consider a courtroom in Caesarea Maritima, because it makes the stakes clearer than any theory does.

In Acts 25 and 26 the Apostle Paul stands before Herod Agrippa II. The name should make us pause. Herod the Great, the great grandfather, is the king who in Matthew 2:16 orders the killing of the boys of Bethlehem. Herod Antipas, a great uncle, had John the Baptist beheaded (Mark 6:14 to 29). Agrippa I, the father, is the Herod of Acts 12:1 to 2, who killed James the brother of John and then arrested Peter. If ever a family looked like a case study in a bloodline of resistance to Christ, this was it.

Now look at what Paul does with it. He doesn't tell Agrippa that his family has doomed him. He tells him about the resurrection, and then he asks him straight out whether he believes the prophets. He says he knows that Agrippa does. Agrippa's reply, in Acts 26:28, is a famous one, half a joke and half a flinch, about whether Paul thinks he can make him a Christian in such a short time.

Let me be careful about what the Herods prove. The four men were not one unbroken line. Antipas was a great uncle, and Agrippa II's own grandfather, Aristobulus IV, doesn't appear in the New Testament at all. And Acts never calls Agrippa II a persecutor. What the family really demonstrates is that ambition and violence can be learned, copied and admired inside a household. That is the first thing the rest of this article will argue: patterns pass down. But Paul treats the man in front of him as someone with a choice, and so should we.

## What the Law Says About Fathers and Children

Now the verses that start most of the fear. Here is the second commandment, as the English Standard Version has it.

> *"You shall not bow down to them or serve them, for I the Lord your God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me,"*
>
> — Exodus 20:5 (ESV)

And again, when God describes his own character to Moses after the golden calf.

> *"keeping steadfast love for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, but who will by no means clear the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children and the children's children, to the third and the fourth generation."*
>
> — Exodus 34:7 (ESV)

Read plainly, it does sound like a mechanism. But slow down. In both places the sentence is set beside the phrase "those who hate me," and in both it is dwarfed by mercy. Steadfast love goes to thousands. Judgment goes to three or four. The same formula comes back in Numbers 14:18, in the very chapter where Israel refuses to enter the land and God sentences that generation to forty years in the wilderness. The children do suffer for their parents' refusal. They spend their childhoods in the desert. No demon needs to be assigned to a bloodline for that to happen. A family goes over the cliff when the people steering it drive it there, and everyone in the back seat goes over too.

The Old Testament scholar Rodney Duke (2015) argues that the phrase "third and fourth generation" is most likely a way of speaking about the living household, the three or four generations who shared one compound in the ancient world. On that reading, the verse describes how one person's sin spills across a whole family at once, not how guilt is stored up and handed down the centuries like an heirloom. Duke notes that Israel's own courts were forbidden to punish children for what fathers had done, and the law of Deuteronomy 24:16 says so in terms. He also doesn't pretend that the children of sinners escape harm. They don't. A father who gambles the school fees damages his children whether or not any spiritual law is involved.

I'd put it this way. The Bible teaches that sin has consequences that run along family lines. It does not teach that those consequences are a spiritual debt that the next generation inherits as guilt. The first claim is just observation. The second is a different doctrine, and the text won't carry it.

## Ezekiel and the Proverb About Sour Grapes

If Exodus is the passage the fear leans on, Ezekiel 18 is the answer that fearful readers seldom visit.

The exiles in Babylon had a saying that put the blame behind them. The parents ate sour grapes, they said, and the children's teeth are set on edge. Jeremiah quotes the same proverb (31:29 to 30). The saying was true to life and a perfect excuse. Our fathers did it, so we are the victims. God's response through Ezekiel is to tell them they are going to stop saying it, and then he sets out the principle.

> *"The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son. The righteousness of the righteous shall be upon himself, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon himself."*
>
> — Ezekiel 18:20 (ESV)

You could hardly say it more clearly. And note who is being corrected. The people being rebuked were using their family history as a reason to put off repenting. That target is worth keeping in mind when a minister tells a whole congregation that their problems come down from the grandfathers. The proverb was comfortable. It put the cause somewhere far away and the cure in someone else's hands, and anyone who has sat through a family meeting where every sentence began with "it was my uncle" or "it was the village" will recognise the appeal. God's refusal to let them keep it was not unkind. It was the first step toward giving them something they could actually do.

Jesus meets the same instinct in John 9. His disciples see a man born blind and ask whose sin caused it, his or his parents'. They have inherited the instinct that every misfortune is a ledger entry somewhere in the family. Jesus answers it.

> *"It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him."*
>
> — John 9:3 (ESV)

I would not claim that this verse settles every case of family misfortune. It certainly settles that the disciples' reflex was wrong, and that a good deal of suffering has no cause that anybody can trace to a sinner, living or dead.

## When Manasseh's Sin Still Brought Exile

An honest reader will object here, and the objection is a strong one. Ezekiel's principle sounds neat, but the Old Testament itself describes Judah suffering for the sin of a dead king.

The writer of 2 Kings says exactly that, and puts it after Josiah's reforms.

> *"Still the Lord did not turn from the burning of his great wrath, by which his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him."*
>
> — 2 Kings 23:26 (ESV)

Manasseh had been dead for over fifty years when Judah fell. If the Bible really teaches individual accountability, why is a long dead king blamed for the exile?

I think the answer is that Scripture holds two things together, and we are always tempted to keep only one. A nation or a family is a body. What the leaders do shapes what everybody inherits: the land, the habits, the enemies, the debts. Those consequences are real, and they can last for generations. Anyone who has watched a family land dispute pass from one brother to his sons, and from them to their children, knows how a quarrel started by a dead man can still be fought at the same fence line. Anyone who has lived under decades of bad government knows it too. But being caught in the fallout is a different thing from being guilty before God. Ezekiel preached to exiles who were suffering because of a nation's sins, and he still told every one of them that his own standing before God would be decided by his own response.

And here the story turns, because the man the exile was blamed on did not stay where the writer of Kings left him. The Chronicler tells what Kings leaves out.

> *"And when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers. He prayed to him, and God was moved by his entreaty and heard his plea and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom. Then Manasseh knew that the Lord was God."*
>
> — 2 Chronicles 33:12 to 13 (ESV)

Sit with that for a moment. Manasseh, whom the Bible itself presents as among the worst kings Judah ever had, who built altars to other gods inside the temple and, according to 2 Kings 21:6, burned his own son as an offering, was forgiven. His grandson's generation still lived with the consequences. But the man himself was not stuck. If Manasseh could be reached, I don't know why a man in Ibadan with a bad temper and an uncle's bad luck should be told he is beyond reach.

That is the turn this article rests on. The family pattern is real, the consequences travel, and the door of repentance stays open the whole time. Not for a favoured few with the right minister. For anyone.

## Josiah and the Father He Chose

Manasseh's son Amon did not repent. The Chronicler says that Amon incurred guilt more and more, and then his servants killed him. His son Josiah was eight when he came to the throne.

The Bible gives Josiah a clear starting point for a spiritual life, and it is worth reading closely.

> *"For in the eighth year of his reign, while he was yet a boy, he began to seek the God of David his father,"*
>
> — 2 Chronicles 34:3 (ESV)

The words are standard for the Old Testament writers, who often speak of David as a father to the kings who followed him, and I wouldn't build a whole doctrine on the choice of phrase. But the effect is hard to miss. This boy had a father who did evil and a grandfather who did worse, and the text points past both of them. Four years after that, he began to clear Judah of its idols. The sixteen year old was already looking for God, and he did it with nobody at home to copy. That detail has always moved me more than the revival that followed. Most of us can follow a good example once somebody has set one. It is harder, and rarer, to start from nothing.

A correction belongs here. It is often said that Josiah, as a teenager, found the lost scroll of the law and was transformed overnight. The text puts the discovery in his eighteenth year as king, which makes him about twenty six. The seeking and the purge came first. The reading of the book deepened a faith that was already moving, and I think that is the more encouraging order, because it means that Josiah began with very little.

There is a further thought, and I'll mark it as a thought. Daniel was taken to Babylon in the reign of Josiah's son, and was young when he went. In Daniel 9:21, in exile and with the temple in ruins, he still marks his prayer by the time of the evening sacrifice. It is plausible that his devotion was shaped by the revival Josiah led. But no text says so, and the reasoning is the kind of inference a reader should hold lightly.

Josiah could not stop the exile. He did not turn the nation back. But what is recorded of him is that he did not follow his father, and nothing in the record suggests he could not have chosen otherwise.

## What Christ Did About the Curse

Everything so far was said in the Old Testament, and some readers will reasonably say that Christians stand somewhere else. They do, and the place is better.

Paul has a sentence in Galatians that matters here. The curse he means is the one the law pronounced on those who broke it, set out in Deuteronomy. He writes that Christ took it on himself.

> *"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us ..."*
>
> — Galatians 3:13 (ESV)

The sentence goes on to quote Deuteronomy about the one hanged on a tree, and I have stopped where the English Standard Version has a dash, so that the quotation stays exact. Paul isn't speaking directly about family patterns here, and I would be overreaching to say he is. His subject is the covenant curse on law breakers, the curses laid out in Deuteronomy 27 and 28 for anyone who walked away from the covenant. But the logic travels. If the worst curse in Scripture was borne by Christ on the cross, a Christian has no remaining sentence hanging over him from his grandfather's sins.

Colossians says the same thing from another side, about the powers behind such claims.

> *"He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."*
>
> — Colossians 2:15 (ESV)

A power that has been disarmed can still shout. It cannot hold a debt that was cancelled.

Then there is Balaam, whom a king hired to curse Israel and who could not do it. Moses later says why.

> *"But the Lord your God would not listen to Balaam; instead the Lord your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the Lord your God loved you."*
>
> — Deuteronomy 23:5 (ESV)

Proverbs 26:2 puts the point in a proverb, that a curse without a cause does not land. I find that more reassuring than any ritual. If God would not let Balaam's professional curse stand against an Israel that kept stumbling, a believer in Christ has more reason to trust him, not less.

## Real Patterns and Honest Help

None of that makes the patterns imaginary.

Anyone who has watched a family for a few decades has seen it. Drinking runs down a family line. So do violence, infidelity, debt and silence. Part of it is learned by watching. A boy who has never seen anyone in his family finish a degree, keep a promise to a wife or save a naira has no picture of how it is done, and the lack of a picture can pass for destiny. Part is circumstance, since poverty has its own momentum and the family that cannot pay school fees this year will find it harder next year. And part may be inherited in the body, since some temperaments and some illnesses run in families. And part is what Paul calls strongholds in 2 Corinthians 10:4, the habits that are fortified by repetition until they feel like destiny. The Bible knows all this. It just doesn't tell the person in that position that he has a spiritual debt on his account.

What it offers is quite practical, and very close to what Josiah did. Name the pattern honestly. Confess what is yours, and forgive what was done to you. Tell somebody, and tell them early. If the pattern is alcohol, anger or depression, see a doctor or a counsellor in the same week as you see your pastor, because a man can take his drinking to prayer and to a clinic on the same Friday, and no minister worth hearing would say otherwise. Then decide who you are going to follow, as Josiah did, and keep deciding it.

About deliverance culture, then, I'd offer a test, not a verdict. Prayer for freedom, with people who love you and know you, is something the New Testament would recognise. Hear what the ministry says about the cross. If the message is that Christ finished it and you are learning to walk in that, you are in reasonably safe company. If the message is that something is still owed, that you need a special session, a special oil, a named relative to be confronted, or a larger offering, then it has quietly added to the work of Christ, and Colossians 2:15 is the answer to it. Nobody should be told that their grandmother is the reason for their trouble on the strength of a vision at a night programme, and nobody should be pointed at an elderly relative on that basis. The Bible has no tradition of accusing the old woman in the compound. It has a long one of loving her. And nobody should be told that the only help available is for sale.

## Whether a Christian Can Be Possessed

The second question deserves honest treatment, because believers disagree about it and always have.

The Gospels use a Greek verb, *daimonizomai*, that translators often render as "possessed by a demon." Some argue that "demonised" is nearer the sense, a word for being under a demon's power or influence without any claim about ownership. Others think the older translation is fine. I don't think the dispute can be settled by a single word.

What the New Testament settles is ownership. Believers have been transferred out of the domain of darkness into the kingdom of the Son (Colossians 1:13). Nothing, including angels and demons, can separate them from the love of God in Christ (Romans 8:38 to 39). A demon cannot own a Christian. On that, careful readers agree.

The disagreement is about influence. Many Christians, including many Nigerian ones, hold that a believer can be oppressed, tempted and harassed, and that a believer who keeps open doors to occult practice, or to bitterness, or to long habits of sin, gives the enemy room to work. Others say the New Testament never describes a Christian being demonised and that the proper response to every such struggle is repentance, truth and community. The picture people reach for is a house you own with a back door left open, so that squatters move in who have no right to be there. Like all pictures it limps. It does say something Scripture says too, that a believer's title is secure while his habits can still let in trouble. Some read Jesus's rebuke to Peter in Matthew 16:23 as an example, the Lord speaking to the voice behind the words and not simply to his friend. I lean toward the first view, with caution, and I would not call a person who holds the second a fool. What both views keep is the thing that matters. The believer is not owned, is not helpless, and does not wait for a specialist.

Ephesians 6 calls for the whole armour of God for a reason. Opposition is real. But a soldier in armour is a different thing from a prisoner waiting for a key.

## Grace Reaches Further Than the Pattern

Go back to the verse where the fear began. Judgment to the third and fourth generation. Steadfast love to thousands. In Deuteronomy 7:9 the thousands become a thousand generations, and in Romans 5:20 Paul says that where sin increased, grace abounded all the more. It would be silly to turn the figures into a sum, but the direction is not in doubt. God is able to reach further than any pattern, and he does it again in every generation.

So the man in Ibadan has a choice that his father never took and that his grandfather probably never heard. He doesn't have to pretend the pattern isn't there. He also doesn't have to carry it as a verdict. He can tell the truth about it, and then he can come to the Father in the one way that has always been open, through the Son who took the curse and rose from the dead.

If you are that man, or that woman, or the quiet cousin who has been wondering since the last family funeral, you don't have to sort out your family history before you come. Nobody does. Paul puts the invitation simply in Romans 10:9, that if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Start there.

And this week, if you can, find one person you trust and tell them about the pattern you are afraid of. Say it plainly, to a pastor or an older friend, and ask them to pray with you and not to charge you for it. Then see whether the fear is still as large as it was. Fear grows best in private, and it tends to shrink when it is said aloud among people who love you.
