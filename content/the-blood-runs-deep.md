## A Question People Ask in Whispers

Picture a man of thirty in Ibadan. His father drank. His grandfather, by every family account, drank harder. Two of his uncles lost their marriages and one lost a business, and now he has noticed his own temper doing a thing he swore it never would. He hasn't told anyone. He has begun to wonder whether the whole thing is a package delivered to his family before he was born, with his name already on the label.

Then a visiting minister at a Friday night programme stops the service and asks whether anyone present comes from a family with a repeating pattern. Hands go up slowly. His goes up with them.

That is a scene, not a memory, but anyone who has spent time in Nigerian church life knows how true to the room it is. The question underneath it is old and it is not silly. *Is there something on my family that I did not choose, and can it hold me?* Just behind it sits a second question, quieter and more frightening. *Can a demon actually get inside a Christian?*

These can free a person completely or lock him in fatalism for life, which is why they deserve a serious answer and not a shrug on one side or a sensation on the other. I think both have plain biblical answers, and I think the answers are better news than most of the people asking have been told. But I won't get there by waving the questions away. Some of the people who dismiss generational curses are as careless as some of the people who sell them. So we begin with why the fear makes sense.

## Why Nigerian Churches Take It Seriously

Let me put the belief the way its own holders would put it, and put it at full strength.

The Bible does say that the sins of fathers are visited on children. It says so in the Ten Commandments. Families really do pass things down: habits, wounds, ways of treating a wife, ways of handling money. Nobody who has watched a family for forty years doubts it. And the African worldview, which does not find the unseen embarrassing, has always held that spiritual causes lie behind material ones. A good deal of Western theology shrugs at that. Nigerian Pentecostalism read the New Testament, with its demons and its healings, and said the shrug was the real departure from the text. I think it has a point, and nothing below takes it back.

The South African scholar Mookgo Kgatle (2022) notes that the ministry of deliverance in African Pentecostalism has traditionally involved "the deliverance from generational curses." The Ghanaian evangelical Ebenezer Adu Ampong (2004), writing a careful assessment of deliverance ministries from inside the evangelical camp, found that such ministries trace sickness, poverty, late marriage and visa refusals to causes that include ancestral curses, and that they draw people for two reasons. Medical care is expensive, and the traditional worldview sees a supernatural dimension in ordinary events. He did not write the movement off. He found that some ministries meet real needs in biblically sound ways while others lean on ideas he judged unchristian and superstitious, and he wanted evangelicals to learn which was which. Matthew Engelke (2010) observes that Pentecostal and African Independent churches alike express a wish to break with the past, and he thinks the idea deserves more debate than it gets.

That wish is easy to understand. Many people in these congregations carry a family history of poverty, sickness and sudden death. A clear cause with a clear remedy is a comfort when the hospital is two hours away and the generator has been off since Tuesday. And in its better forms the belief does something the cooler churches often fail to do. It believes the person who says their trouble has a shape. Plenty of people have been told by a relative that it is only stress, by a doctor that it is only nerves, and by a polite congregation that it is only time. A church that says, in effect, "we see this, we will pray with you, and you are not mad" has met a real need.

There is also a current reason the question is alive. A November 2025 report from Baylor's Institute for Studies of Religion, summarising a paper by the Nigerian sociologist Edlyne Anugwom, describes a resurgence of ancestral and idol worship pursued for money and power, especially in the east of the country, with church leaders reporting that young men are drawn to it. Whatever one thinks of the details, the picture is of a society in which the ancestors have not gone quiet, and a church that said nothing about them would be talking to an empty room.

The belief also gets exploited, and honesty requires saying so. The Nigerian writer and sceptic Leo Igwe (2021) wrote that family deliverance "has become a huge business enterprise," that people pay "up to a hundred thousand naira (220 dollars) for deliverance," and that evangelists "call out elderly persons or any other suspected agents of the devil in the family or community." He calls the practice a form of witch hunting, and he says poverty, disease and sudden death come from misgovernance, not from anything inherited from the past. I don't share his conclusion that the spiritual question should leave the conversation, because Scripture won't let me drop it. But he describes what a good many Nigerians have also heard about: a price list for prayer, a named aunt blamed for a family's trouble, a household told the remedy needs one more fee. Kgatle makes the same worry from the inside, quoting Matthew 10:8, where Jesus tells his disciples to give freely what they have freely received. A teaching that can only be fixed by a specialist, and only for a payment, ought to be tested against the text before anyone pays.

So that is what we do. The text first, then the practice.

## What Kind of Curse We Mean

The honest answer to "are generational curses real?" is that it depends on what the word is made to carry. People use it for at least four different things, and most of the confusion comes from sliding between them.

The first is consequence. A father who drinks the school fees damages his children whether or not any spiritual law is involved. The second is covenant curse, the formal sanctions Moses set out in Deuteronomy 27 and 28 for Israel's breaking of the covenant with God. The third is the spoken curse, a person with a grievance, or a hired specialist, speaking harm against someone. The fourth is the claim that I stand under a standing spiritual debt, assigned to my bloodline by some ancestor's sin, which gives dark powers a legal right over me that is only removed by a particular prayer ritual. When a minister says "generational curse" at a night programme, he usually means the fourth.

My argument is that the first is real and heavy, the second was carried by Christ, the third cannot land on someone God is protecting, and the fourth is not in the Bible at all. The rest of this essay is the work of showing that, one text at a time, without skipping the difficult ones.

## The Family Paul Could Have Written Off

Before Exodus, consider a courtroom in Caesarea Maritima, because it makes the stakes clearer than a theory does.

In Acts 25 and 26 the Apostle Paul stands before Herod Agrippa II. The name should make us pause, because there are four men called Herod in this part of the New Testament and they are related. Herod the Great, Agrippa's great grandfather, is the king who in Matthew 2:16 orders the killing of the boys of Bethlehem. Herod Antipas, a great uncle, had John the Baptist beheaded (Mark 6:14 to 29), and Luke 23 shows him mocking Jesus on the eve of the cross. Agrippa I, the father, is the Herod of Acts 12:1 to 2, who killed James the brother of John and then arrested Peter. If ever a family looked like a case study in a bloodline of resistance to Christ, this was it.

Now look at what Paul does. He doesn't tell Agrippa that his family has doomed him. He gives his own story, he tells him about the resurrection, and then he asks him straight out whether he believes the prophets, adding that he knows Agrippa does. Agrippa's reply in Acts 26:28 is a famous one, half a joke and half a flinch, about whether Paul thinks he can make him a Christian in such a short time. Paul does not soften the message because of the family tree. He talks to Agrippa as a man who can still answer.

I should be careful about what the Herods prove. They were not one unbroken line of identical men. Antipas was a great uncle and not a grandfather, and Acts never calls Agrippa II a persecutor. What the family does show is that ambition, cruelty and contempt for the true King can be learned, copied and admired inside a household across several generations. That is the first thing the rest of this essay will say: patterns pass down. The second thing is what Paul shows us by his posture. A pattern is not a sentence, and the man in front of you is a person with a choice.

## What the Law Says About Fathers and Children

Now the verses that start most of the fear. Here is the second commandment.

> *"You shall not bow down to them or serve them, for I the Lord your God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children to the third and fourth generation of those who hate me,"*
>
> — Exodus 20:5 (ESV)

And again, when God describes his own character to Moses after the golden calf.

> *"keeping steadfast love for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, but who will by no means clear the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers on the children and the children's children, to the third and the fourth generation."*
>
> — Exodus 34:7 (ESV)

Read plainly, it sounds like a mechanism, and the person who says so is not being foolish. But slow down. In the first passage the sentence carries a qualifier, "those who hate me," and in both it is dwarfed by mercy. Steadfast love goes to thousands. Judgment goes to three or four. The same formula comes back in Numbers 14:18, in the very chapter where Israel refuses to enter the land and God sentences that generation to forty years in the wilderness. The children do suffer for their parents' refusal. They spend their childhoods in the desert. No demon needs to be assigned to a bloodline for that to happen. A family goes over the cliff when the people steering it drive it there, and everyone in the back seat goes over too.

What does "third and fourth generation" actually mean? The Old Testament scholar Rodney Duke (2015) argues that the phrase may be an idiom for the whole family living together at one time, since an ancient household commonly held three or four generations under one roof within a lifespan of seventy or eighty years (Psalm 90:10). On that reading the verse describes how one person's sin spills over a whole household at once. It does not describe guilt stored up and handed down the centuries like an heirloom. Duke also reads "those who hate me" as describing a settled disposition against God, an ongoing disobedience, and not one act. And he notes, fairly, that this qualifier is missing from Exodus 34 and Numbers 14, which is why the collective dimension of Israel's life still has to be explained and not waved away. I will come back to that.

Meanwhile the Law itself blocks the misreading. Deuteronomy 24:16 is plain.

> *"Fathers shall not be put to death because of their children, nor shall children be put to death because of their fathers."*
>
> *"Each one shall be put to death for his own sin."*
>
> — Deuteronomy 24:16 (ESV)

The historian of Judah's kings goes out of his way to say that Amaziah obeyed this when he executed his father's murderers and spared their sons (2 Kings 14:6). So Israel's courts could not treat a child as guilty of a father's crime, and Israel's prophets and kings knew the rule. The same Moses who wrote Exodus 34 wrote Deuteronomy 24. He did not think he was contradicting himself, which should make us slower than we are to think we have caught him.

There are texts that look harder, and a fair reader should name them. Achan's sin at Jericho brought death to his household (Joshua 7). Jeremiah 32:18 speaks of God repaying the guilt of fathers into the lap of their children after them. Jesus tells the leaders of his day that the blood of all the prophets will be required of "this generation" (Matthew 23:35 to 36). Israel was a covenant people, a body in which the head's act touched the members. These texts show corporate consequence, shared fortune and shared accountability inside a community, and they sit beside, not against, the plain rule that a person is judged for his own sin. I'd put the whole matter this way. The Bible teaches that sin has consequences that run along family lines. It does not teach that those consequences are a spiritual debt the next generation inherits as guilt. The first claim is observation. The second is a different doctrine, and the text won't carry it.

## Four Hundred Years Before the Promise

There is a story that nearly always goes missing from conversations about family suffering, and it may be the most clarifying of all.

In Genesis 15, God makes a covenant with Abram. The scene is sacred and strange, with animals cut in two in the dark and a smoking fire pot passing between the pieces. Then, in the middle of this holy encounter, God says something that sounds at first like a curse.

> *"Know for certain that your offspring will be sojourners in a land that is not theirs and will be servants there, and they will be afflicted for four hundred years."*
>
> — Genesis 15:13 (ESV)

Four hundred years of affliction, announced to the man God had just promised to bless. Read three verses further to see how it fits.

> *"And they shall come back here in the fourth generation, for the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete."*
>
> — Genesis 15:16 (ESV)

Notice what is and isn't said. Nothing in the text says Abraham's descendants were being punished for anything. God is keeping a schedule. He is waiting while the nations around Canaan fill up their own record, and during that time the family of Abraham is becoming a people. Egypt was a hard place to become one. But Israel left it with an identity, a memory of what oppression feels like, a cry that God had heard, and a story the whole region later told. The plagues of Egypt were not local news.

I want to be careful here, because there is a cheap version of this point. Some preachers tell every suffering family that their pain is a secret calling, and that is a cruelty of its own. The slavery in Genesis 15 was announced in advance, in a covenant, with a promised end and a named judgment on the oppressor. Most family pain comes with no such notice. Still, the passage permits a question that the fear of curses never asks. The question is not only "why is this happening to my family?" but also "what is God doing with us in it, and what does he mean to make of the people who come out the other side?" Joseph says something close when he tells his brothers that what they meant for evil God meant for good (Genesis 50:20). The suffering was real, the sin was real, and God was not absent from either.

## Ezekiel and the Proverb About Sour Grapes

If Exodus is the passage the fear leans on, Ezekiel 18 is the answer fearful readers seldom visit.

The exiles in Babylon had a saying that put the blame behind them. The fathers have eaten sour grapes, they said, and the children's teeth are set on edge. Jeremiah quotes the same proverb (31:29 to 30), and promises a day when everyone will die for his own iniquity (Deuteronomy 24:16 is echoed in 31:30). The saying was true to life and a perfect excuse. Our fathers did it, so we are the victims. God's reply through Ezekiel is to forbid them the proverb, and then he sets out the principle.

> *"Behold, all souls are mine; the soul of the father as well as the soul of the son is mine: the soul who sins shall die."*
>
> — Ezekiel 18:4 (ESV)

Then comes the part people forget. The chapter does not leave this abstract. It walks through three generations, a righteous man, his violent son, and that man's son who sees his father's sins and does not do likewise, and it declares in each case that each one stands or falls on his own. The famous summary comes at the end of the argument.

> *"The soul who sins shall die. The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father, nor the father suffer for the iniquity of the son. The righteousness of the righteous shall be upon himself, and the wickedness of the wicked shall be upon himself."*
>
> — Ezekiel 18:20 (ESV)

Read the whole of it and the sweet side shows. A wicked man who turns away from his sins and keeps God's statutes shall surely live (verse 21). God has no pleasure in the death of anyone, and the chapter closes with an appeal, "turn, and live" (verse 32). The chapter is an invitation as much as a ruling, and a person who stood in the line of three bad generations is told that his past does not have the last word.

Note who is being corrected. The people God rebukes were using family history as a reason to put off repenting. Keep that in mind when a minister tells a whole congregation that their trouble comes down from the grandfathers. John Calvin read the passage exactly this way. In his lecture on Ezekiel 18 he treats the proverb as a complaint against God, as if he were punishing the innocent, and he says God's oath that it will no longer be used takes away the excuse and makes their own guilt plain. The proverb was comfortable because it put the cause far away and the cure in someone else's hands. Anyone who has sat through a family meeting where every sentence began with "it was my uncle" or "it was the village" will recognise the appeal. God's refusal to let them keep it was not unkind. It was the first step toward giving them something they could do.

Jesus meets the same instinct in John 9. His disciples see a man born blind and ask whose sin caused it, his own or his parents'. They have inherited the idea that every misfortune is a ledger entry somewhere in the family. Jesus answers it.

> *"It was not that this man sinned, or his parents, but that the works of God might be displayed in him."*
>
> — John 9:3 (ESV)

I would not claim that this verse settles every case. It certainly settles that the disciples' reflex was wrong, and that a good deal of suffering has no cause anyone can trace to a sinner, living or dead.

## Confessing the Sins of the Fathers

Here is an objection from the other side, and it is a good one. If sins don't pass down as guilt, why do Israel's best men confess their fathers' sins as if they were their own? The line is in the Law itself. Leviticus 26:40 promises restoration to those who confess "their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers." Jeremiah says, "We acknowledge our wickedness, O Lord, and the iniquity of our fathers, for we have sinned against you" (14:20). Nehemiah 9:2 describes the Israelites standing and confessing their sins and the iniquities of their fathers. Daniel 9 is the great example.

> *"To us, O Lord, belongs open shame, to our kings, to our princes, and to our fathers, because we have sinned against you."*
>
> — Daniel 9:8 (ESV)

Does this mean a curse sat on the family until the confession was made? Look at what the confessors say. Daniel begins with "we have sinned and done wrong and acted wickedly and rebelled" (9:5). The psalmist says, "Both we and our fathers have sinned" (Psalm 106:6). They are not praying as innocent victims of a stranger's debt. They are praying as members of a people who have shared the same sin and are sharing the same exile. The word is "we." Daniel, whom the Bible never accuses of idolatry, puts himself inside the confession because he lives inside the people.

Lamentations 5:7 is the sharpest verse of all.

> *"Our fathers sinned, and are no more; and we bear their iniquities."*
>
> — Lamentations 5:7 (ESV)

It is a raw sentence, and I would not smooth it. The poet is living in the ruins of Jerusalem, and what he sees is that children are paying for what their parents did. He does not say they are guilty of it. He says they are bearing it, carrying the weight, and he says it as a complaint brought to God in prayer, which ends with a plea for restoration (5:21). Bearing the iniquities, in the sense of living under the consequences, is exactly what I said earlier is real. It does not turn into a doctrine of inherited guilt, and the next move the Bible makes after it is to pray.

So what does this teach a Christian family today? It gives us leave to be honest about what our families did. If your grandfather's land was got by fraud, if your family kept an idol shrine, if your household's prosperity was built on a crooked contract, you can say so to God without pretending it isn't there. You can ask forgiveness for what you have shared in, renounce what you are still practising, and forgive what was done to you. What Scripture does not give is a formula that unlocks a curse by naming the right ancestors in the right order. No prophet instructs the exiles to reach back and break the claim of a named forebear. They confess, they turn, and they ask for mercy on the ground of God's covenant love. That is prayer in the ordinary sense. It can be done in a living room with a friend, and nobody needs to be paid.

## When Manasseh's Sin Still Brought Exile

An honest reader will press harder, and the objection is a strong one. Ezekiel's principle sounds neat, but the Old Testament itself describes Judah suffering for the sin of a dead king. The writer of 2 Kings says exactly that, and puts it after Josiah's reforms.

> *"Still the Lord did not turn from the burning of his great wrath, by which his anger was kindled against Judah, because of all the provocations with which Manasseh had provoked him."*
>
> — 2 Kings 23:26 (ESV)

Manasseh had been dead for over fifty years when Judah fell, and yet a long dead king is blamed for the exile. That sits awkwardly beside a Bible that teaches individual accountability.

I think the answer is that Scripture holds two things together, and we are always tempted to keep only one. A nation or a family is a body. What the leaders do shapes what everybody inherits: the land, the habits, the enemies, the debts. Those consequences are real, and they can last for generations. Anyone who has watched a family land dispute pass from one brother to his sons, and from them to their children, knows how a quarrel started by a dead man can still be fought at the same fence line. Anyone who has lived under decades of bad government knows it too. But being caught in the fallout is a different thing from being guilty before God. Ezekiel preached to exiles who were suffering because of a nation's sins, and he still told every one of them that his own standing before God would be decided by his own response.

And here the story turns, because the man the exile was blamed on did not stay where the writer of Kings left him. The Chronicler tells what Kings leaves out.

> *"And when he was in distress, he entreated the favor of the Lord his God and humbled himself greatly before the God of his fathers. He prayed to him, and God was moved by his entreaty and heard his plea and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom. Then Manasseh knew that the Lord was God."*
>
> — 2 Chronicles 33:12 to 13 (ESV)

Sit with that for a moment. Manasseh rebuilt the high places his father Hezekiah had torn down. He served Baal and Asherah and the host of heaven, practised sorcery and divination, dealt with mediums, set an idol in the house of the Lord, and according to 2 Kings 21 and 2 Chronicles 33 burned his own son in the fire and filled Jerusalem with innocent blood. The Bible presents him as among the worst men ever to hold a throne. And he was forgiven. His generation's consequences stayed. But the man himself was not stuck. If Manasseh could be reached, I don't know why a man in Ibadan with a bad temper and an uncle's bad luck should be told he is beyond reach.

That is the turn this essay rests on. The family pattern is real, the consequences travel, and the door of repentance stays open the whole time. Not for a favoured few with the right minister. For anyone.

## Josiah and the Father He Chose

Manasseh's son Amon did not repent. The Chronicler says he incurred guilt more and more, and then his own servants killed him (2 Chronicles 33:23 to 24). His son Josiah was eight when he came to the throne. Hold the whole line in view for a moment, because it carries an argument. Hezekiah was godly and his son Manasseh was a monster. Manasseh repented late and his son Amon did not. Amon was wicked and his son Josiah was the best king Judah had seen since David. Then Josiah, whom Jeremiah praises for doing justice and righteousness, left sons who did evil. Likewise Samuel's sons "did not walk in his ways" (1 Samuel 8:3). If there were a mechanical curse that flowed down bloodlines, this record would look different. Wickedness fails to pass down on a schedule, and godliness fails to pass down on a schedule too. Each generation has to choose, and the Bible says so in the way it tells the story.

The Chronicler gives Josiah a clear starting point for a spiritual life, and it is worth reading closely.

> *"For in the eighth year of his reign, while he was yet a boy, he began to seek the God of David his father,"*
>
> — 2 Chronicles 34:3 (ESV)

The phrase is standard for the Old Testament writers, who often speak of David as the father of the kings who followed him, and I wouldn't build a whole doctrine on the wording. But the effect is hard to miss. This boy had a father who did evil and a grandfather who did worse, and the text points past both of them to David. Four years later, at twenty, he began to clear Judah of its idols. In the eighth year of his reign he was sixteen, and he was already looking for God with nobody at home to copy. That detail has always moved me more than the revival that followed. Most of us can follow a good example once somebody has set one. It is harder, and rarer, to start from nothing. 2 Kings 22:2 says he did what was right in the eyes of the Lord and walked in all the way of David his father, turning aside neither to the right nor to the left.

A correction belongs here, because it is commonly told otherwise. It is often said that Josiah, as a teenager, found the lost scroll of the law and was transformed overnight. The text puts the discovery in his eighteenth year as king (2 Kings 22:3), which makes him about twenty six. The seeking and the purge came first. The reading of the book deepened a faith that was already moving, and I think that is the more encouraging order, because it means Josiah began with very little and God met him there.

Josiah's reign also raises a thought about Daniel, and I'll mark it as a thought. Daniel and his three friends were taken from Jerusalem to Babylon in the reign of Jehoiakim, Josiah's son, a few years after Josiah's death, and they were young when they went. In Daniel 9:21, in exile and with the temple in ruins, Daniel still marks his prayer by the time of the evening sacrifice. It is attractive to say that the backbone of those four young men came from the revival Josiah led, and it is plausible that a boy raised in Jerusalem in those years absorbed something of it. But no text says so. The evening offering was a regular part of temple life under other kings as well, and the boys were small children when Josiah died. I hold the idea loosely, and I would not preach it as fact. What I would preach is what is recorded: one young man in a bad family decided to seek God, and a generation later other young men in a far worse place still knew how to pray.

Josiah could not stop the exile. He did not turn the nation back. But what is recorded of him is that he did not follow his father, and nothing in the record suggests he could not have chosen otherwise. That is also the answer to the question Jewish and Christian readers have long asked about him, whether a king's repentance matters if the judgment is coming anyway. God's own commentary on Josiah's tender heart in 2 Kings 22:19 to 20 says it mattered to God.

## Balaam and a Curse That Could Not Land

Another piece of evidence deserves its place here, because spoken curses are what many people really mean, and the Bible has a story about one.

In Numbers 22 to 24, the king of Moab, Balak, hires a pagan diviner called Balaam with one assignment, to curse Israel. Balak had watched Israel defeat kingdom after kingdom, and he believed the only way to stop a people like that was a supernatural one. So he brought in the best man for the job. Balak took him to three different hilltops, built altars, and tried again each time. What came out was blessing, and the fourth oracle was no kinder to Balak than the first three. Balaam could not do it, and not for lack of will. His own words are that he can speak only what God puts in his mouth.

Moses later gives God's commentary.

> *"But the Lord your God would not listen to Balaam; instead the Lord your God turned the curse into a blessing for you, because the Lord your God loved you."*
>
> — Deuteronomy 23:5 (ESV)

A professionally commissioned, ritually prepared, earnestly spoken curse was turned round before it could land, and the mechanism was love. Proverbs 26:2 says the principle in a line, that a curse without a cause does not alight. This is a very comforting text, and I'd only add the honest second half. The people who got hurt in that story were not hurt by Balaam. The next chapter, Numbers 25, tells how Israel went to Baal Peor and joined itself to the worship there, and a plague followed, and Numbers 31:16 says Balaam's counsel lay behind it. What Balaam could not do by speaking, Israel did to itself by sinning. If you want to know what is truly dangerous to a believing family, it is rarely a stranger's words. It is the open door of our own conduct.

The Bible's most abused curse deserves a mention too, because Africans know what it cost. Genesis 9:25 records Noah saying "Cursed be Canaan." The curse falls on Canaan, Ham's son, and not on Ham, and not on any African people. Yet for centuries it was twisted into a licence for slavery, as David Goldenberg (2003) documents in his history of how that story was read by Jews, Christians and Muslims. A generational curse theology that treats a family's bad luck as a standing sentence is not a harmless cousin of that reading. It is the same habit of mind, finding a hidden sentence in a text the text never wrote.

## What Christ Did About the Curse

Everything so far was said in the Old Testament, and some readers will reasonably say that Christians stand somewhere else. They do, and the place is better.

Paul has a sentence in Galatians that matters here. The curse he means is the one the Law pronounced on those who broke it, set out in Deuteronomy. He writes that Christ took it on himself.

> *"Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us"*
>
> — Galatians 3:13 (ESV)

The verse goes on to quote Deuteronomy 21:23 about the one hanged on a tree, and the next verse gives the purpose, that the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles in Christ Jesus. Paul isn't speaking directly about family patterns, and I would be overreaching to say he is. His subject is the covenant curse on law breakers. But the logic travels. If the worst curse in Scripture was borne by Christ on the cross, then a believer has no remaining sentence hanging over him from his grandfather's sins. Nobody is more cursed than the one on that tree, and he took it so that we would not.

Colossians says the same from another side, about the powers that stand behind such claims.

> *"He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."*
>
> — Colossians 2:15 (ESV)

The verb Paul uses means to strip off, and translators render it "disarmed," which is the picture of a defeated enemy having his weapons and armour taken away. In the preceding verses Paul says that God cancelled the record of debt that stood against us, nailing it to the cross (2:14). A power that has been disarmed can still shout, and a liar who cannot show a document still cannot collect. Jesus said something like this about himself when he answered the charge that he drove out demons by Satan. You cannot plunder a strong man's house, he said, unless you first bind the strong man (Matthew 12:29). The binding was done. What the church does now is walk through a house that has already been entered.

The strongest objection to everything I have said comes from within Christian theology itself. Doesn't the church teach that all of us inherit Adam's sin? If Adam's guilt reaches me, why can't my grandfather's? The objection is serious and the answer needs care. The great traditions say different things about what exactly passes down from Adam. The Reformed churches speak of Adam as the covenant head of the human race, whose guilt is counted to all his descendants, which is the doctrine called imputation. The Catholic Church teaches that original sin is transmitted by propagation to all mankind (Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 404), a sin contracted and not personally committed. Eastern Orthodox teaching speaks of an ancestral sin and tends to put the weight on inherited mortality and corruption more than on inherited guilt. Those differences matter a great deal in other conversations.

But on this question the traditions agree on two things. First, Adam's case is unique. He is the one head of the whole human race, and Paul says so in Romans 5 in a passage that is about Adam and Christ, not about Adam and Abraham, or Adam and your grandfather. No other forebear stands where Adam stands. Second, the very same passage says the remedy is as wide as the damage. Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more (Romans 5:20), and as one man's trespass led to condemnation for all, so one man's act of righteousness leads to justification and life (Romans 5:18). Augustine, who shaped the Western doctrine of original sin more than anyone, read Exodus together with Ezekiel's promise in chapter 46 of his Enchiridion. He understood Ezekiel to speak of a day when sons would no longer bear their fathers' iniquity, and he found the answer in the new birth. A person is freed from the sin he was born into by being born again, he said, and later sins are dealt with by repentance. Whatever you think of the rest of Augustine, he did not tell Christians to go looking for a second hidden debt.

That is the shape of the good news here. You do have an inherited problem, and every human being does. You also have a second Head. Union with Christ is not a metaphor for good vibes. It is the New Testament's way of saying that your standing before God is now decided by where you are placed in him, and not by who your people were. Romans 8:15 and Ephesians 1:5 call this adoption. You receive a new family, a new name and a new inheritance.

The Ghanaian theologian Kwame Bediako saw something of this in his own context. According to Christianity Today's 2024 account of his essays, Bediako argued from Hebrews that Christ is the elder brother who fills the role that African traditional religion had given to the ancestors as mediators, and that conversion means turning to Christ with the whole of one's being and not abandoning one's heritage. Not everyone has agreed with him, and critics like Byang Kato, the first African general secretary of the Association of Evangelicals of Africa, feared that any linking of African religion and the gospel would end in syncretism. I find the debate useful because both men are asking what a person owes his ancestors once he belongs to Christ. The New Testament's answer is honour, memory and love for the living family, and no second allegiance for the unseen dead. The mediator between God and man is one, the man Christ Jesus.

## Real Patterns and Honest Help

None of that makes the patterns imaginary.

Anyone who has watched a family for a few decades has seen them. Drinking runs down a family line. So do violence, infidelity, debt and silence. In Nigeria the evidence about violence is stark. A national survey of violence against children, carried out by the National Population Commission with UNICEF and the US Centers for Disease Control and reported in 2016, found that about one in two children experienced physical violence and six in ten experienced violence of some kind before the age of eighteen (TheCable, n.d.). It is a safe bet that a good part of this is learned, since people who were beaten hold a model of how a child is raised and hand it down with the best intentions. Nobody needs a curse to explain it, and a curse does not explain it better.

Part of what runs in families is learned by watching. A boy who has never seen anyone in his family finish a degree, keep a promise to a wife or save a naira has no picture of how it is done, and the lack of a picture can pass for destiny. Part is circumstance, since poverty has its own momentum, and the family that cannot pay school fees this year will find it harder next year. Some of it may be inherited in the body, because some temperaments and some illnesses run in families. And part is what Paul calls strongholds. I should correct the way the word is sometimes used. Paul's strongholds in 2 Corinthians 10:4 to 5 are arguments and lofty opinions raised against the knowledge of God, ideas that must be taken captive to obey Christ. The word is nearer to a fortified way of thinking than to a demon owning a bloodline. But the analogy is a useful one for habits of sin that have been fortified by repetition until they feel like destiny, and the New Testament's answer to a fortress is the one Paul gives, tear it down with the truth.

The Bible knows all this. It just doesn't tell the person in that position that he has a spiritual debt on his account. What it offers is practical, and very close to what Josiah did. Name the pattern honestly. Confess what is yours, and forgive what was done to you. Tell somebody, and tell them early. If the pattern is alcohol, anger or depression, see a doctor or a counsellor in the same week as you see your pastor, because a man can take his drinking to prayer and to a clinic on the same Friday, and no minister worth hearing would say otherwise. Then decide who you are going to follow, as Josiah did, and keep deciding it.

About deliverance culture, then, I'd offer a test, not a verdict. Prayer for freedom, with people who love you and know you, is something the New Testament would recognise. Hear what the ministry says about the cross. If the message is that Christ finished it and you are learning to walk in that, you are in reasonably safe company. If the message is that something is still owed, that you need a special session, a special oil, a named relative to be confronted, or a larger offering, then it has quietly added to the work of Christ, and Colossians 2:15 is the answer to it. Nobody should be told that their grandmother is the reason for their trouble on the strength of a vision at a night programme, and nobody should be pointed at an elderly relative on that basis. The Bible has no tradition of accusing the old woman in the compound. It has a long one of loving her. And nobody should be told that the only help available is for sale.

## Whether a Christian Can Be Possessed

The second question deserves honest treatment, because believers disagree about it and always have.

Begin with the word. The Gospels use a Greek verb, *daimonizomai*, that the King James Version and many later translations render as "possessed with a devil" or "possessed by a demon." It occurs thirteen times, all in the Gospels, and means to be under the power of a demon. Sam Storms, a Reformed charismatic theologian, prefers "demonised" because "possession" implies ownership and the Bible never uses the phrase. Grimsley and Miller (1993) in the *Christian Research Journal* reply that the lexicons support "possessed," and that the real question is where the demon is in relation to the person. I don't think the dispute can be settled by one word. So let me use the three senses of the English word *possess* as a way to sort what the Bible actually says.

To possess can mean to own. A demon owning a Christian is plainly out of the question. Believers have been transferred out of the domain of darkness into the kingdom of the Son (Colossians 1:13 to 14). Nothing in creation, angels and demons included, can separate them from the love of God in Christ (Romans 8:38 to 39). The question beneath so many anxious prayers, "can I lose my salvation to a demon?", is really whether God can lose a Christian, and Jesus says of his sheep that no one will snatch them out of his hand (John 10:28). On this definition a Christian cannot be possessed. On that, careful readers agree.

To possess can also mean to occupy. Here the disagreement begins, and I want to state both sides at full strength. Storms argues that a born again believer could possibly be demonised in the sense of indwelt by a demon, since demonisation always entails indwelling in his view, and he finds the standard objections unpersuasive, because the Holy Spirit already coexists with sinful human nature, and the "cannot" in texts like 2 Corinthians 6:14 to 16 is moral and not metaphysical. Fred Dickason, the author of a well known defence of this view, admits that the Bible gives no conclusive proof and rests part of his case on what he has seen in pastoral work, according to Grimsley and Miller. The opposite side, argued by Grimsley and Miller and by Randy Alcorn (2010), holds that a believer cannot be inhabited or controlled by a demon because the Holy Spirit lives in him, though he can be oppressed, tempted and harassed, and that the New Testament tells believers to resist the devil and never to cast demons out of fellow Christians.

Both sides can point to texts. Jesus said of a woman bent double for eighteen years, a daughter of Abraham, that Satan had bound her (Luke 13:16), which suggests that a child of the covenant could be afflicted. Peter asks Ananias why Satan filled his heart to lie to the Holy Spirit (Acts 5:3). Paul tells the Ephesians not to give the devil a foothold, literally a place (Ephesians 4:27). Those who deny indwelling answer that none of these texts says a demon lived in the person, and that the New Testament's repeated answer to the enemy is to stand firm, resist, and put on the armour of God (Ephesians 6; James 4:7).

I find the picture of the open back door useful, though like every picture it limps. You own the house and have the deed. But if you leave the door open night after night and invite in people who have no business there, they can occupy space in your home, as squatters, with no legal right and every practical presence. The title is secure. The house is still under pressure. A believer's standing before God is settled, and yet a believer who keeps open doors to occult practice, or bitterness, or long habits of sin, gives the enemy room to work. The authority to close the door was never lost. Jesus was raised and seated far above all rule and authority and power and dominion, and God put all things under his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church (Ephesians 1:20 to 22). That is the ground on which a believer stands, and it is always greater than whatever came in at the back.

To possess can finally mean to take on the characteristics of something. This is the sense in which the New Testament speaks most often. When Jesus says to Peter in Matthew 16:23, "Get behind me, Satan," he is not performing an exorcism on the chief apostle. Peter had just said, "This shall never happen to you," and the words did not come from God. Some readers take it that Jesus addressed the spirit behind the words, and others that he was naming Peter's mind as for that moment aligned with the adversary. Either way, a man who loved the Lord was speaking in a way that served the other side, because he had not yet understood the cross. It is a sober warning, and I do not mean it as a cause for panic. It is a reason for vigilance.

So where do I land? I lean toward the view that a Christian cannot be inhabited by a demon, that he can be oppressed and tempted and deceived, and that a believer who gives ground to sin or the occult gives the enemy a place to work and may need help to take it back. I would not call a person who holds the other view a fool, and I would not let anyone use my caution as a reason to turn away someone in torment. What both views keep is what matters most. The believer is not owned, is not helpless, and does not need to wait for a specialist. Ephesians 6 calls for the whole armour of God for a reason, since opposition is real. But a soldier in armour is a different thing from a prisoner waiting for a key.

## Grace Reaches Further Than the Pattern

Go back to the verse where the fear began. Judgment to the third and fourth generation. Steadfast love to thousands. In Deuteronomy 7:9 the thousands become a thousand generations for those who love God and keep his commandments, and in Romans 5:20 Paul says that where sin increased, grace abounded all the more. The Greek verb there means to overflow beyond measure. It would be silly to turn the figures into a sum, but the direction is not in doubt. God reaches further than any pattern, and he does it again in every generation.

So the man in Ibadan has a choice his father never took and his grandfather probably never heard. He doesn't have to pretend the pattern isn't there. He also doesn't have to carry it as a verdict. He can tell the truth about it, and then he can come to the Father in the one way that has always been open, through the Son who took the curse and rose from the dead.

That is the pastoral centre of all this, and it is personal. There are people reading this in villages and cities, in homes where the ancestors are feared and in homes where they are simply ignored, who have spent years looking at their family tree and concluding, quietly: *I am probably going to end up like him. This is just what our family does. Maybe I am cursed.* The Bible says you have a new Father. That is not a figure of speech. Josiah did not sit down in despair because his father was Amon and his grandfather Manasseh. He looked past the wreckage, found David, and sought the God of David. You can do the same, only the Father you come to is not David but the one David's greater Son revealed. The cycle can end with you. The stronghold can fall in your generation. The faith that was lost in your family can be found and passed forward.

If you have never put your faith in Jesus, you don't have to sort out your family history first. Nobody does. You don't arrive clean and then receive Christ. You come to Christ because you can't clean yourself, and he makes you new. Paul puts the invitation simply in Romans 10:9, that if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. Start there.

And this week, if you can, find one person you trust and tell them about the pattern you are afraid of. Say it plainly, to a pastor or an older friend, and ask them to pray with you and not to charge you for it. Then see whether the fear is still as large as it was. Fear grows best in private, and it tends to shrink when it is said aloud among people who love you.
