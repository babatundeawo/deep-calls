## A Chair in a Teaching Interview

Picture a teacher candidate giving a demonstration lesson in a school hall, with the panel at the back and a class of bored students in front. The topic is matter. She keeps it simple. Matter, she says, is anything that has mass and takes up space. To make it real she points at an ordinary classroom chair, four legs and a backrest, the sort that has held a thousand half asleep students through a thousand morning lectures. It has mass. It takes up space. And unless some force moves it, it stays exactly where it is.

The class nods. Nothing in the lesson is dramatic, which is why a remark like that can go off later. Some explanations are slow. You take them in calmly, walk home, and hours afterwards they begin to work on your thinking.

Now picture the same evening. A forwarded message goes past on WhatsApp, a line from a preacher that has done the rounds in Pentecostal circles for a while: *"Learn to confront the devil from your space because he has lost his place."* I couldn't trace who first said it, so I won't put a name to it. It's a good line, and if you've just been thinking about chairs, a thought arrives uninvited. The chair is still in the room. The devil, supposedly, has left it.

That is the argument I want to test. I think much of it is true and that it helps people. I also think it is stated a little too neatly, and the neatness can leave a tired believer feeling cheated when the enemy plainly hasn't gone quiet. So let's give it its best case first, then put it under examination and see what survives.

## The Argument at Its Strongest

Here is how a good preacher would build it, and he would have the Bible on his side for more of it than you might expect.

Start with the definition. Matter has mass and occupies space, and two ordinary objects cannot occupy the same space at the same time. You can't sit on a chair that someone else is sitting on. Carry that into Scripture. There was a created being who wanted a place that wasn't his, and Isaiah's taunt against the king of Babylon, which the church has long heard as an echo of the devil's own fall, has five "I wills" in a row.

> *"I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will make myself like the Most High."*
>
> — Isaiah 14:14 (ESV)

Then Jesus, when the seventy two came back delighted that even demons were subject to them, said this.

> *"I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven."*
>
> — Luke 10:18 (ESV)

Lightning doesn't drift. It goes from where it is to where it isn't. The preacher would add that Revelation 12 describes a war in heaven in which Michael and his angels fought the dragon, and that the dragon was defeated and "there was no longer any place for them in heaven." There is the line itself. He has lost his place.

Then comes Paul, who says that God raised believers with Christ and seated them with him in the heavenly places (Ephesians 2:6). The letter to the Hebrews supplies the posture. The old priests stood, day after day, because the work was never done, but Christ, having offered one sacrifice for sins for all time, sat down (Hebrews 10:11 to 12). A judge who sits has finished deliberating. A king who sits has asserted his reign. So when the preacher says "confront the devil from your space", he means that you don't fight to win a position. You fight from one already won, and you enforce downward an authority already granted.

I think that is a strong case. Each of its legs is a real text. Now to see where it holds and where it doesn't.

## Where the Physics Gives Out

The chair starts to wobble first at the physics, and I'll say so plainly because sermons usually say it with a straight face.

The textbook definition does the devil an unexpected favour. Matter has mass and takes up space. A spirit has neither. Jesus himself drew the line when the disciples thought they had seen a ghost: "a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have" (Luke 24:39, ESV), and John says that "God is spirit" (John 4:24). Angels, fallen or not, are spirits in this sense. By the very definition that started the conversation, the devil is not matter, and the neat proof falls over. The claim that he has "forfeited his matter hood" is a good joke. It is not an argument.

Even within physics, the principle is less tidy than it sounds. The rule that two objects can't share a place holds firmly for the stuff of chairs and people. It doesn't hold in the same way for light, where countless photons can pile into one region. A seat is a good picture of a place that can be taken. It is a poor picture of a being whose kind of presence we do not know how to measure.

So the physics can't prove the point. That doesn't make the point false. It means that if the claim is to stand, it has to stand on the Bible's own categories, which are legal and relational (place, standing, access, accusation) and not spatial. The question to ask is what exactly the devil lost. The slogan answers "his place", and I'd like to look at that more closely.

## What He Lost and What He Kept

Take the losses first, because they are real. Before the cross, Satan appears in the heavenly court in the book of Job as one who comes in among the sons of God and is given a hearing. He is the accuser, and Zechariah shows him standing at the right hand of the high priest to accuse him, until the Lord says, "The Lord rebuke you." After the cross, John hears a voice in heaven.

> *"The accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them day and night before our God."*
>
> — Revelation 12:10 (ESV)

That is the loss. His standing as the one who brings the charge, with the record to back it, has been taken from him, because Paul says the record of debt was cancelled and "nailed to the cross" and the rulers disarmed (Colossians 2:14 to 15). Jesus said it ahead of time. "Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out" (John 12:31, ESV). The writer of Hebrews says that Christ became flesh to "destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14, ESV). And John says the Son of God appeared "to destroy the works of the devil" (1 John 3:8, ESV). The preacher is right. Something decisive was taken from him.

Now the other side of the ledger, which the slogan tends to skip. The same Bible calls him a lion on the prowl, the god of this age who blinds unbelievers (2 Corinthians 4:4), and says that the whole world lies in the power of the evil one (1 John 5:19). Revelation 12 doesn't end with a tidy eviction. It says that when he was thrown down, he came down in great fury because he knew his time was short (Revelation 12:12). A defeated enemy can be a furious one. And Paul gives the most awkward text for the slogan.

> *"For we do not wrestle against flesh and blood, but against the rulers, against the authorities, against the cosmic powers over this present darkness, against the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly places."*
>
> — Ephesians 6:12 (ESV)

Same letter, same phrase. Believers are seated "in the heavenly places" (Ephesians 2:6), and the forces of evil are in the heavenly places too. So it can't be that the enemy has been cleared out of the space where we sit, because Paul has him in the same neighbourhood. What the New Testament says is subtler, and I think better. His charge against us has been cancelled. His authority to condemn has been broken. His access as accuser has been closed. His power to harass, tempt, deceive and frighten has been limited and not removed, and it will be removed at the end.

I should say a word about Isaiah 14, because the popular argument leans on it. Tertullian and Origen heard the devil's voice in that speech, and so did generations of preachers. But the passage introduces itself as a taunt against the king of Babylon, and many careful commentators, from Calvin to John Oswalt, with Daniel Block on the parallel in Ezekiel 28, read it as about human kings and the pride of empire (summarised in Spriggs, n.d.). Some allow a darker power behind such rulers. I don't think we should rest the argument on it. It isn't needed. Luke 10:18, John 12:31 and Colossians 2:15 say all the sermon wants to say, and they were not written to a king of Babylon. Even Luke 10:18 is debated. The Greek verb can mean "I was watching", so commentators divide over whether Jesus describes an event before time, the downfall that the seventy two's success revealed, or the defeat won in his own ministry. All three agree on the direction of travel. The enemy's authority is going down.

## A Chain on the Lion

Now we come to what the original slogan rightly senses and the Bible says better. The devil is a creature, and creatures have limits. This is the single most comforting thing in the whole doctrine, and the book of Job states it before any New Testament text does.

> *"And the Lord said to Satan, 'Behold, all that he has is in your hand. Only against him do not stretch out your hand.'"*
>
> — Job 1:12 (ESV)

When Satan returns for a second round, the leash is longer, and it is still a leash.

> *"And the Lord said to Satan, 'Behold, he is in your hand; only spare his life.'"*
>
> — Job 2:6 (ESV)

Notice who is setting the terms. The devil needs permission, and he is given a boundary. A rival would have negotiated. A creature takes what it is allowed. The pattern repeats in the New Testament. Jesus says to Peter that Satan "demanded to have you, that he might sift you like wheat" (Luke 22:31, ESV), and then he tells Peter that he has prayed that his faith will not fail. The sifting was permitted, and the prayer was already in place. Paul tells the Corinthians of a thorn in the flesh that he calls a messenger of Satan, and the same sentence says it was given to him to keep him from pride (2 Corinthians 12:7). The devil's messenger is carrying out the purposes of God, which is why the answer Paul receives is about grace and not about a rebuke.

Even the most powerful angel kept his manners. Jude says that "the archangel Michael, contending with the devil, was disputing about the body of Moses", and that Michael did not dare pronounce a judgment of his own but said, "The Lord rebuke you" (Jude 9, ESV). The text I wish more of us would read slowly is Jude's lesson. If Michael wouldn't bring his own accusation against the devil, our cheerful insults from the prayer ground might need a second look.

This is where the Reformed tradition has done some of its best work, and it should be stated as its own holders would put it. The Heidelberg Catechism of 1563 begins by saying that Christ has "delivered me from all the power of the devil", and that "without the will of my heavenly Father, not a hair can fall from my head." Later it adds that all creatures are so in God's hand that without his will they cannot so much as move. Calvin took the same line. Leithart's summary of the *Institutes* says that Satan's power over people depends on God's judgment, and that Calvin used Augustine's picture of a horse with two riders, one guiding it wisely and the other driving it recklessly, to show that God and the devil are not equals in the saddle (Leithart, 2008). For the Reformed reader, the devil is real, he is malicious, and he is on a chain. It is a great comfort and it asks a great deal of trust, because it means that God permits what he could stop, for reasons he does not always give.

## A Lion Is Still a Lion

None of this makes him harmless. Peter, who knew what it was to be sifted, writes it directly.

> *"Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to devour."*
>
> — 1 Peter 5:8 (ESV)

And what Peter commands in response is not a speech at the lion. He says to be sober minded and watchful, and to resist him, firm in the faith. James has the same shape. "Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (James 4:7, ESV). Notice the order. You submit first, and you resist second, and the fleeing is his.

Paul wrote his great letter on the armour from prison, calling himself an ambassador in chains (Ephesians 6:20), and nobody could accuse him of naivety about spiritual opposition. He begins with "be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his might" (Ephesians 6:10, ESV), and the verbs that follow are about standing. A lion that has lost its claim to the territory is still a lion.

C. S. Lewis put the danger on both sides of the road in the preface to *The Screwtape Letters* (1942), signed in July 1941.

> *"There are two equal and opposite errors into which our race can fall about the devils. One is to disbelieve in their existence. The other is to believe, and to feel an excessive and unhealthy interest in them."*
>
> — C. S. Lewis, *The Screwtape Letters* (1942), Preface

Anyone who has spent time in Nigerian church life has met both errors, sometimes in the same household. There is the believer who treats every delayed result and every bad Tuesday as a demonic operation, and there is the cooler one who has quietly stopped believing in any of it. Neither has much to say to the person who is really being harassed.

## What African Christians Have Said

I would rather lean on what the African church has actually written. The Ghanaian theologian Kwame Bediako (1993) described how African Christians have come to see Jesus as supreme over the spirit realm, writing that "he is supreme over all 'gods' and authorities in the realm of the spirits." That conviction is older than the slogan and sturdier. It doesn't depend on a pun about matter. It depends on a resurrection.

The Nigerian church historian Ogbu Kalu, who died in 2009, argued that African Pentecostalism shares with the older African worldview a belief in an unseen world that touches the material one. Richard Burgess's study of Kalu reports his view that Pentecostals "address the spirits that govern the structures of the public sphere" and that prayer in this setting is a form of political practice (Burgess, 2019). The Pew Research Center's 2010 survey of sub Saharan Africa put it in one sentence, that large numbers of Africans actively participate in Christianity or Islam yet also believe in witchcraft, evil spirits and sacrifices to ancestors. So the person praying in Lagos or Enugu or Kano is not holding a fringe belief. This is the common ground of the continent.

Two Nigerian evangelical voices help us read it. J. Ayodeji Adewuya (2012) argues that African Pentecostal beliefs about spirits share real common ground with the world that Paul addresses in Ephesians 6, and he cautions in the same article against an uncritical application of Paul's teaching. Byang Kato, whose *Theological Pitfalls in Africa* (1975) shaped a generation, was no sceptic about the spirit world, and a paper on his work notes that his understanding of salvation includes deliverance from demons as well as from sin. He was also fierce about syncretism, as Paul Bowers summarised, refusing to let traditional religion count as an authoritative source of revelation or of salvation (Bowers, 1981).

And it is worth being fair to both camps. A Pentecostal reading says that Jesus gave his disciples authority over the demons. We saw it in Luke 10, where he says, "I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy", and the early church used it, casting out a spirit in the name of Jesus (Acts 16:18). A Reformed or cautious evangelical reading replies that the letters never tell believers to address the powers by name or to bind territorial spirits, and that their commands are to submit, resist, stand and pray. Each has a point. Both would agree that the authority is Christ's and not ours, and Luke 10:20, which comes straight after the verse the first camp loves, says "do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven." The joy is moved somewhere safer.

## Seated and Standing

Take the chair seriously for a moment longer, because the Bible does.

The priests stood because there was always more to do. Christ sat because the work was finished. When Paul says we are seated with him, he is telling believers where they are fighting from. And yes, a puzzle appears here that you have probably noticed. In the same letter the people who are seated are told, four chapters later, to stand: to put on the armour and stand firm against the schemes of the devil. I don't think that is a contradiction. We sit for our position and we stand for our posture. A person who knows she belongs to the winner stands differently from one who thinks the result is still in doubt. She doesn't charge up the hill to win the right to be there. She holds ground that has already been paid for.

But there is a second thing about sitting that the slogan skips. Hebrews adds that Christ sat down and is "waiting from that time until his enemies should be made a footstool for his feet" (Hebrews 10:13). He is seated, and he is waiting. The decisive thing has been done, and the full outworking has not. Revelation 3:21 gives the same picture from the other side, where the risen Christ promises that the one who conquers will sit with him on his throne. So our seat is both a fact (Ephesians 2:6) and a promise (Revelation 3:21), and the days between are the days of Ephesians 6. The preacher who says "you are seated" is right. The believer who feels the strain of the wrestling is also right. They are describing the same Tuesday from two sides.

And from that seat one thing continues which the slogan leaves out. Paul says that Christ Jesus, who is at the right hand of God, "is interceding for us" (Romans 8:34). The seated Christ isn't resting while we fight. He is praying, as he did for Peter.

## For the Person Who Doesn't Feel Seated

This is the part where the theology has to become pastoral, because there is always someone reading who has heard the declarations and sung the songs and still feels the enemy winning. It's late on a Wednesday, the praise music has stopped, and the anxiety is louder than the sermon.

To that person I'd say three things. First, your position in Christ is not a mood. It doesn't rise and fall with how much you feel on a given evening, and it doesn't depend on how well you prayed this week. Peter's faith did not hold because it was strong. It held because Christ had prayed for it.

Second, feeling besieged is not a sign that you've failed to believe. Believers in every age have written about it honestly, and the psalms are full of it. Paul himself asked three times for the thorn to be taken away and was told that grace was enough (2 Corinthians 12:8 to 9). The thorn stayed, and so did the grace. If the strongest apostle in the New Testament spent years in that tension, you needn't conclude that you are failing.

Third, and I say this carefully because I know how it sounds in some churches, not everything that besieges you is a demon. If what you're facing is persistent anxiety, low mood, panic or sleeplessness, it is not unspiritual to see a doctor and talk to someone you trust, and it will not weaken your faith. Many Nigerian families have been told that every such trouble is a spiritual attack, and have gone months or years without help they could have had. Plenty of people are helped by prayer and by treatment together, and the two were never meant to compete. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, tell a person today and go to a hospital or a doctor. That is wisdom, and it is no betrayal of Christ.

Then something practical. When the accusing thought comes, the one that says you're too far gone and God has left the room, answer it out loud with a sentence Christ has already settled. Say that the record against you was nailed to the cross. Say that the Lord rebukes him. Do what Michael did, and don't try to out argue the accuser in your own name. Tell a fellow believer what you're hearing, because accusations lose a good deal of their force once they are said aloud to a friend.

## What Still Matters About the Devil

So does the devil matter? In one sense, no. He doesn't decide who you are, he can't revoke what Christ has done, and he is on a leash held by the one who loves you. In another sense, yes, and Peter doesn't let us forget it. He matters enough that Scripture tells you to be sober, to stand, to resist and to pray. He doesn't matter enough to be the centre of your attention, which belongs to the one who sat down.

A chair in a classroom started all this. Not a vision, only a physics demonstration on an ordinary day and an evening reflection that made the ordinary suggestive. The chair holds its place until a force moves it. The devil has been moved, and the force was the cross. And you, in Christ, are seated by grace, even on the days you can't feel the cushion.

If you have never given your life to Jesus, you can begin where Paul begins in Romans 10:9, by saying that he is Lord and trusting that God raised him from the dead. The chair is there for you too. If you have, the invitation is smaller and can be kept this week. When you next hear the accusation, ask whose book he is reading from, and then pray the way you would speak to someone who has been listening to you all along, because he has.
