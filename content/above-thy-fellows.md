## Two Sermons and Neither Reads the Verse

Two sermons are preached in Nigerian churches, and a good many believers have sat under both without noticing that they contradict each other.

The first says that wanting to be good at anything is dangerously close to pride. Stay small, stay quiet, pay your tithe, and whatever happens, happens. Nobody wrote this into a statement of faith, and no denomination would own it, but you can feel it in certain sanctuaries and hear it in the advice given to bright young people. The ones who hold it are sincere, and they are not lazy. They have learned that ambition is worldly and that average is where the humble live, and so they let their expectations of themselves shrink to fit. Real prayer, real tears, a real love for God, and a career of doing the minimum.

The second sermon says you were born to be above your fellows. It has a verse for this, and the verse is Hebrews 1:9. It comes with declarations, and sometimes with a small bottle of oil offered at a price and a promise that your destiny will wake up by next Tuesday. Anyone who has scrolled through church adverts on a phone for ten minutes knows the style.

Both sermons get the verse wrong. I think the first does the quieter damage, because nobody argues with it. A believer who has spiritualised being ordinary can sit through forty years of work and call it trust in God. The second does its damage out loud, and it feeds on the damage of the first, because a congregation told for years to expect nothing will run toward anyone who promises everything.

So the verse deserves a slow reading from the beginning, before anyone tells us what it promises.

> *"Thou hast loved righteousness, and hated iniquity; therefore God, even thy God, hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness above thy fellows."*
>
> — Hebrews 1:9 (KJV)

## Whose Fellows Are These

The writer of Hebrews is quoting. These words come from Psalm 45, which a translators' note describes as a wedding song for a royal bridegroom (SIL International, n.d.). Hebrews 1:8 and 9 quote Psalm 45:6 and 7 (Ellingworth & Nida, 1983), and the letter applies them to God's Son in a chapter whose whole business is his rank above the angels. Here is the verse in the English Standard Version, where the key word is easier to see.

> *"you have loved righteousness and hated wickedness. Therefore God, your God, has anointed you with the oil of gladness beyond your companions"*
>
> — Psalm 45:7 (ESV)

The Hebrew word that the King James Version renders "fellows" means associates or companions (Bible Hub, n.d.). In the psalm, the oil of gladness sets the king above the other royals gathered around him at his wedding. Nobody in that room is being told to compare a salary with a cousin's.

Then comes a detail that surprised me when I first checked it. The Greek word the letter uses for "fellows" is a word for partners, those who share in something. Hebrews uses it again of believers, who are called sharers in a heavenly calling in Hebrews 3:1 and sharers in Christ in Hebrews 3:14. So the companions include more than the angels or the other kings. In the letter's own vocabulary, they may be us, the people with whom the Son shares a nature, a point that I'll come back to.

I should say that commentators differ here. Some take the fellows to be the angels of the chapter, some the royal company of the psalm, and some the human family he came to join. The one reading that nobody in the tradition has defended is the popular one, that the verse promises each believer a place above colleagues.

Athanasius, the bishop of Alexandria in the fourth century, spent real effort on this text because his opponents, the followers of Arius, had seized on the word "therefore." If the Son was anointed *because* he loved righteousness, they said, then he earned it, he improved, and so he must be a creature who could change. Athanasius answered that the anointing is no reward for progress. The Son was already God and already eternal King, and the anointing did not make him either. The words are written, he says, on our behalf, so that the Spirit who came upon him as man might come to us through him (Athanasius, n.d., Discourse I, sections 46 and 49). And on the word "fellows," he argued that the Word cannot be counted one among the created sharers, since he is the Father's own Word.

Whatever we make of the finer points, the consequence for anyone who wants to turn the verse into a personal promise is plain. If the verse concerns Christ's unique place, then "above thy fellows" is not a guarantee that you will outrank your colleagues at the ministry, your classmates, or the man who got the promotion. To read it that way takes the one verse in the chapter about his rank and treats it as a promise about yours.

I'd rather say that plainly than build a whole essay on a misreading and hope nobody checks.

## Why the Oil Is Not for Sale

The preachers who sell oil are in the oldest company on record. In Acts 8, Simon the sorcerer watches the apostles lay hands on people and the Spirit come, and he offers money for the ability. Peter's reply is one of the sharpest in the New Testament, a wish that Simon's silver perish with him, because he thought the gift of God could be bought (Acts 8:18 to 20). The anointing of the Spirit has never had a price tag, and any ministry that lists one has told you what it is.

Notice also what the oil actually is in Scripture. Kings, priests and prophets were anointed to do something for other people. Saul was anointed over the people, David to shepherd them, and Jesus himself read from Isaiah in the synagogue at Nazareth that the Spirit of the Lord was upon him to bring good news to the poor (Luke 4:18). Oil in the Bible is an appointment to service. A man who wants it as a personal upgrade has misunderstood what it is for.

## The Anointing Came by the Road of Obedience

The verse has a shape that is easy to miss. Before the oil comes the cause. He loved righteousness and hated iniquity, and therefore God anointed him. The "therefore" is a hinge, and it says that the lifting followed the life. Athanasius is right that the Son was never a candidate on trial. But the letter does not leave his exaltation floating in the air either. A chapter later the writer says that we see Jesus, who for a little while was made lower than the angels,

> *"crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, so that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone."*
>
> — Hebrews 2:9 (ESV)

Glory because of suffering. That is the order of events in the one life the verse is actually about. The oil of gladness was poured on a man who went to a cross.

Paul tells the same story in Philippians 2. Christ did not treat equality with God as something to grasp. He emptied himself, took the form of a servant, and was obedient to death on a cross. And then, in the verse that matters for any theology of promotion,

> *"Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name,"*
>
> — Philippians 2:9 (ESV)

There is another "therefore." The pattern of the New Testament is that exaltation is God's response to a descent, and the descent was chosen first. Nobody climbed to the name above every name. He went down to it, and was lifted.

So here is the first thing the prosperity talk leaves out. If the believer is to share in this anointing at all, and I think in a real sense we do, because the word Christ means the anointed one and we are in him, then the sharing comes by union with a person whose road went through suffering. It does not come by claiming a position over other people.

And the second thing is that the condition, loving righteousness and hating iniquity, cannot be a performance requirement, or none of us would qualify. Paul himself confessed that in his flesh dwells no good thing (Romans 7:18). The answer comes in his letter to Corinth.

> *"For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."*
>
> — 2 Corinthians 5:21 (ESV)

The righteousness the verse names as the condition has been handed to the believer in Christ. You are not trying to earn the oil by being good enough. You are asked to live as someone who has already been given the standing, and to let the love of righteousness grow into a habit.

## Excellence Begins With the God Who Made You

Before turning to ambition itself, it helps to say why excellence matters to God at all, because the sermon about staying small never asks.

The psalmist cries that the Lord's name is excellent in all the earth (Psalm 8:1). The Hebrew word he uses carries the sense of majestic, mighty, glorious, and nobody uses it of things that merely function. Everything God makes carries that signature. The mathematics folded into a nautilus shell, the thunderstorm that builds over Lagos in August until the sky turns the colour of charcoal and the rain comes down like applause, the particular blue that water takes on only at a certain depth. He does not turn out near misses. In Genesis he looks at what he has made and calls it very good.

You were made in the image of that God. Genesis 1 says so about every human being, not about the gifted few. A theology that tells you to do poor work because humility requires it has to explain why the One whose image you carry did not work that way. Excellence is not a standard imposed on you from outside. It belongs to what you were made to be.

The fall bent it. Sin distorted it. But redemption restores, and the restoring is what Paul means when he says we are being renewed. A believer who gives a bad day's work and calls it modesty has not found the humble road. They have simply stayed in the ruin and put a spiritual label on it.

## The Strongest Case for Claiming the Oil

I want to give the best objection in words its holders would accept. It runs like this. The Bible shows God lifting people. Joseph went from a prison to the right hand of Pharaoh. David went from a flock to a throne. Daniel was preferred above every other official. Proverbs says that a man skilled in his work will stand before kings (Proverbs 22:29). The Son was anointed, and we are told to follow him. A church that has spent generations telling its young people to expect little has produced a lot of talent buried in the ground. If the "claim it" teachers are guilty of excess, they are at least trying to treat a real sickness, and the other side has only offered silence.

I think that is half right, and the half that is right matters. The sickness is real. Many believers do treat mediocrity as modesty, and it does hurt them, their families and the witness of the church. Nothing in Scripture tells us to be bad at our work, and plenty tells us otherwise.

But the cure the objection offers is aimed at the wrong thing. It takes God's lifting, which Scripture shows as his own act and often in his own time, and turns it into an entitlement that can be claimed on demand. It also quietly changes the question from "what am I for?" to "where do I rank?" And once ranking is the question, the Son's unique position has become a ladder, and you have put yourself on it.

## What Joseph, David and Daniel Actually Teach

The three men the objection leans on need a closer look, because none of them was lifted the way the oil sermon imagines.

Joseph was seventeen when he dreamed of sheaves bowing to his sheaf, and he told the dream to the very brothers it humiliated. Nothing in the text suggests the boy was a model of humility. What followed was a pit, a slave market, a house where he did his work well and was falsely accused, and a prison where he did it well again and was forgotten for two years. Then, in a single day, Pharaoh set him over Egypt (Genesis 41:39 to 44). It looks like a promotion, and it was the end of a long school. When his brothers stood before him afraid, he named what had happened.

> *"As for you, you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good, to bring it about that many people should be kept alive, as they are today."*
>
> — Genesis 50:20 (ESV)

Notice the purpose. The power was given so that many people could be kept alive. It was never given to impress a family.

David was anointed by Samuel as a boy, and then sent back to the sheep. He was anointed in secret, and for years afterwards he did nothing to take what he had been promised. Twice he had Saul at his mercy, in a cave and in the camp, and each time he refused to lift his hand against the Lord's anointed (1 Samuel 24 and 26). Here is a man with a promise of a throne and a spear within reach, who waited. Ambition that cannot wait is not faith in God's lifting. It is a plan to do the lifting yourself.

Before that, there was the shepherd's field. No one in Israel's army was expecting David. Saul stood head and shoulders above every man in Israel (1 Samuel 9:2), and the tribe of Benjamin was famed for left handed slingers who could hit a hair (Judges 20:16). For forty days Goliath's challenge hung in the valley of Elah, and none of them could answer it. David had killed a lion and a bear when no one was watching, and when he faced the giant he said, in the verse that explains the whole business, that he came in the name of the Lord of hosts (1 Samuel 17:45). The valley did not discover David. It showed what the field had been forming. Excellence is built somewhere private before it is displayed somewhere public.

Then Daniel. When Nebuchadnezzar took Daniel and his companions, still young men, into Babylon, every force around them pressed them to become Babylonians in language, literature, food and gods. The argument for accommodation was a good one. They were captives, with no leverage and no network. But Daniel decided before the moment came that he would not defile himself with the king's food (Daniel 1:8), and when the king examined them at the end of their training, he found them ten times better than all the magicians and astrologers in his realm (Daniel 1:20). I notice that the decision came first and the distinction after. What you settle in advance is what survives the pressure. What you improvise under pressure tends to be whatever costs the least in that moment.

Years later, under another empire,

> *"this Daniel was preferred above the presidents and princes, because an excellent spirit was in him; and the king thought to set him over the whole realm."*
>
> — Daniel 6:3 (KJV)

An excellent spirit. It was resident in him, steady across decades and kings, and that is the difference between excellence as performance and excellence as character. Performance goes up and down. Character builds on itself.

Three men, then, and one pattern. A long private preparation, a refusal to grab, and a lifting that arrived on God's schedule, for a purpose beyond the man. Psalm 75 states it as plainly as anything in the Bible, that lifting up does not come from the east or the west or the wilderness.

> *"but it is God who executes judgment, putting down one and lifting up another."*
>
> — Psalm 75:7 (ESV)

## Is Ambition Itself a Sin

Now the strongest objection from the other side, which deserves its own answer. Some of the most serious Christians I know would say that ambition is a sin, full stop, and they have Scripture for it. God tells Baruch through Jeremiah not to seek great things for himself (Jeremiah 45:5). Paul tells the Philippians to do nothing from selfish ambition (Philippians 2:3). James says that where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there will be disorder and every vile practice (James 3:16). Augustine, in the preface to *The City of God*, names the lust for domination as the engine of the earthly city, and the church has never stopped warning about pride, the first of the deadly sins. A believer who has watched a church split over a title is entitled to be suspicious.

I take that seriously. The sin is real. But notice what each warning aims at. Jeremiah's word is for a man hoping to be great at a moment when God was about to tear a nation down, and the issue was his timing and his self concern. Paul's phrase is *selfish* ambition, set against counting others as more significant than ourselves. James is condemning a rivalry that destroys community. In each case the target is ambition that points back at the self, and the Bible nowhere condemns wanting to do something large and good.

It goes further. Paul speaks of his ambition without apology. He aimed to preach the gospel where Christ was not yet named (Romans 15:20), the word translated "ambition" there being the very verb for love of honour. He tells the Thessalonians to aspire to live quietly, to mind their own affairs and work with their hands (1 Thessalonians 4:11), using the same verb. And in a plain sentence, he says that if anyone aspires to the office of overseer, he desires a noble task (1 Timothy 3:1). A faith that treated all desire to rise as sin would have no pastors, because Paul says wanting the work is fine.

Jesus, too, was never shy about the subject. When James and John asked for the best seats in his kingdom (Mark 10:35 to 41), he did not tell them to want nothing. He redirected the wanting. The other disciples were indignant, and he called them all together.

> *"But it shall not be so among you. But whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all."*
>
> — Mark 10:43 to 44 (ESV)

Greatness is allowed. What changes is the direction. Great means useful to somebody.

So I'd put the matter this way. Ambition is a desire, and desires take their moral shape from their object and their method. Ambition for the Son's glory and the neighbour's good is a virtue, and the same energy turned toward my own rank becomes the very thing Augustine feared. There are two tests I'd put to my own ambitions, and you can borrow them. Who gains if I succeed, besides me? And what will I do if I'm passed over and the person who gets the post is less able than I am? An ambition that has no answer to the first question is hunger, and one that falls apart at the second has been resting on the ranking all along.

Paul gives the model in one verse that holds the two in one hand. He worked harder than all the other apostles, "though it was not I, but the grace of God that is with me" (1 Corinthians 15:10, ESV). He does not pretend he was lazy, and he does not pretend the work was his own achievement. You work as hard as you can, and then you tell the truth about where the strength came from. Humility was never the opposite of excellence. It is what excellence sounds like when it knows who paid for it.

## The Nigerian Habit of Titles and Lifts

All of this lands hard in a country that loves a title. Anyone who has seen a wedding programme or a funeral poster knows that the names come with a queue in front of them, a chief, a doctor, a barrister, an engineer, a pastor, sometimes a pastor who is also a doctor, and the order matters. We announce promotions on the family WhatsApp group with all the letters spelled out. Who can doubt that part of this is old and good, a respect for learning and age and public service? But part of it is a hunger to be seen above, and the church has not been immune. Open any church bulletin and count the apostles, the general overseers and the prophets, and you will see that the oil sermon did not arrive from nowhere.

There is a related culture of promotion that every Nigerian worker knows about. Posts go to the person with a sponsor, and the person who does the work and has no sponsor watches, and decides that the system is the only god that matters. Many believers react in one of two ways. Some conclude that the only way up is to find a godfather, and others decide that the lifting must be waiting for them as a spiritual entitlement, because they have fasted. Both make the same mistake. They have put rank at the centre.

Jesus saw this exact market in Matthew 23. He told his disciples not to seek the titles that the religious leaders loved, the places of honour at feasts and the greetings in the marketplaces, and he closed the thought in words that every Nigerian professional should keep nearby.

> *"The greatest among you shall be your servant. Whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted."*
>
> — Matthew 23:11 to 12 (ESV)

That is not an instruction to refuse every title. It is a warning that the title is not the thing. A man may be called doctor because he has done a doctorate, and that is simply true. What Jesus is after is the heart that needs to be called it.

## When Excellence Gets You Into Trouble

Now the part that the oil sermons skip, because it is bad for sales.

Daniel is the standard example of excellence in the Bible, and he deserves it. Read the verse after the one about the excellent spirit. The other officials looked for a ground for complaint against him, and found none, "because he was faithful, and no error or fault was found in him" (Daniel 6:4, ESV). Their answer to a man they could not fault was to make a law against his prayers. The excellence that made him preferred was the reason he ended up in a den of lions. God shut the lions' mouths, and that is the good news of the chapter, but it was not a reward for being good at his job. Daniel could not have known in advance how it would end.

I'm not saying this to scare anyone. I'm saying it because most Nigerians already know it from the office. Do your work well in a place where others cut corners and see what happens to your standing with them. Refuse to inflate a figure and watch who stops inviting you. Excellence is admired in the abstract and resented in the building. If your picture of the anointing leaves out the resentment, you will be shocked the first time it arrives, and you may decide you misheard God.

History gives the same warning in two African lives.

Samuel Ajayi Crowther was taken from his home in March 1821, at about twelve years old, by slave raiders, and sold to Portuguese traders. A Royal Navy ship freed the captives and landed them at Freetown. He became the first student admitted to Fourah Bay College, was consecrated at Canterbury in 1864 as the first African Anglican bishop, and supervised the Yoruba Bible that was completed in the mid 1880s (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.b). By any measure that is a life above his fellows. Then, in 1890, two European missionaries accused several African pastors of fraud, ignorance and immorality and held Crowther responsible, and he was pressured out of the Niger mission, with the work placed under European secretaries (Oke, n.d.). A later writer notes that the accusations were almost unanimously refuted by the time anyone checked, and that by then the damage was done (Guthrie, 2017). He died in Lagos on 31 December 1891 (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.b). I haven't found anything that tells us what he said in his last year, and I won't guess. What the record shows is that excellence in the church could be rewarded and punished in the same life.

Ameyo Adadevoh, the lead consultant physician at First Consultant Medical Centre in Lagos, was the doctor who suspected in July 2014 that Patrick Sawyer, a Liberian traveller who said he had malaria and wanted to go to a conference, had Ebola. She kept him in the hospital against the request of his country's ambassador, saying that for the greater public good she would not release him. She died of the disease on 19 August 2014, aged 57 (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.a). The sources I read are silent on her religious faith, and I'm not going to claim her for any camp. What they do show is skill and firmness under pressure, and her son has said that the Liberian government pressed her to let Sawyer go (NPR, 2014). She did not live to see any recognition. The national honour, Officer of the Order of the Niger, came in October 2022, eight years after her death (Wikipedia contributors, n.d.a).

Neither life fits the promise that you will be elevated over your fellows. Both fit the verse, if the verse is about a path through cost to a gladness that arrives, when it arrives, on God's schedule and not ours.

## Excellence on an Ordinary Tuesday

So what do we do with all this on a Tuesday morning in an ordinary office, or a lecture hall, or a classroom full of children who need someone to mark their books?

We do what Paul told the church in Colossae to do, which was to take the work in front of us seriously and leave the distinction to God.

> *"Whatever you do, work heartily, as for the Lord and not for men,"*
>
> — Colossians 3:23 (ESV)

Notice who the audience is. The supervisor might not notice, and the congregation might clap, and neither of them sets the standard. The standard is set by the person you work for, and he sees the version of the job that nobody checks. The Preacher says the same in Ecclesiastes 9:10, that whatever your hand finds to do, you should do with your might, since there is no work in the grave where you are going, and the grave takes no late submissions. And Proverbs 12:24 makes a claim about governance that is plain enough, that the hand of the diligent will rule while the slothful will be put to forced labour.

In education, it means studying to master, and not only to pass. The Nigerian student who has learned exactly how much to write to clear the pass mark has a skill, but it is a skill for an examiner. Daniel did not aim to pass Nebuchadnezzar's examination. Paul told Timothy to do his best to present himself approved to God, a worker with no need to be ashamed (2 Timothy 2:15), and the approval he means comes from God, whatever the grading curve says. In the workplace, it means a diligence that does not need a supervisor's eye and a punctuality that comes from your own values, whatever the policy document says. It means the kind of integrity that survives the moment when cutting a corner would save you an afternoon and no one would ever know. Even in leadership, Jethro told Moses to look for men who were able, who feared God, who were trustworthy and who hated a bribe (Exodus 18:21). Competence came in the same sentence as character.

The same goes for the clerk who could pad a figure and doesn't, the teacher who marks every script when half the pile would pass unread, and the young pastor who prepares as carefully for twelve people on a rainy Wednesday as he would for twelve hundred. Nobody gives out prizes for these. Most of them will never be noticed at all. That is exactly why they are the real test. A person who is excellent only when watched is acting, and the Lord, who is the one reading the work, has seen the acting before.

Peter lays out the building of character as a master builder would, adding to faith virtue, to virtue knowledge, to knowledge self control, and so on up to love (2 Peter 1:5 to 7). It is a life's work that compounds daily, and nothing in it can be bought in a bottle.

There is also a smaller honesty to be kept. False modesty is a kind of pride too. The person who says "I'm just a small person, who am I to do that" about work they could plainly do well is often protecting themselves from the risk of failing in public. God didn't give the gift for that purpose. If you can do a thing well and the need is real, saying so, and then doing it, is not arrogance. It's obedience.

And if lifting does come, as it came to Joseph and to Daniel, take it as a loan. The oil is for the room you've been put in, and it isn't for display. Jesus said that he who would be first must be last, and he said it as a man heading toward a cross.

## Rising Without Needing to Be Seen

Before I leave you, there is a word from Isaiah that I think belongs in this conversation, if we read it in the right place. The prophet tells a people in darkness to arise and shine, for their light has come and the glory of the Lord has risen upon them (Isaiah 60:1). It is addressed to a city, with nations coming to its light. It is not a private promotion. And Paul's closing blessing over Ephesus says that God is able to do far more abundantly than all we ask or think, according to the power at work within us (Ephesians 3:20). The power is real, and it works within us. What it produces is a community that shines, a church whose members do remarkable work and are not tempted to count their rank.

I will leave you with one small task and one question. Pick one piece of your work that you currently do at about seventy percent because no one will check it, a lesson note, a report, a small repair, a reply to a customer, and do it properly this week, as though the Lord were the one reading it. Then ask yourself honestly what you wanted from it. If the answer is applause, you have found out something worth knowing. If the answer is only that it should be right, then you are closer to the verse than you thought. And whatever you find, tell someone you trust, and let them ask you again in a month how it went.
