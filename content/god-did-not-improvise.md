## The Story We Tell Children

Most of us learned it in Sunday school, with a felt board. God makes a garden. Adam and Eve spoil it. The garden is in ruins, and so God, after some thought, sends his Son to put things right. First the plan, then the breakdown, then the repair.

Nobody tells it that way to be unkind to God. But listen to what the order says. It says something happened in Eden that heaven had not made provision for, and that the cross is what you do when the first arrangement fails. A good many adult sermons keep the same sequence and just use longer words. The cross becomes a rescue mission launched in a hurry.

I want to argue that this is wrong, and that people pay for the mistake in two places. The first is the hospital corridor, where someone is wondering whether God was looking the other way. The second is the three o'clock waking, when a believer who has failed again wonders how firm a fix can be if it was patched together after the damage. A God who improvises is a God whose repairs might not hold.

Scripture tells a different story. In it the cross is in view before the world has a floor under it. Before Adam reached for the fruit, the blood had already been counted. That sentence will bother some readers, and it should be tested before it is believed, so this essay does the testing slowly. It reads the texts that say "before", admits where one famous verse is honestly divided, asks why the Old Testament sounds like a rehearsal, and then faces the best objection I know. After that it sets out, in the words their own holders would accept, how four serious schools of Christian thought answer it. None of that removes the pain from the corridor. It changes who is standing there with you.

## What Scripture Says About Before

Start with the texts that say it most plainly. Paul, writing to the church in Ephesus, says this of the people who belong to Christ.

> *"even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him."*
>
> — Ephesians 1:4 (ESV)

Peter says something similar about Christ himself. He has told scattered and pressured Christians that they were ransomed, and he says with what.

> *"knowing that you were ransomed from the futile ways inherited from your forefathers, not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot. He was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times for the sake of you"*
>
> — 1 Peter 1:18 to 20 (ESV)

Notice what Peter has put in one breath. The lamb, the blood, and the phrase "before the foundation of the world" sit together. Paul adds a third witness when he tells Timothy that God's grace was given to us "in Christ Jesus before the ages began" (2 Timothy 1:9, ESV). Jesus himself speaks of a kingdom "prepared for you from the foundation of the world" (Matthew 25:34, ESV).

Then there is Revelation 13:8, the verse many preachers reach for first. It deserves honest handling because it can't carry what is often put on it. The English Standard Version, in its current text, reads that those who worship the beast are the people

> *"everyone whose name has not been written from the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain."*
>
> — Revelation 13:8 (ESV)

On that reading the phrase "from the foundation of the world" attaches to the writing of the names. The New International Version, the King James Version, the New King James Version and the New Living Translation attach it to the slaying instead, so that the NIV ends with "the Lamb who was slain from the creation of the world." Greek word order allows either. The United Bible Societies handbook on Revelation prefers the reading with "written", partly because Revelation 17:8 uses the same phrase and can only mean that. It also names Phillips, the NIV and the Revised English Bible as translations that go the other way, with commentators who point to 1 Peter 1:19 to 20 (Bratcher & Hatton, 1993). One small practical note. For years the ESV said "before" the foundation of the world here, and some Bible websites still show that older wording. The Greek preposition is the ordinary one for "from".

So what do we do with a verse that's honestly divided? We stop asking it to be the whole case. Either way it places something before creation, whether the book of the Lamb or the slaying of the Lamb. And the other texts I have just read don't have the problem. Ephesians has no ambiguity about when the choosing happened. Peter has none about who was foreknown. That is a stronger position than one proof text, because nobody can knock it over by arguing about a preposition.

There is also a plain point about language. When Scripture says the Lamb was "slain" before the world began, it is speaking the way it often speaks of what God has settled. Nothing was bleeding in the dark before creation. The meaning is that the cost was already accepted and the outcome was already fixed in God's purpose, so that the event on Calvary is the arrival in time of something God had already resolved. That is how Peter can say a person was "foreknown" before the foundation of the world and "made manifest" in the last times, in a single sentence.

## An Eternal Remedy for an Eternal Problem

Why would the remedy have to be settled so early? Begin with what it means that God is eternal. It means more than that he has no beginning and no end. It means that what he does carries the quality of who he is. Ecclesiastes 3:14 says that whatever God does endures forever, and that nothing can be added to it or taken from it. His words don't come with a sunset clause. When God says something is so, it is so the way gravity is so, whether or not your denomination endorses it.

Carry that into the garden. God told Adam that on the day he ate of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil he would surely die (Genesis 2:17). The apostle Paul later puts the principle in six words, that "the wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23, ESV). Nobody reading these verses honestly can treat sin as a clerical error that a sincere apology tidies up. A holy God has attached a real consequence to it, and a real consequence needs a real answer.

Here the arithmetic gets uncomfortable for every religion that treats the problem as small. A person can bring a goat, a bull, a year of fasting or a lifetime of attendance at Sunday service and still not have touched the root. The animal was not born before the foundation of the world. It owes its own death to the same fallen order. You do not put out a house fire with a cup of water. So if there was going to be an answer at all, it would have to come from outside the system that needed rescuing, and from someone whose worth matched the problem. Peter's list is exactly that, "not with perishable things such as silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ". The currency was never going to be ours.

And that is why the timing matters. A God who finds out about the problem on the day it happens has to work out the answer afterwards. A God who has always known what the answer costs does not. The answer was not made to fit the problem. The problem was met by an answer that had been waiting there all along.

## Why the Old Testament Sounds Like a Rehearsal

If the cross was always the plan, then what was the long run of sacrifices before it? Anyone who has run a generator knows the answer in outline. You fill the tank in the morning, and by evening you are filling it again. It does the job, and it also tells you every day that you're not connected to the grid. The sacrifices of Israel were like that. The Day of Atonement came once a year, as Leviticus 16:34 lays down, and the following year it came again. Cover, accumulate, shed blood, cover again. Every high priest who walked into the holy place knew he would be back next year.

Many of us grew up around something not entirely unlike it in traditional African religion. The seasonal offerings at family shrines, the rituals repeated because the forces being appeased were never quite satisfied, all carried the same instinct. I would not mock it. It shows that people everywhere have known that the gap between the human and the divine is real and needs something to close it. What the repetition showed was that none of those offerings could close it.

The writer of Hebrews is blunt about what that repetition meant. "For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (Hebrews 10:4, ESV). Then he turns the argument on its head with a sentence that is easy to read past.

> *"for then he would have had to suffer repeatedly since the foundation of the world. But as it is, he has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself."*
>
> — Hebrews 9:26 (ESV)

Look at the starting line he picks. He doesn't say "since Adam" or "since Sinai". He says since the foundation of the world. The writer is measuring the sacrifice of Christ against the whole span of creation, and finding that it has been in view over all of it. Each sacrifice before Calvary was a promissory note, written in blood, against a payment that had already been agreed in heaven.

The earliest hint comes in Genesis, in the same chapter as the fall. God tells the serpent that the woman's offspring "shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel" (Genesis 3:15, ESV). The verse reads like a plan already moving. Isaiah, many centuries on, says of the suffering servant that "it was the will of the LORD to crush him" (Isaiah 53:10, ESV), which is a hard sentence and not one to soften. And when John the Baptist sees Jesus walking towards him, the first thing he does is point. "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29, ESV).

Notice the small word "the". John does not say a lamb, one more in a long procession since Leviticus. He says the Lamb, the one all the others had been about. The verb for "takes away" is a present participle in the Greek, and I would not build a doctrine on a tense alone, since Greek tenses carry less than English readers expect. But the picture agrees with Hebrews. What the tabernacle enacted in yearly rehearsal, Christ did once, and what he did does not wear off.

## Why God Did Not Simply Forgive

An honest sceptic will ask the next question at once. If God is God, why a cross at all? Why not announce a pardon and be done? Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, took that question seriously in the fourth century in *On the Incarnation*. He said it was "out of the question to leave men to the current of corruption; because this would be unseemly, and unworthy of God's goodness" (Athanasius, chapter 6, section 10). A few paragraphs later he argues that repentance on its own could stop sinful acts but could not restore a nature that corruption had already entered, and that only the Word, through whom everything was first made, could remake it (chapter 7, paraphrased). I would rather not claim more for him than that. But notice the shape of his reasoning. He doesn't treat the cross as a convenient gesture. He treats it as the fitting answer to the actual size of the problem, which is what you would expect if the answer had been prepared before the problem arrived.

That brings us to the person at the centre. John opens his Gospel by saying that the Word was with God and was God, and that the Word is the one through whom everything was made. Then, in the verse most of us can recite, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14, ESV). The eternal entered time. Paul puts the timing in a sentence that has been turned over for two thousand years. "But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4, ESV). Everything before that moment, the patriarchs, the exodus, the tabernacle, the prophets, the exile, the return, the long silence, was a corridor God was walking down towards a door he had chosen to open before the corridor existed.

Some say this is the language of later councils, that the divinity of Christ was an invention of bishops at Nicaea in 325. The record doesn't support that. Nicaea met to answer Arius, a presbyter in Alexandria who taught that the Son was a created being, and it defended what the churches had been saying in worship and in baptism long before. It is worth noticing who did the heavy lifting. Athanasius, an African, argued the case against the pressure of emperors and bishops, and the language he fought for is the language we still use. Thomas Oden, a Methodist scholar who spent years among the early African writers, has argued that the intellectual seedbed of much Western Christian thought was African (Oden, 2007). You need not take his whole thesis to see the point. The doctrine that God came himself was never a foreign import. It was defended in Alexandria and Hippo before it was defended in Rome.

C. S. Lewis put the exchange in one line in *Mere Christianity*. "The Son of God became a man to enable men to become sons of God" (Lewis, 1952, Book IV, chapter 5). The same Son who was foreknown before the foundation of the world is the one who walks into history on schedule.

## The Strongest Objection

The best objection I know goes like this. If the Lamb was in view before Adam ate the fruit, then God saw the fall coming. If he saw it coming and made the world anyway, he is responsible for it, and Adam was a character in a script. The alternative is that God did not see it coming, and then he improvised after all. You have picked one of the horns, and neither is comfortable.

Scripture refuses the choice, and refuses it in the plainest place. Peter, preaching at Pentecost, says of Jesus that he was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God", and that "you crucified and killed" him "by the hands of lawless men" (Acts 2:23, ESV). The plan is God's and the guilt is theirs, in the same sentence. The church prays the same truth in Acts 4:27 to 28, where Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and the people of Israel gather against Jesus "to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place". Peter doesn't stop to explain how the two fit together. Christians have not stopped arguing about it since, and it is fairer to the reader to lay out the arguments than to pretend there is only one.

## Four Ways Christians Answer It

The first answer is the Reformed one, and I want to give it in its full and proper form. The Westminster Confession of Faith, written in the 1640s, says in its chapter on God's eternal decree that "God from all eternity, did, by the most wise and holy counsel of His own will" ordain whatever comes to pass. It adds three qualifications at once. God is not the author of sin. No violence is done to the will of the creatures. And the freedom and contingency of second causes is not taken away but established (Westminster Assembly, 1646, chapter 3, section 1). It goes on to say that although God knows whatever may or can come to pass under any condition, he has not decreed anything on the ground that he foresaw it (section 2). A Reformed theologian will explain that there are two levels of causation. God is the first cause, and creatures are the second causes who choose from their own desires and so are answerable for what they choose. This is what is meant by saying freedom and decree are compatible, which the technical word calls compatibilism. In this account the cross was settled in what the tradition calls the decree, and the fall was within that decree, but the sin was Adam's own, as Acts 2:23 says of the crucifixion. Reformed writers also distinguish an order of logic from an order of time. The question whether God logically decided to save before or after deciding to permit the fall, which is the old argument between supralapsarians and infralapsarians, was never about what happened first on a calendar, since there was no calendar.

The second answer is the Arminian one. Jacobus Arminius's followers, the Remonstrants, set out their position in 1610, and the first article speaks of what Schaff's commentary calls conditional predestination. God decreed from eternity to save those who believe through his Son, and to condemn the unbelieving, so that election rests on foreseen faith (Schaff, 1877, §66). An Arminian reader of Ephesians 1:4 points to the words "in him" and says that the people chosen are those found in Christ, which is what a church is. On this view God foreknows what free creatures will do without causing it, in the way that your seeing yesterday does not cause yesterday. He foresaw the fall, and the Lamb was provided because of it, not as a reaction but as a provision made in advance. Arminians hold that grace goes before every human response, which they call prevenient grace, so that nobody comes to God without being drawn first. Their strongest point is the one about love. A God who wanted creatures capable of real love had to make creatures capable of real refusal, and the cross is what that love costs. John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, held this view and spread it widely.

The third is the Molinist answer, named for Luis de Molina, a sixteenth century Spanish Jesuit. It begins with a distinction between three kinds of divine knowledge. First there is God's natural knowledge of all possibilities and necessary truths. Last there is his free knowledge of what he has actually chosen to bring about. Between them, as the name suggests, comes middle knowledge, which has been defined as "God's prevolitional knowledge of all true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom" (Laing, n.d.). In plain English, before God created anything he knew what any free creature would freely do in any situation he might place them in. He then chose, among all the worlds he could have made, one in which free creatures freely do what is needed for the cross and for a church of the redeemed. That is how a Molinist holds on to both halves of Acts 2:23. The plan is God's because he picked the world. The guilt is the crowd's because they acted freely within it. The strongest objection is what philosophers call the grounding objection, which asks what makes those conditional statements true before the creatures exist. Replies have been offered, and the debate continues (Laing, n.d.).

The fourth is open theism, which has been the most controversial of the four among evangelicals. Its best known statement is *The Openness of God* (1994), by Clark Pinnock, Richard Rice, John Sanders, William Hasker and David Basinger. Open theists hold that although God is omniscient, he does not know what we will freely do in the future, because there are not yet truths about the undetermined future for him to know. They say this is no limit on his power, any more than the fact that God cannot make a square circle is a limit on it. They read texts such as Genesis 22:12, where God says "now I know that you fear God", as describing a real test with a real outcome (Rissler, n.d.). On the Lamb slain, an open theist does not say God foresaw Adam's fall as a fact. As I understand them, they say God knew every possibility, and had settled before creation what he would do if his creatures turned away, so that the resolve to redeem was fixed even though the fall was not. I could not confirm a single source that states it in exactly those words, and a reader should check it against the open theists' own books. Critics respond with texts such as Isaiah 40 to 48, Romans 9 and Ephesians 1:11, and say the apostle's language of choosing before the foundation of the world is hard to hold with a future that is open (Rissler, n.d.).

I have tried to give each position the form its holders would recognise. I will say plainly where I stand. The first three can all say what this essay needs, that the cross was never an afterthought, and they differ over how. The fourth can say that God's resolve to redeem was never an afterthought, which is a smaller claim, and I find it strains against the plain sense of "foreknown before the foundation of the world". That is a disagreement among friends, and I would rather you argued with me than skipped it.

What all four agree on is what the objection wanted us to deny. The sin belongs to the sinner. And God's answer to it was never improvised.

## What an Unsurprised God Means for Suffering

Here the theory meets the burial ground. Picture a woman in Ibadan whose brother has died after a crash on the expressway. At the burial, someone kind and well meaning leans over and says that God knows best. Anyone who has stood at a Nigerian burial knows that sentence can land like a slap, and that the person who says it often means well and has run out of words. It is a cheap sentence when it travels alone, because it asks the grieving to admire a plan they can't see.

The four traditions would answer her differently, and she deserves to hear all four. The Reformed pastor says that nothing falls outside God's purposes, that God is not the author of sin, and that her brother's death, though it came through secondary causes, was not a surprise to heaven. The Arminian says that God permitted a world in which people can choose badly, that the driver and the road and the failure of the system were real human failures, and that God grieves them as she does. The Molinist says that God chose a world in which her brother's life and death fit into a larger story that God can redeem, without making God the author of the crash. The open theist says that God did not plan the crash, did not know it was coming as a fact, and weeps with her without having scripted it. Each answer has a cost. The first asks her to trust a purpose she cannot see. The second and fourth ask her to hold that some of what happens was not willed by God at all. Any of them can be said in a way that is cruel, and any of them, said gently at the right time, can be a kindness.

What none of them can do is make the grief small. The cross is what stops the sentence being cheap, and here the four traditions agree. Look at what the Bible shows at the centre of heaven. In John's vision, "between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders I saw a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain" (Revelation 5:6, ESV). The wounds are still there. The one who rules is the one who was hurt, and he carries the marks of it into the throne room. That doesn't explain why a brother died on the road. Scripture doesn't give us that explanation, and I would distrust anyone who says they have it. What it gives is something different and, I think, sturdier. Whatever else is true of that death, it was not met by a God who is making it up as he goes, and the one in charge is no stranger to loss.

I should say what this doesn't claim. It doesn't say every sorrow is good, or that your pain is secretly a gift. Joseph told his brothers that what they meant for evil God meant for good (Genesis 50:20), and he said it after the fact, as a man looking back, not to anyone in the middle of the pit. The promise isn't that you will understand. The promise is that you aren't being managed by someone who is improvising.

## What It Means for the Person Who Keeps Doubting

Now go back to the three o'clock waking. A believer has failed at the same thing for the fourth time this year, and the voice in the dark says that surely this is the failure that finally goes too far for God. If the cross were a hurried fix, that voice would have a point. A patch has limits. But Paul tells Timothy that God "saved us and called us to a holy calling, not because of our works but because of his own purpose and grace, which he gave us in Christ Jesus before the ages began" (2 Timothy 1:9, ESV).

Before the ages began, you had done nothing. There was no list of your failures to be surprised by, because grace was not waiting on the list. The cross was set against sin, and that includes the sin you're ashamed of tonight. It wasn't drafted against some milder version of you.

One thing needs saying carefully. Ephesians 1:4 says God chose "us", the people in Christ, and it is written to a church. So I wouldn't tell any reader that the text hands them a private certificate with their name printed on it. How a person comes to be "in him" is the very thing the four traditions argue about, and I have not settled that for you. What I would say is that if you are in Christ, you are not an afterthought in his plan, and that the apostle who wrote those words thought it enough to stop a troubled conscience. Nor is any of this a licence. The same verse says the purpose is that we would be holy and blameless, and the passage from Timothy speaks of a holy calling. The assurance is meant to steady a person who is running toward God, and not to let one stop running.

Augustine, bishop of Hippo in North Africa, wrote in the opening of his *Confessions*, in the English of E. B. Pusey, that God had made us for himself, "and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee" (Augustine, Book I). He had tried a good deal else before he wrote that. The restlessness he describes is not a malfunction to be managed. It is the sign that we were made for a particular person, and the Lamb slain before the foundation of the world is the answer prepared for that exact longing, in advance, at cost.

## Where to Start Tonight

Tonight, if you can, read 1 Peter 1:17 to 21 slowly, from start to finish, and not only the verses I have picked out. Notice that Peter is writing to people who were scattered and under pressure, and that he reaches for the lamb and the foundation of the world to steady them. He doesn't reach for a theory of suffering.

Then, if you are the one who has been waiting for God to catch up with your situation, try saying aloud that he isn't behind. It may feel like a thin sentence at first. It is carrying the weight of a cross that was in view before you had a name.

And if some part of this argument sits badly with you, particularly the part about the plan and the guilt in Acts 2:23, or if you belong to a church that holds one of the four views and think I have been unfair to it, take it to your pastor or to an older believer you trust, and let them argue back. It is a question worth an evening's conversation, and I would rather you wrestled with it than left it alone.
