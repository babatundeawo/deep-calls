## The Night the King Could Not Sleep

Somewhere in the palace at Susa a man is lying awake. He is the most powerful person on earth, and he cannot sleep. The book does not say he was ill, or troubled, or full of wine. It says only this.

> *“On that night the king could not sleep.”*
> — Esther 6:1 (ESV)

Anyone who has lain awake while the generator coughed outside, or while a phone screen lit the ceiling at three in the morning, knows how little that sentence seems to carry. A king can't sleep. It happens. And yet the whole fate of a people turns on it.

To pass the time he asks for the royal chronicles to be read to him. By what looks like pure chance, the attendant reads an entry about a plot to kill the king, foiled years earlier by a Jewish clerk called Mordecai. The king asks what reward the man received. The young men who serve him say that nothing was done for him (Esther 6:1 to 3).

That small exchange, in the small hours when nobody important is awake to notice, begins to unpick a plan to wipe out every Jew in the Persian Empire. I want to walk you back to how the story reaches that night, because the strangest thing about Esther sits in plain view. The book holds no miracle. Nobody in it is said to pray. And nowhere in the Hebrew text does anyone say that God did any of it.

## A Book With No Name for God

Esther is the one book of the Bible in which no name or title of God appears. None of the Old Testament's names for him occurs in it, and no one in the story says "the Lord". The Song of Songs is sometimes put beside it, though many translators hear a divine name in the "flame of the Lord" in Song 8:6, so Esther stands alone more securely than its neighbour does (Hong Kong Baptist Theological Seminary, n.d.). Fasting appears in Esther, and prayer is never mentioned (Hong Kong Baptist Theological Seminary, n.d.).

Why? Honest scholars say they are not sure. Benjamin Shaw, who teaches Old Testament at Reformation Bible College, was asked exactly this and answered that "in a certain sense, none of us really knows why it doesn't" (Shaw, n.d.). He points out that other narratives, Genesis 37 among them, get along without naming God, and he reads Esther as a book where God is at work behind the scenes. Aaron Garriott goes further and says the author "portrays God's presence by not mentioning the presence of God at all" (Garriott, 2019). I find that plausible and I notice that it can't be proved.

There are other proposals. Some readers have found the divine name hidden in four acrostics, formed from the first or last letters of consecutive Hebrew words, and three ancient manuscripts mark those letters in red (Book of Esther, n.d.). The rabbis played on the sound of Esther's own name. In the Babylonian Talmud (Hullin 139b) her name is drawn from Deuteronomy 31:18, where God says he will surely hide his face, so that the queen who hides her people's identity is also a sign of a God who conceals himself (The Concealed Face of God, n.d.). Whether the author meant any of that, nobody can show. I think the acrostics are a lovely thing to know and a thin thing to build a sermon on.

What I can say with some confidence is that the silence is a feature of the book. The author knew how to tell a story. He chose to leave every coincidence sitting on the page without a label, and the effect is to put you in the position of the Jews of Susa, who had no prophet, no pillar of cloud and no temple, and had to read their own lives with the facts in front of them.

Mordecai's own phrase for what will happen if Esther fails is "relief and deliverance will rise for the Jews from another place" (Esther 4:14). Many interpreters hear a veiled reference to God, and a modern translators' handbook calls that the most common view, while admitting that the later Hebrew habit of saying "the Place" for God may not yet have existed when Esther was written (Omanson & Noss, 1997). So even where the book comes closest to naming him, it declines.

And that, I suspect, is why Nigerian believers read Esther with such recognition. If you grew up in a church where the vigil ran until four in the morning, where a prophet's word could make the back of your neck prickle, you were taught that God shows up loudly. Sometimes he does, and I'm not dismissing a minute of it. But anyone who has waited for a visa decision, a result, a job offer or a husband has also met the other season, where the prayers go up and the sky gives back nothing you could point at. The Bible has a whole shelf for that season, and Psalm 88 sits on it, ending in darkness. Isaiah 45:15 calls God a God who hides himself and, in the same sentence, a Saviour. Esther is the book of that shelf where the hiding is the whole plot.

## What the Greek Additions Supply

The Greek Bible tells the story differently, and the difference matters for how Christians have read Esther. The Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made by Jews before the time of Christ, contains six passages that have no parallel in the Hebrew, labelled A to F by modern editors. Together they run to 107 verses, and of the Greek book's 270 verses, those 107 have no counterpart in the Hebrew (How the Book of Esther Changed, n.d.).

They fill the silence on a large scale. Addition A opens with a dream of Mordecai and his discovery of the plot against the king. Addition B gives the text of Haman's decree and Addition E the text of the king's second edict. Addition C is the prayers of Mordecai and Esther, and Addition D has Esther before the throne in a more dramatic scene than the Hebrew offers. Addition F explains the dream at the close, and Mordecai concludes that all these things were done by God. The name of God occurs not only in the additions but at several other points in the Greek story as well (How the Book of Esther Changed, n.d.).

Scholars read the motive in different ways. David Clines has argued that the Greek translators added the religious dimension to bring Esther into line with what a scriptural book was expected to sound like, and the Greek Esther is also more careful about Jewish observance (How the Book of Esther Changed, n.d.). That is a fair reading. Another is that the translator was simply responding to an embarrassment in the Hebrew, the way a preacher at a graveside might hurry to fill a silence.

Jerome, translating the Latin Bible in the fourth century, doubted that the additions were part of the inspired text and moved them to the end of the book, with notes showing where each had belonged (Encyclopedia.com, n.d.). The Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches still count them as canonical Scripture. The Protestant churches, following the Hebrew canon, set them in the Apocrypha (How the Book of Esther Changed, n.d.). So an Orthodox Christian in Lagos and a Baptist in Ibadan open books called Esther and read, in effect, different lengths of the same story.

Here is what I take from that. The church has always felt the quiet of Esther, and some of the church felt it enough to fill it. The Hebrew author was prepared to leave it unfilled. I think the Hebrew is the more daring and, finally, the more honest account of how most of us experience God's work, and the Greek is a valuable witness to how badly a believing community wanted to hear him named.

## Was Esther Ever Doubted as Scripture

The silence made some of the early readers uneasy, and it is worth saying so plainly. Esther is the only book of the Hebrew Bible of which no fragment has turned up among the Dead Sea Scrolls (Kruger, n.d.). Michael Heiser cautioned that this "does not indicate much about its canonical position today", since the scrolls are a fraction of what once stood at Qumran, and even if that community had rejected Esther, it would show only the view of one Jewish sect (Heiser, n.d.). Kruger calls it an argument from silence, which is exactly what it is.

In the church the record is mixed. A table of early canon lists shows Origen counting Esther as holy Scripture, while the list of Melito of Sardis, about the year 160, gives the Hebrew canon without it. Athanasius in 367 puts it in a lower class of books to be read but not received as canonical, and Gregory of Nazianzus in about 380 does not mention it, while his contemporary Amphilochius of Iconium says that "some add Esther" (Bible Research, n.d.). The pattern is of a book that was never really rejected, and not at once embraced either.

Martin Luther belongs here, though a good deal of what is repeated about him is wrong. He is famous for supposedly tossing Esther into the Elbe. The sentence comes from a Table Talk entry in which, on the account of Luther's defenders, the original has "Esdras" and not Esther, an error that Hamilton's 1834 English translation repeated. What seems sound is that in his reply to Erasmus, Luther said that if any book were to be called noncanonical Esther deserved it, and in his German Bible he removed the Greek additions because they were not in the Hebrew (Armstrong, 2010; Beggars All, 2011). I would hold that loosely, since both pages are secondary and Luther's table remarks were recorded by students, but it is enough to say that the great Reformer did not love the book.

The Jewish tradition went the other way. The twelfth century scholar Maimonides ruled that in the days of the Messiah the books of the Prophets and the Writings would be set aside, except for the scroll of Esther, which in the words of a modern teacher's rendering "will continue to exist just as the Five Books of the Torah" (Genack, 2026). A book the rabbis thought would outlast most of the Hebrew Bible is not a book in danger of being forgotten.

So the book had to earn its place among Christians, and it did. The reason it did, I think, is that readers kept finding in it something the rest of Scripture does not do as well. The prophets say what God is doing. Esther shows you what it looks like from the inside when you can't see him doing it. A church that never meets that experience soon begins to teach a faith that only works in good weather.

## A Queen Refuses and a Girl Is Chosen

The story opens in Susa, one of the Persian capitals, with a king called Ahasuerus. Modern historians usually identify him with Xerxes I, whose Persian name lies behind the Hebrew form, and who ruled from 486 to 465 BC (Xerxes I, n.d.). He is the Xerxes whose fleet lost the sea battle of Salamis to the Greeks in September 480 BC. In the third year of his reign he gives a banquet for his nobles and officers that lasts a hundred and eighty days, followed by a week of feasting for everyone in the citadel (Esther 1:3 to 5). It reads like a man showing off. A person who is quietly sure of his power does not need half a year to prove it.

At the end of the week he sends for his queen, Vashti, to come before the guests with her crown on, so that they may look at her beauty. She refuses. The text doesn't say why, and I would be inventing a motive if I claimed to know. Some readers think the summons was harmless. Others point to the drink, the all male gathering and the stress on her looks, and suspect something more demeaning than a formal appearance. Either way, the king burns with anger, his advisers panic that every wife in the empire will copy her, and she is removed by a law that cannot be changed (Esther 1:10 to 22).

Vashti is not the hero. The narrator moves on from her quickly, and what I notice is how small the machinery is. A wounded king, a panel of courtiers who are frankly afraid of their wives, a rule that can't be taken back. Nobody in that room thinks he is serving anyone's plan. Yet the vacancy they create is the one the story needs. This is the thing about providence that unsettles comfortable theology. God, if he is at work here, is working through petty and even shameful decisions, and nobody in them is a saint.

> *“The king’s heart is a stream of water in the hand of the LORD; he turns it wherever he will.”*
> — Proverbs 21:1 (ESV)

A search begins for a new queen. Young women are gathered from across the empire and prepared for twelve months before each is taken in to the king (Esther 2:12 to 14). Among them is a Jewish orphan named Hadassah, raised by her older cousin Mordecai, who is of the tribe of Benjamin and descended from those carried into exile from Jerusalem (Esther 2:5 to 7). She is known in the palace by her second name, Esther. Mordecai tells her to say nothing of her people (Esther 2:10).

That instruction can be read as cowardice, and I don't think it is. It belongs with Joseph, who lived in Potiphar's house and Pharaoh's court without becoming an Egyptian in his soul, and with Daniel, who served Babylon faithfully and did not hand over who he was. A hidden identity is a card held face down. Whether it is wise depends on whether the day ever comes to turn it over.

The dates show how slowly the story moves. The text places Esther's presentation to the king in his seventh year (Esther 2:16), which on the usual reckoning is within a couple of years of Salamis. A king who has just been humbled by the Greeks comes home and wants a new queen. A reader might imagine God preparing a Jewish queen for decades. The text claims something smaller and more believable. A king lost a war, a queen lost her place, and a girl with no power happened to be in the right city. The king prefers her above all the others and sets the royal crown on her head (Esther 2:17).

Then comes a detail that is easy to read past. Mordecai, sitting at the king's gate, overhears two of the king's doorkeepers plotting to kill him. He tells Esther, she tells the king in Mordecai's name, the plot is checked, the men are executed, and the matter is written into the royal chronicles (Esther 2:21 to 23).

And the book moves on. Nothing comes to Mordecai. The record goes into a store and stays there.

## The Man Who Could Not Bear One Refusal

Chapter 3 introduces Haman, whom the king promotes above all his officials, and the temperature in the book drops. The king's servants at the gate are ordered to bow to him. Mordecai doesn't.

The text is brief about why. It says only that when the others asked him day after day, he told them he was a Jew (Esther 3:4). I should be careful here, because it is tempting to say that Mordecai refused because the commandments forbid bowing to anyone but God (Exodus 20:3 to 5). That cannot be the whole of it. Jacob bowed to Esau and Joseph's brothers bowed to Joseph, and nobody in Genesis counts it as idolatry. The honour paid to a prince was one thing, and the worship due to God another. Whatever Mordecai saw in this particular bow, the book gives us his identity, not a ruling. A Jew was someone who would not do this.

There is a history behind it all the same. Haman is called an Agagite, and Agag was the Amalekite king whom Saul failed to destroy when God told him to (1 Samuel 15:8 to 9). Mordecai's line runs back through a man called Kish (Esther 2:5), the name of Saul's father, and the text seems to want us to hear the echo. An old quarrel between two families may be walking around Susa in court dress.

What Haman does next is the part that ought to alarm us. A normal offended official wants the offender disciplined or removed. Haman looks at one man who won't bow and decides that Mordecai's whole people must go (Esther 3:5 to 6). Scapegoating works like that. It takes a single grievance and finds a whole category of people to carry it, and it has never needed much more than a persuasive tone and a willing signature. Don't tell me that evil is only structural, a matter of poor institutions and inherited habits that better schooling will iron out. Esther 3 shows what can live in the heart of a man who has the ring, the rank and the bowing crowd, and who still can't enjoy any of it because one person at the gate stays upright.

> *“Pride goes before destruction, a haughty spirit before a fall.”*
> — Proverbs 16:18 (NIV)

He goes to the king and describes this people in careful, bloodless language. They are scattered, their laws differ from everyone else's, and it does not profit the king to tolerate them (Esther 3:8). He offers silver. The king doesn't ask who they are or what they have done. He hands over his ring. Lots are cast, which is where the festival of Purim gets its name, to choose a day, and the day chosen is the thirteenth of the twelfth month, Adar (Esther 3:7, 13). On the dates the book supplies, the decree is issued in the twelfth year of the king's reign, about 474 BC, and the killing is set for March 473 BC (Book of Esther, n.d.).

The couriers ride out. The king and Haman sit down to drink, and the text tells us the city of Susa was perplexed (Esther 3:15).

## What Mordecai Actually Said to Esther

When the news reaches Mordecai he tears his clothes, puts on sackcloth and ashes, and goes through the city crying aloud. Esther, inside the palace, hears of it, and the two of them begin to exchange messages through a servant. He sends her a copy of the decree and asks her to go to the king and plead for her people (Esther 4:8).

She hesitates, and I don't think we should judge her for it. She reminds Mordecai of the rule every servant of the king knows. Anyone who goes into the inner court without being summoned is put to death, unless the king holds out his golden sceptre, and she has not been called to him for thirty days (Esther 4:11). It was a rule with teeth. Herodotus, who wrote within living memory of Xerxes' reign, says that when six men joined Darius in his coup they were granted the right to enter the king's palace unannounced, except when he was with a woman (Herodotus, 1920 to 1925, 3.84). That privilege would have meant nothing if everybody else could walk in. Esther isn't dramatising. She is counting the cost out loud.

Mordecai's reply is the hinge of the book, and it is a strange mixture of comfort and threat. It is also one of the most quoted lines in the Bible, and it is quoted so freely, on conference banners and campaign posters alike, that it is worth reading in its place.

> *“Do not think to yourself that in the king’s palace you will escape any more than all the other Jews. For if you keep silent at this time, relief and deliverance will rise for the Jews from another place, but you and your father’s house will perish. And who knows whether you have not come to the kingdom for such a time as this?”*
> — Esther 4:13 to 14 (ESV)

Look at what he assumes. He assumes that the people will be saved, and that the saving doesn't depend on Esther. That is a claim about God's purposes without the name of God. What he refuses to assume is that she will be part of it. She might sit it out, in which case she and her family will be lost along with everything she thought she had secured. There is a hard sentence there about position. Esther was not a bystander who happened to have influence. She was a Jew in the palace, and the palace would not protect her. Her safety was borrowed.

Then comes the last sentence, and it is a question. Who knows? He isn't announcing that she was put there for this purpose. He is asking her to consider that it might be so. To me that is an honest way for a believer to speak about providence. Mordecai claims no view from heaven. The facts of your life have an arrangement to them, he says, you can't prove who arranged it, and you are now being asked to act as though somebody did. When the line is turned into a poster, the question mark is the first thing to go, and that is when it starts to sound like flattery. It was never meant as a compliment to Esther. It was a summons.

Esther sends her answer back.

> *“Go, gather all the Jews to be found in Susa, and hold a fast on my behalf, and do not eat or drink for three days, night or day. I and my young women will also fast as you do. Then I will go to the king, though it is against the law, and if I perish, I perish.”*
> — Esther 4:16 (ESV)

This is where the book turns, and it is easy to miss what changes. Before this verse the story is something that happens to people. After it, people begin to decide. Esther has stopped managing the outcome and started offering herself to it. The line sits in a short company of sentences in Scripture that land like a fist on a table. Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego told Nebuchadnezzar that their God was able to deliver them, and that even if he did not, they would not bow (Daniel 3:17 to 18). Paul wrote from prison that to live was Christ and to die was gain (Philippians 1:21). Esther's sentence is not triumphant. It is the sentence of a person who has looked at the worst thing and decided to go in anyway.

## A Fast, Two Dinners and a Gallows

Three days of fasting follow, and then she puts on her royal robes, stands in the inner court, and the king holds out the sceptre (Esther 5:1 to 2).

It is natural to say that the fast was heard. The people of God in Susa went without food and water, and a pagan king lay awake. I feel the pull of that, and many Christian readers have drawn the line straight through. But the author never draws it. Fasting in Israel almost always went with prayer (Nehemiah 1:4 and Ezra 8:23), and I think it is fair to hear prayer behind this one. What the narrator leaves open is whether the sceptre and the sleepless night are the answer to it. He puts the two things side by side and lets you decide, which is exactly what he does everywhere else.

What Esther does next puzzles people. The king asks what she wants, offering her up to half the kingdom, and she answers by inviting him and Haman to dinner. At the dinner he asks again, and she invites them back the next evening (Esther 5:3 to 8). The text gives no reason. Maybe she was frightened. Maybe she was testing the mood. Maybe she had learned the thing impatient people find hardest, which is that an open door isn't the same as a signal to speak.

Meanwhile Haman walks home from the first dinner feeling like the most important man in Persia, until he passes Mordecai at the gate, still not bowing. He gathers his wife and friends, lists his wealth and his honours, and confesses that none of it counts for anything while that one Jew sits at the gate (Esther 5:9 to 13). They tell him what to do, and the book records the advice in a single line.

> *“Let a gallows fifty cubits high be made.”*
> — Esther 5:14 (ESV)

A cubit was about forty five centimetres, so Haman's gallows stood nearly twenty three metres tall, about the height of a seven storey building. The Hebrew word is the ordinary word for wood, and some readers take it to mean impalement on a stake, which would fit the empire's habits. Herodotus says that Darius, once he had retaken Babylon, impaled about three thousand of its leading men (Herodotus, 1920 to 1925, 3.159). Haman is ordering something the empire already did.

## How the Night Turned

That is the night we began with. On one side of Susa a queen has fasted and has one more dinner to get through. On the other side a man is admiring the gallows he has just had built. And between them, in the palace, a king who answers to neither of them can't sleep.

The chronicles are read, the old entry about Mordecai turns up, and the attendants admit that nothing has been done for him. Think about the odds of that for a moment, not as a mathematician but as a clerk. Of all the pages in the royal archives, this reader finds this page, on the one night it could save a life. Then Haman walks in, early, to ask permission to hang Mordecai. The king speaks first. What should be done for a man the king wants to honour? Haman, certain the king means him, describes robes from the king's wardrobe, the king's horse and a herald crying out through the square. The king tells him to do all of that for Mordecai the Jew, and to leave out nothing (Esther 6:4 to 10).

The text doesn't describe Haman's face. It doesn't need to.

The next detail is easy to pass over. When Haman goes home humiliated, his wife and his advisers tell him that if Mordecai is of the Jewish people, he will not overcome him but will surely fall before him (Esther 6:13). It is the nearest the book comes to a creed, and it is spoken by the household of the villain. In Esther the people who sense that something is working against them are the ones with the most to lose.

> *“He who watches over Israel will neither slumber nor sleep.”*
> — Psalm 121:4 (NIV)

I am not saying the king's insomnia was a miracle, or that the psalmist had it in mind. The psalm was not written about Susa. But a person who believes in a God who does not slumber will read a night like this one differently from a person who doesn't, and the author of Esther seems to have written so that both readings remain open.

At the second dinner Esther finally speaks. She says that if her people had merely been sold as slaves she would have kept silent, but they have been sold to be destroyed (Esther 7:3 to 4). The secret Mordecai told her to keep is the only card she holds, and she plays it at the one moment it can still count. The king storms out into the garden. When he comes back, Haman has thrown himself on the couch where Esther is lying to beg for his life, and the king takes it for an assault. One of the attendants mentions that a gallows fifty cubits high is standing in Haman's courtyard, and Haman is hanged on it (Esther 7:5 to 10).

> *“Do not be deceived: God is not mocked, for whatever one sows, that will he also reap.”*
> — Galatians 6:7 (ESV)

At every step, God, if he is acting here, allowed the plan to proceed. Haman's pride drafted the decree. Haman's vanity arranged the parade that honoured his enemy. Haman's rage built the gallows that received him. The gallows were not prevented. They were repurposed. And that is the part of the story that makes uncomfortable reading for anyone trained to think God's goodness is measured by how quickly he steps in. We want the deliverance before the decree is signed. In Esther it comes later than we would have chosen, and it is more complete than we would have planned.

The decree itself could not be cancelled, because Persian law did not allow it. So a second decree went out, with Mordecai now holding the king's ring in Haman's place, allowing the Jews to defend themselves (Esther 8:2, 8 to 11). Fighting followed on the appointed day. The book counts five hundred killed in Susa and seventy five thousand in the provinces (Esther 9:6, 16), and it adds, three times, that the Jews did not lay a hand on the plunder (Esther 9:10, 15, 16). Many readers see a deliberate answer to Saul, who took the spoil of the Amalekites when he had been told not to. The old failure is undone, in the same family line, five centuries on.

Mordecai and Esther then establish the festival of Purim, named for the lots Haman cast, as an annual celebration of feasting, gladness, gifts of food to friends and gifts to the poor (Esther 9:20 to 22). Jewish communities still keep it. This year it was marked from the evening of 2 March to 3 March, with the whole scroll read aloud, a festive meal, charity to the poor and gifts of food (Hebcal, n.d.). The festival remembers a rescue that arrived without a heavenly voice. That may be why the scroll is read to the end every year.

## Coincidence and the Cost of Deliverance

The strongest objection to all of this is simple, and a sceptic would put it fairly like this. Stories collect coincidences. A king who can't sleep, a clerk whose good deed was filed and forgotten, a reading of the exact page in the chronicle, a villain who builds his own gallows. In a long enough history a few things line up. And in a book that never says God did any of it, why would you be the one to say so? You would be reading your own faith into the gaps.

I would concede the logic. The author of Esther never gives you a proof, and I suspect that is deliberate. If the book had written "and God made the king sleepless", it would have given the sceptic nothing to argue about and the believer nothing to do. As it stands, the evidence is exactly as ambiguous as most of our lives are. You can read the night of Esther 6 as luck or as a hand. The book will not take that decision away from you.

I would add two things. The first is that the coincidences are not scattered. They have a direction. A queen is removed, a girl is promoted, a plot is foiled and forgotten, a refusal to bow is magnified into a decree, and everything converges on one night, one entry and one reversal. A single lucky break is luck. A chain in which every link seems made to bear the weight of the next is harder to wave away. That is why Shaw can say that God works behind the scenes without reading a name into the text (Shaw, n.d.).

The second is that Esther does not describe a world where hidden providence keeps everybody safe. The Jews of Susa were not spared the decree. They were spared its result, at the price of a battle, and the book counts the dead on the other side. That is a hard passage and I'm not going to tidy it. The deliverance in Esther is real and it is not clean. It comes with fear, it needs courage, and it doesn't undo the grief of anyone who was in the way.

There is a further honest thing to say. Mordecai's good deed went unrewarded for years. The king whose sleeplessness saved a people was himself murdered in August 465 BC by Artabanus, the captain of his own bodyguard (Xerxes I, n.d.). Nothing in Esther claims that well placed providence makes a palace safe. Providence in this book is not a guarantee that the right people stay alive. It is the claim that no decree, however irrevocable, and no villain, however well supplied, is the last word on the people of God.

One more thing about how Christians read it. The New Testament never quotes Esther, and some Christian readers have found Christ in it anyway, hearing Esther's walk into the throne room as an echo of intercession. I respect the instinct, but the book never asks to be read that way, and I would not rest the argument on it. What I think the book does support is a smaller and sturdier claim. The God who is hidden here is the God who does not abandon his people. Isaiah 45:15 put the two together long before Esther was written down.

## What Christians Mean by Providence

It helps to say precisely what we are claiming, because the word is used loosely. Providence is not a general optimism that things work out. It is the doctrine that God preserves what he has made and guides it, all of it and every part, to the ends he has chosen.

The Reformed churches have stated this most carefully. The Westminster Confession of 1646 says in chapter 5 that God upholds, directs, disposes and governs all creatures and things, "by his most wise and holy providence", according to his free and unchangeable counsel (Westminster Assembly, 1646/n.d.). It adds at once that this does not turn creatures into puppets. All things come to pass infallibly under God's decree, yet he orders them according to the nature of second causes, whether necessarily, freely or contingently. In his ordinary providence he uses means, though he is free to work without them, above them or against them. And the Confession refuses to make God the author of sin. His providence reaches even to the fall and to every sin of angels and men, not by bare permission, it says, but by a wise and powerful bounding and governing of them to his holy ends, while "the sinfulness thereof proceedeth only from the creature, and not from God" (Westminster Assembly, 1646/n.d.).

Look at Haman through that lens. The decree was Haman's, with every bit of its malice, and the guilt is wholly his. The night of the king's sleeplessness, the page, the dinner and the gallows are, on this account, all ordinary means, working through second causes, and no one in the book is overridden. That is exactly the shape of Esther. Esther is a textbook case of providence in the ordinary use of means.

Not every Christian holds this in the same form, and a fair account states the others in their own terms. Catholic and Orthodox teaching affirms that God governs creation and acts through secondary causes and human cooperation, and both traditions read the longer Greek Esther as Scripture. Arminian and Wesleyan Christians affirm God's providential care and his foreknowledge, and hold that God works through free human choices without determining them. Molinists, following the sixteenth century Jesuit Luis de Molina, say that God knows what free creatures would do in any circumstance and arranges the world accordingly. Open theists hold that the future is partly open even to God, so that he responds to events as well as ordaining them. On each of these accounts the book of Esther is a story of providence, though they tell different stories about how much of it was settled in advance.

I am more at home with the Reformed account, because it takes both Haman's guilt and God's rule seriously without turning one into the other. But the point I would urge on every reader is the one they all share. Whatever the mechanism, the Christian doctrine says that the silence is not emptiness, and that is what Romans 8:28 has always claimed.

> *“And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose.”*
> — Romans 8:28 (NIV)

The line gets printed on mugs, and the weight of it is easy to lose that way. In Esther you can see what it looks like when it is true. Every single thing in the book works toward the deliverance, the bad things included. A king's temper, a drunken command, a villain's grudge, an insomniac night. And nobody in the book is given a way of seeing it while it happens. The people of Susa fasted and waited without knowing how it would end. Notice that Paul promises good, which is a promise about where things are heading, and says nothing about comfort along the way.

## Waiting on the Near Side of the Reversal

Picture a young woman in Enugu, a scene I am inventing, though you may recognise her. She finished her degree two years ago. She has sent out applications, queued for interviews, and prayed through the nights with her mother. Her friends post their good news on WhatsApp status and she types a congratulation each time. Nothing has moved. If someone tells her that God is working all things together, she is entitled to ask for evidence.

Esther's answer to her is neither a slogan nor a scolding. It hands her a story in which the evidence is only visible from the far side of the reversal, and then it asks what a person does on the near side. The book of Esther is told from the far side. Esther herself lived on the near side, and so do we. The Jews of Susa spent nearly a year under a decree with a date on it. The fast ended and the sceptre had not yet moved. That is the long Wednesday, the day on which the prayer has been said, the decree has gone out, you have no countermeasure, and you do not know how this ends.

I would not tell her that her waiting has a purpose I can see. I can't see it, and anyone who says they can ought to be asked for their source. I would say a few ordinary things. The record of faithfulness is not lost, and it may be in an archive nobody has opened yet. A closed door in Susa was also the place where a king's sleep was being arranged. And it is possible to do the next faithful thing while the explanation hasn't come. Esther did not know the king would be awake. She went in anyway.

Believers who want an African commentator at their elbow will find one in the Africa Bible Commentary, which Tokunboh Adeyemo edited and which was written by African scholars with the stated aim of relating the Bible to African realities today (Africa Bible Commentary, n.d.). It is worth having beside Esther when the waiting is long.

There is a modest thing to try this week. Read Esther in one sitting, all ten chapters, which will take less time than most of the videos on your phone. Don't hunt for a lesson. Watch for the places where the author could have written God's name and left a blank. Then think of the one thing in your own life you have been waiting to see explained, and ask whether there is something in front of you that you can do while the explanation hasn't come.

And if you are tired, ask yourself honestly whether you have been waiting on a God who is absent, or on one who is acting where you cannot yet see. Esther will not answer that for you. It says only that the two can look exactly alike from where you are standing, and that the Bible you are holding speaks of a Keeper who neither slumbers nor sleeps.
