## The Rulers Who Did Not Understand

> *"But we impart a secret and hidden wisdom of God, which God decreed before the ages for our glory. None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 2:7 to 8 (ESV)

Read that slowly, because it says something about the devil that most of us never expect to hear. We tend to picture him as the cleverest figure in the story, always a move ahead, working in the dark while we scramble to keep up. Paul says the powers behind the cross did not understand what they were doing. Commentators argue over whether "the rulers of this age" means Pilate and the council, or the spiritual forces behind them, or both. I think both, and the argument below holds whichever way you land.

Anyone who has sat through an all night programme in which the enemy was named more often than the Lord knows the assumption I mean. The devil is always ahead, the believer is always catching up, and every setback proves a masterstroke. It makes for lively prayer points. It also gives him a good deal more credit than Paul does.

There is a second assumption underneath that one, and it is quieter. Somewhere between the way we sing about the cross and the way we talk about the fall of man, many of us picked up a story in which heaven was caught off guard. Nobody says it aloud, least of all in church, where suggesting that God was surprised would earn you an usher and a side eye from three rows away. But it sits in the grammar of our theology. Man fell, heaven was shaken, and God in his mercy arranged a rescue. The cross becomes the answer to a problem nobody saw coming. Jesus is Plan B, and the atonement is a reaction.

The story is moving, and it is mistaken, and the two mistakes feed each other. If God was surprised, the devil must have been clever. If the devil is clever, we must live as refugees from a battle that went badly. The New Testament tells a different story. A brilliant and dangerous creature read the most important event in history and got it wrong, and his reading is what carried it out. To see how, we need to look at what he could see, what had been settled before he arrived, and what he thought he was doing on the Friday.

## A God Who Was Not Surprised

The Bible doesn't describe a God who scrambled. It describes a God who had a purpose before there was anything to be surprised by. Paul begins his letter to the Ephesians by saying so, in one long breathless sentence that English translators have to break into pieces.

> *"Even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him."*
>
> — Ephesians 1:4 (ESV)

Before the foundation of the world. The sentence goes on to speak of adoption, of redemption through his blood, and of a purpose he set forth "as a plan for the fullness of time, to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth" (Ephesians 1:10, ESV). Nothing in it sounds like a reaction. It sounds like an architect talking about a building that is still going up, who has known from the first drawing what the finished roof will look like.

Peter says the same of Christ himself. Having told his readers that they were ransomed by "the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot," he adds that Christ "was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times for the sake of you" (1 Peter 1:19 to 20, ESV). And Paul, in the passage we began with, speaks of a wisdom "which God decreed before the ages." The cross was not an emergency measure. It was an intention, held in God before Adam had a name.

Here Christian thinkers have not all agreed, and it is worth saying so. Thomas Aquinas thought that the Son would probably not have become flesh if humanity had not sinned, because the Incarnation is aimed at the remedy (Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, III, q. 1, a. 3). Duns Scotus argued the other way, that Christ was always the goal of creation and that sin only changed the shape of what he came to do. I'm not going to settle that between them. What both of them hold, and what Paul and Peter plainly say, is that the cross did not catch God off balance. Where they differ is whether the Incarnation would have happened without the fall. Neither of them thinks the fall surprised God.

And that, I want to say plainly, is the best news in the argument. A God who reacts to disasters might fail to keep up. A God whose purposes were never in danger is the God you can bring a collapsing business, a sick child or a long delay to. The sovereign God of Ephesians 1 doesn't get ambushed by your Tuesday either.

## Where the Phrase Attaches in Revelation

The text most often quoted on this theme needs care, because the way English Bibles print it can mislead. Here is the verse in the English Standard Version as it reads now.

> *"And all who dwell on earth will worship it, everyone whose name has not been written from the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain."*
>
> — Revelation 13:8 (ESV)

Set that beside the New International Version, which ends the verse with "the Lamb who was slain from the creation of the world." The difference isn't vocabulary. In the Greek, the phrase "from the foundation of the world" sits at the end of a long sentence and can hang on either of two verbs. It can modify "written", so that the names were written from the foundation of the world. Or it can modify "slain", so that the Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world. The ESV takes the first reading and the NIV takes the second. The English versions split down the middle, and so did the older commentators. The handbook for translators by Bratcher and Hatton (1993) says most English versions connect the phrase to "written", relying partly on Revelation 17:8, and recommends that reading. Yet it also notes that the NIV and others attach it to "slain", with 1 Peter 1:19 to 20 as a parallel. A 2025 article in the *Biblical Studies Journal* by A. Thirumurugan favours "written" on the strength of 17:8 but calls the other reading "equally valid".

The parallel in 17:8 is the strongest card for "written." There the ESV reads that the dwellers on earth are those "whose names have not been written in the book of life from the foundation of the world." Nothing is left to hang the phrase on but "written." The card for "slain" is the word order, and nothing is wrong with it.

One more thing affects anyone with an ESV on a shelf or a phone. For years it said "before the foundation of the world" in this verse. The Greek word is the ordinary preposition for "from", and in its 2025 text edition the translation committee changed "before" to "from", one of sixty eight word changes (as reported by Text and Translation and by Protestia, and the ESV site now prints "from"). If your copy still says "before", it carries the older text.

None of this is a reason to give up the doctrine. It is a reason not to stand on one verse alone, and we don't have to. Ephesians 1:4 and 1 Peter 1:19 to 20 carry the same weight with no grammatical wobble, and 1 Corinthians 2:7 does the same for the wisdom. On either reading of Revelation 13:8, the book belongs to the Lamb, and the Lamb is the one who was slain. A plan settled before the world began had no vacancy in it for the devil to fill. He walked into a story that had already been told.

## A Rebellion Older Than Eden

Now to the devil himself, and where he came from. Many of us were taught that the story begins in the courts of heaven, with a creature of great beauty and intelligence who looked at the throne of God and wanted it. The passage that carries that teaching is Isaiah 14.

> *"I will ascend above the heights of the clouds; I will make myself like the Most High."*
>
> — Isaiah 14:14 (ESV)

Count the "I wills" in the speech, as the old preachers did. There are five. I will ascend to heaven, I will set my throne above the stars of God, I will sit on the mount of assembly, I will ascend above the clouds, I will make myself like the Most High. A creature with finite power speaks in the grammar of infinite intention, and every sentence is a promissory note he has no currency to honour. The name Lucifer, often attached to the speaker, comes from the Latin word for the morning star in the Vulgate's rendering of verse 12.

I should be honest about the debate, because a reader with a good study Bible will find it. Origen and Tertullian read Isaiah 14, and Ezekiel 28 beside it, as speaking of the devil, and so did a long line of preachers after them. But Isaiah introduces the speech as a taunt against the king of Babylon, and a great many careful commentators, from Calvin to John Oswalt, read it as about human kings and the pride of empire, with Daniel Block taking Ezekiel 28 the same way (these are summarised in Spriggs, n.d.). Some of them allow that a darker power stands behind such rulers. The United Bible Societies handbook (Ogden & Sterk, 2011) reads the passage as a taunt aimed at the king of Babylon, with echoes of an older story of a figure thrown down from heaven. So the first reading is a traditional one with real support, and the second is the plain reading of the context.

I rest instead on the New Testament, which says the same thing without needing Isaiah to testify. John writes that "the devil has been sinning from the beginning" (1 John 3:8, ESV). Paul warns that a new convert might become puffed up and "fall into the condemnation of the devil" (1 Timothy 3:6), which implies that pride was the devil's own undoing. Jesus says of him that he was "a murderer from the beginning" (John 8:44). Jude and Peter speak of angels who did not keep their position (Jude 6 and 2 Peter 2:4). And Jesus himself says, in the same breath as the commissioning of the seventy two, that he saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven (Luke 10:18). So the picture stands even if you hold Isaiah 14 at arm's length. Sin isn't a human invention. It's an import, and the devil had been rebellious a long time before the serpent spoke in a garden.

The consequence is the part that matters here. When we talk of the fall of man as though it were the origin of evil, we start the story in the second chapter. Revelation 12 describes a war in heaven in which Michael and his angels fought the dragon, and he was thrown down. Eden was not the beginning of the conflict. It was a second front. Adam didn't create a problem that God then had to solve. Adam became the door through which a rebellion that was already underway gained a foothold on earth. And the God who had seen it unfold in heaven had, as Paul says, made his provision before the world had a foundation.

## What the Serpent Heard in Eden

Notice how the devil works in Genesis 3. His first move is a question about what God said, and the whole conversation is about God's words and God's motives. He never has to attack God's power. He plants a suspicion about God's character, and the first couple do the rest. Anyone who has watched a rumour do its work in a church or a family knows the method. It needs no miracle, only a listener who half believes it.

Then he reaches for the lure, and here the irony is almost too rich. "You will be like God," the serpent says (Genesis 3:5, ESV). The creature whose own catastrophe began with the words "I will make myself like the Most High" now offers the same bait to humanity. He could not take the throne, so he went after the image of the one who sat on it. If he could not have God, perhaps he could have God's proxy, the creature made in his likeness and given dominion over the earth. And it worked. So thoroughly did it work that when Jesus came, the devil could stand on a mountain and offer him the kingdoms of the world.

But he had also heard his sentence, early. Before Adam and Eve were out of the garden, before the curse had been spoken over the ground, God spoke to the serpent.

> *"I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel."*
>
> — Genesis 3:15 (ESV)

Theologians call this the protoevangelium, the first gospel. The church has long heard in it the promise of a descendant who would defeat the serpent, and Irenaeus of Lyons, in the second century, built his reading of the cross on exactly that enmity. God didn't wait for humanity to ask for a saviour. He announced one before humanity had the vocabulary to ask. That is a mercy so far ahead of our failure that it was waiting at the place where we fell.

The devil could read the words. What he could not read was how a wound to the heel would be the thing that crushed his head. That depended on something he had no category for, a God who pays the cost of his own mercy.

## Foreknown and Still Freely Done

A sharp reader will push back here, and the objection deserves its full strength. If the cross was decreed before the ages, and God knew Adam would sin, then is God responsible for the sin? Worse, the whole thing starts to look like a performance, a script in which the devil and Judas and Pilate only read lines they were handed. Either they are innocent, since nobody can be blamed for following a script, or God has made himself the author of the worst crime in history.

Christians have answered in three main ways, and each deserves to be stated as its own holders would. The Arminian says that God foresees what free creatures will choose without causing it, and that his foreknowledge no more makes the choice necessary than my knowing that the sun will rise makes it rise. The Reformed theologian says that God ordains all things and that creatures still act freely in the sense that they do what they want to do, and the Westminster Confession adds that God is not the author of sin and that no violence is done to the will of the creature. The Molinist, following the Jesuit Luis de Molina, says that God knows what any free creature would freely do in any circumstance and arranges the world accordingly. I find none of these a complete map. Nobody can give a full account of where God's purpose ends and a creature's choice begins, and Peter doesn't try. He says both things in one sentence.

> *"This Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men."*
>
> — Acts 2:23 (ESV)

The plan is God's. The crucifying is theirs, and Peter charges them with it. The early church prays in the same shape when Peter and John are released, saying that Herod and Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel gathered "to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (Acts 4:28, ESV). The same Gospel that says "Satan entered into Judas called Iscariot" goes on to say that Judas "went away and conferred with the chief priests and officers how he might betray him to them" (Luke 22:3 to 4, ESV). The devil moves, and the man deliberates. Both are in the text.

Think of a sting operation, since most Nigerians have a feel for how those work. Detectives who plan one to catch a thief don't become the authors of the theft. They knew it was coming, they built the room it would happen in, and the thief still walked in on his own two feet and took the money. I'd be careful with the comparison, because God did not set a trap for something he wished would happen. But it shows how foreknowledge and guilt can sit together without one cancelling the other. A parent who warns a child away from the edge of the swimming pool knows, with the knowledge that comes from loving a small reckless creature, that the child will very likely run anyway. The knowing doesn't make the running any less the child's choice, and a good parent prepares for it. The difference with God is that his preparation was complete. The provision was his own Son, and it was made before the accident was possible.

The apostles felt no obligation to dissolve the puzzle. They preached the plan to comfort the frightened and the guilt to wake the careless, and saw no conflict between the two sermons. I think we can live with the same tension, as we do whenever we thank God for a harvest and still plant the seed ourselves. What it does to the devil is clear. He was never directing the cross. He was acting within it, and the part he thought was his own idea was written into a plan older than he was.

## A Creature, Not a Rival

Here we need to talk about the devil's strategic thinking, and it takes a measure of charity, because Lucifer is no fool. He is a creature of great intelligence, long experience and real cunning. The tragedy of his position is that he is brilliant and still playing the wrong game.

Most Nigerians have played ludo. Each player's pieces begin in a yard, race the same track on the same board, and can be sent home when an opponent's piece lands on the same square. It is a game of equal players on one board, and the whole thing runs on the assumption that your opponent is your rival. Chess is the better picture of the devil's own intelligence, since it rewards foresight and a clear head. A good chess player sees three moves ahead. But even chess is a game between two sides of the same kind, and the devil has misjudged that.

Look at what the wilderness shows. The devil took Jesus up and showed him all the kingdoms of the world.

> *"And the devil took him up and showed him all the kingdoms of the world in a moment of time, and said to him, 'To you I will give all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whom I will. If you, then, will worship me, it will all be yours.'"*
>
> — Luke 4:5 to 7 (ESV)

Jesus doesn't call it a bluff. Later he calls the devil "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31, ESV), so the title has some standing. Yet look at the grammar of the boast. The authority "has been delivered to me." Delivered by whom? The passive voice does quiet work. The ruler of this world is a tenant with a borrowed key, and the one boasting of it is also the liar. He offers all of it for a single act of worship, which tells you what he wanted more than the kingdoms.

Think what that offer assumes. The devil is offering the Son of God something that already belongs to him, since "the earth is the Lord's and the fullness thereof" (Psalm 24:1). It is playing ludo against the one who made the board and the dice and the people who play. A rival would have bargained for terms. A creature takes what it is allowed. That is why the book of Job has him asking permission and given a limit on what he may touch.

> *"And the Lord said to Satan, 'Behold, all that he has is in your hand. Only against him do not stretch out your hand.'"*
>
> — Job 1:12 (ESV)

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy, "You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve" (Luke 4:8, ESV), and the exchange ends. The devil knows his Bible. In Matthew's version of the same scene he quotes Psalm 91 at Jesus (Matthew 4:6), and he quotes it accurately. Knowing the words was never his problem. What he lacked was the author's purpose, and a man can know every verse of a letter and still misread what the writer wants.

Jesus didn't even dignify the offer with a theological argument. "Away from me, Satan," in the words of Matthew 4:10. That isn't frustration. It's the calm of someone who knows how the game ends.

## God Enters as a Baby

Here is what the devil did not see coming. He did not see it because there was no precedent, no category in his long intelligence that could have prepared him. God himself would enter creation.

Not as a conqueror arriving in power to take back by force what had been lost. As a baby. In a feeding trough in Bethlehem, under the rule of an occupying empire, born to parents who had to explain the circumstances to anyone who cared to ask. C. S. Lewis described the world as enemy occupied territory, with the rightful king landed in disguise (*Mere Christianity*, Book 2, chapter 2). The disguise was the point. The King of eternity arrived in the most defenceless form creation has to offer, and the enemy simply had no playbook for this move.

> *"But when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons."*
>
> — Galatians 4:4 to 5 (ESV)

"The fullness of time." God didn't rush. He waited through the covenants with Noah and Abraham and Moses and David, through the prophets and their declarations, until every piece was in place. Then he came. And the writer of Hebrews explains what the move was for.

> *"Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things, that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery."*
>
> — Hebrews 2:14 to 15 (ESV)

The genius of the Incarnation is not only that God became human. He became mortal. He took on the very condition through which the enemy held his power, in order to take that power apart from inside. John says it as plainly as it can be said.

> *"The reason the Son of God appeared was to destroy the works of the devil."*
>
> — 1 John 3:8 (ESV)

Not to improve them or negotiate with them. The Incarnation was no pastoral visit. It was an act of war, and the most unexpected one in the history of creation.

## How the Devil Read Good Friday

Scripture doesn't tell us what the devil was thinking on that Friday, and I won't pretend it does. What we can say is that, by every measure he understood, the plan worked. The man who had claimed his territory, who had sent him away in the wilderness and driven his servants out of the sick, was arrested at night. His friends scattered. A court found a reason, a governor found it convenient, and by mid afternoon he was dead. By evening he was buried, and a stone and a guard were set at the door.

Read the way a rival would read it, it was a clean win. The devil worked through the religious establishment and the machinery of Roman power, and by his own reckoning he had captured the king. Kill the Son of God, end the threat, hold the dominion for good. The idea of a contest between two sides on one board would have made the capture of the Son look like the winning throw.

But the Lamb was never a piece on his board. He was the one who made it, and he gave himself to be taken. The devil assumed a contest between rivals. What he walked into was an event of another kind altogether.

There is a reason he missed it. A creature that wants to be worshipped measures glory by what it can grab and keep. A king who allows himself to be arrested, mocked and nailed up is, by that measure, throwing the game. Nothing in the devil's reckoning allowed for a God whose glory is shown by giving himself away. A sacrifice in chess works on an opponent who takes the offered piece because he can see what he gains and cannot see what it costs.

And the mistake is easily passed on. Scripture records that Peter, who had just confessed Jesus as the Christ, took Jesus aside and told him the cross must not happen, and that Jesus answered as though the voice were Satan's own (Matthew 16:21 to 23). A good man who could not imagine a Messiah who suffers had picked up the devil's reading without noticing. That should make us humble. The misreading is not only in the pit. It sits in any pulpit that promises a Christ who spares us every cross.

The devil had also misjudged his own best weapon. Death was his instrument, and the writer of Hebrews says so directly. The Son took flesh so that through death he might destroy the one who held death's power. The devil's strongest tool was the one the plan intended him to use.

The early church noticed and reached for pictures. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyons in the second century, wrote that the enemy would not have been "fairly vanquished" unless it had been a man born of woman who conquered him (Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, 5.21.1). Augustine, bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached in the fifth century that the cross had been a trap. In the Schaff edition of Sermon 130 (as quoted online by Transfigurism), he says: "The Redeemer came and the deceiver was overcome." He goes on to say that Satan "could indeed shed that blood; but he deserved not to drink it." Those are pictures, and sermons are not creeds, but the pictures catch what Paul states plainly in Corinthians. The powers did not see it coming.

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, also an African, wrote in *On the Incarnation* that the cross had turned death on its head. In the English of Archibald Robertson's translation, the devil is like "a tyrant" who "has been defeated by a real king, and bound hand and foot," so that "even women who were formerly deceived by him, now mock at him as dead and paralyzed" (Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*, 27.4). A little later he says that a lion made sport of by children must be dead or powerless (29.5). What strikes me is how little Athanasius thinks of the devil's cleverness. Two Africans, a century or so apart, and neither of them is impressed by him.

## Many Pictures of One Cross

At this point a careful reader will want to ask what exactly the cross did, and whether "victory over the devil" is the whole of it. It is not, and the other accounts deserve to be heard in their own best words. The church has used several pictures of the atonement, and Gustaf Aulén, a Swedish Lutheran bishop and theologian, gave the modern discussion its shape in *Christus Victor* (1931, translated by A. G. Hebert from the Swedish of 1930).

Aulén sorted the historic accounts into three types. The first he called the classic or dramatic view, the one he named Christus Victor, in which the atonement is "a victory over the powers which hold mankind in bondage." Christ fights and conquers sin, death and the devil, and God himself is the victor in the contest. The second he called the Latin or objective type, associated with Anselm of Canterbury and later with the Protestant Reformers, in which Christ satisfies the demands of God's honour or justice. The third he called the subjective type, associated with Peter Abelard, in which the cross changes human hearts by showing God's love. Aulén argued that the classic view dominated the church's first thousand years, held by the Fathers from Irenaeus on, and returned in Luther. That historical thesis has been challenged, particularly by those who defend satisfaction and argue that the Father and the Son are never opposed in the cross. But the typology has stuck.

Let me set out the main accounts, because each belongs to a tradition that its holders are right to want stated fairly.

The ransom picture takes its cue from Jesus' saying that the Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Origen and Gregory of Nyssa pushed it further and said the ransom was paid to the devil, with the humanity of Christ as the bait that hid the hook of his divinity. That stretch was criticised in the church from early on. Gregory of Nazianzus asks to whom the blood was offered, and answers sharply that if it was offered to the Evil One, "fie upon the outrage!", since the robber would then receive as ransom God himself (Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 45). Anselm, seven centuries later, gave a chapter of *Cur Deus Homo* to explaining "how the devil had no justice on his side against man." So the best of the tradition says the devil had power over us and no right to it.

Recapitulation is the picture of Irenaeus. Christ sums up Adam in himself, goes through the human story from birth to death obediently where Adam failed, and so reverses the disobedience. Irenaeus also taught that God redeemed his own property from the apostate power "not by violent means" (*Against Heresies*, 5.1.1), a nice correction of anyone who thinks the victory was a show of force. The Eastern churches build on it. They speak of theosis, which means that the Word became what we are so that we might share his life, and their Easter hymns speak of Christ trampling down death by death.

Satisfaction is Anselm's. In *Cur Deus Homo* (late eleventh century) he argued that sin robs God of the honour owed to him, that humanity could not repay, and that only one who is both God and man could. The Catholic tradition took it up in Aquinas and at the Council of Trent, which says Christ "made satisfaction" for us to the Father. Anselm's logic is not about the devil at all. It is about God and the debt.

Penal substitution is the Reformers' development, in Calvin and after. It says that the debt was a penalty, that the law's sentence of death fell on the sinner, and that Christ bore it in our place. Isaiah 53, "upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace" (Isaiah 53:5, ESV), and 2 Corinthians 5:21 ("For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God") lean this way, and so does Romans 3:25 to 26. J. I. Packer's Tyndale lecture of 1973 begins with the claim that "Christ's death on the cross had the character of penal substitution" and that this "takes us to the very heart of the Christian gospel." What is worth noticing is that Packer himself does not set it against victory. He says it is "much more like Aulén's 'classic idea' of divine victory" than critics allow, and he argues that victory and substitution belong together.

Moral influence is Abelard's, in the twelfth century. It says that the cross is the supreme display of God's love, and that it awakens a love in us that answers it. Romans 5:8 and 1 John 4:10 lean this way. Its weakness is that on its own it asks the cross to do too little. A man who throws himself into a river to save a drowning stranger shows love, but only if the stranger was in fact drowning. Its strength is that no account of the cross is complete without it.

And the governmental view, which Hugo Grotius set out in the seventeenth century and which the Wesleyan tradition has often held, says that the cross is a public demonstration of how seriously God takes sin, which lets him forgive without cheapening his law.

I don't think these are rival answers to a single question, and I think that's why the church has never settled on one. The New Testament itself speaks of the cross in the language of the courtroom (justified, Romans 3:24), the temple (sacrifice, Hebrews 9), the slave market (redeemed, 1 Corinthians 6:20), the family (adoption, Galatians 4:5) and the battlefield (disarmed, Colossians 2:15). Each picture shows something the others cannot, and each becomes false when it claims the whole. My own argument leans on the battlefield picture. I think it is true, that it is not the only one, and that it stands on the courtroom picture rather than against it. The devil is beaten because the debt that gave him his power is gone. How that works is the next question.

## The Ledger and the Accuser

Here is the turn, and it is where the popular telling of this story goes wrong in both directions. What looked like the devil's victory was, from the other side, the act that dealt with him. Paul says so in Colossians, and he uses the language of the ledger.

> *"And you, who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our trespasses, by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross. He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."*
>
> — Colossians 2:13 to 15 (ESV)

Anyone who has bought goods on credit from a neighbourhood shop knows the exercise book behind the counter, with names down the left and amounts down the right. Now picture that book. The question is who owns it. According to Paul, the record stood against us "with its legal demands", and God is the one who sets it aside. The translators' notes on the passage say that most scholars take God as the subject of the verbs in 2:13 to 15, and that the Greek pronoun at the end can mean either "in the cross" or "in him", with the sense nearly the same either way. The debt was never the devil's to collect. What the devil had was the role of reading the entries aloud.

That is exactly what Scripture calls him. John hears a voice in heaven after the dragon is thrown down.

> *"The accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them day and night before our God."*
>
> — Revelation 12:10 (ESV)

Accusing is what he does. For an accuser to have a case he needs a record, and Colossians says the record is gone. A man who goes on reading from a book whose pages the owner has torn out can still say the words, but the words no longer settle anything.

Now a word about a popular version of this teaching, one many Nigerian readers will have heard from the pulpit. It says that Satan's dominion was legitimate, grounded in a legal transaction that Adam signed, and that the cross cancelled the contract when sin and law were dealt with together. There is a true thing in that, and an overstatement. The true thing comes from Paul. "The sting of death is sin, and the power of sin is the law" (1 Corinthians 15:56, ESV), and Ezekiel says the soul that sins shall die (Ezekiel 18:4). Death is the sentence on sin, and the law is what makes the sentence just. The devil's hold on humanity ran through our guilt, and the writer of Hebrews says he held "the power of death." So when Christ bore the sentence and the guilt was cancelled, the hold had nothing left to hold with.

The overstatement is to call the devil's dominion legitimate, as if he had a right on his own account. That is the picture Gregory of Nazianzus and Anselm argued against, and Augustine gives a warning of his own. It is easy to hear his mousetrap picture as a story in which the devil legitimately held a contract over humanity and had to be paid off or outwitted. Scripture doesn't say that. The debt was owed to God. The devil's authority, as Luke 4:6 has him say, "has been delivered to me", and borrowed authority does not give a title. He held us the way a jailer holds a prisoner on the orders of a court, and when the court reverses its sentence, the jailer has no case. The same preaching often says that "sin died with Jesus" and went into the ground and did not come back out. I understand what is meant. Paul says that "the death he died he died to sin, once for all" (Romans 6:10, ESV), and that the body of sin might be brought to nothing. But Scripture does not say that sin was buried like an object, and the New Testament is clear that believers still struggle with it. What was broken was sin's reign and its right to condemn. That's enough, and it's a good deal.

Jesus himself said where this would happen, and said it before it happened.

> *"Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself."*
>
> — John 12:31 to 32 (ESV)

Lifted up means the cross. So the cross that the devil took as his triumph is, on the lips of Jesus, the hour his rule is cast out. Look at who knew it. The Lord of glory knew, and said so in advance, to hearers who did not catch it. The rulers of this age did not know, which is the exact sense of Paul's sentence in Corinthians. If they had known, they would not have done it.

A fair objection is that all this makes God a trickster, baiting a trap for an enemy who could not see it. The answer, I think, is that God hid nothing. The serpent heard his sentence in Eden. Isaiah described the suffering servant seven hundred years earlier. Jesus told his disciples that he must go to Jerusalem and be killed, and told the crowd that the ruler of this world would be cast out. The wisdom is "secret and hidden" only to those who will not listen, and the devil's blindness was the blindness of pride, which hears everything and understands nothing. That is a different thing from a deception.

And the writer of Hebrews gives the human side of it, which matters as much as the cosmic side. The Son became flesh so that he might "deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Hebrews 2:15, ESV). The enemy had been holding people by the throat with the fear of dying. A hand on the throat is not the same as a legal claim, and the fear is what Christ went to remove.

## Why Death Could Not Keep Him

But God was not finished. The first Adam, Scripture tells us, was formed from the dust of the ground and God breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, so that he became a living creature (Genesis 2:7). He was beautiful, he bore the image of God, and he received his life from another. Paul says the second Adam is different in kind. The first Adam became a living being, and the last Adam became a spirit who gives life (1 Corinthians 15:45). How do you hold such a being in a tomb?

Jesus gave a rehearsal at the tomb of Lazarus, who had been dead long enough that his sister warned of the smell. He didn't plead with death. He said, "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25), and Lazarus walked out. Three days after his own burial, he proved it permanently. Death did not release Jesus because it had been persuaded. It released him because it had no authority over the one who had taken away the legal ground on which death operated. The resurrection is more than a miracle. It is a verdict, God's public declaration over what happened at the cross, and Paul says in the opening of Romans that Jesus was declared to be the Son of God in power by his resurrection from the dead (Romans 1:4).

When he came out of that tomb, he came out holding everything. "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to me," he told his disciples on the mountain in Galilee (Matthew 28:18, ESV). All authority, none of it shared with the powers that had claimed the earth, and none of it pending appeal. Colossians renders the moment in the language of a Roman triumph, the ancient procession in which a victorious general paraded his defeated enemies through the streets for the city to see. The Greek verb means to lead in a victory parade, and God is the one leading it. It is a public spectacle, not a quiet administrative transfer. That is why Athanasius could say that the powers had been shown up in the street like a tyrant bound hand and foot. The cross was a trophy before it was a wound, and he was drawing on Paul when he said so.

## Armed, and Still at War

Jesus didn't ascend and leave his people without recourse. When the seventy two came back delighted that even demons were subject to them, he told them what the delight meant.

> *"Behold, I have given you authority to tread on serpents and scorpions, and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing shall hurt you. Nevertheless, do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice that your names are written in heaven."*
>
> — Luke 10:19 to 20 (ESV)

Notice both halves. The authority is real, and it is stated in the widest terms, over all the power of the enemy. And then in the next breath Jesus moves the joy somewhere else, to the one thing that cannot be taken, a name in the book. Paul says the same of our position. We are "filled in him, who is the head of all rule and authority" (Colossians 2:10, ESV), complete in the one who stands above every power that exists.

Now we must be precise, because the Nigerian church has not always been. This is not the theology of the preacher who says that if you are sick or struggling it is because you lack faith, or because your seed sowing has been too small, or because you have not found the right prophet to decree your breakthrough. That is a different kind of lie, and it has hurt people who were already hurting. "Nothing shall hurt you" cannot mean that no harm will reach you, since Jesus told these same disciples that some of them would be put to death (Luke 21:16), and the book of Acts shows him keeping his word. Paul understood the authority of the believer as well as anyone, and he wrote that we are "afflicted in every way, but not crushed; perplexed, but not driven to despair; persecuted, but not forsaken; struck down, but not destroyed" (2 Corinthians 4:8 to 9, ESV). The victory is real. The war is still active. Both are true.

Theologians call this the already and the not yet. The decisive battle has been won, and the enemy's hold is broken. But the writer of Hebrews says "at present, we do not yet see everything in subjection to him" (Hebrews 2:8), and Paul says the last enemy to be destroyed is death (1 Corinthians 15:26). It is like the weeks after a war's decisive battle, when a beaten army can still do real damage and the news has not reached every outpost. John's Revelation says the believers conquer in a particular way, and the way is not triumphant.

> *"And they have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they loved not their lives even unto death."*
>
> — Revelation 12:11 (ESV)

The blood of the Lamb has done the work. The testimony of the believers carries it into the ordinary day. And the cost is not hidden. Anyone who has stood at the funeral of a Nigerian family after an attack on their village will not need convincing that the last clause is no figure of speech. The cross didn't remove the enemy's access to your Tuesday. It removed his case against you. These are very different gifts, and only one of them was promised.

What does it change, then? Mainly the question of whom you are afraid of. If the devil is a rival who is always a step ahead, every bad week is a loss in a game you may be losing, and the right response is to scramble. If he is a creature who misread the most important day in history, and whose accusations have lost their standing, you are not in his game at all. You can be sober about him. You can recognise what he is doing when the accusation arrives, and refuse to treat the old record as the last word, because the one who owns the book has cancelled it.

## The Nigerian Church at Its Prayers

Nowhere is this argument closer to home than in the way we pray. Anyone who has attended a Nigerian prayer meeting knows the vocabulary. Binding the strongman, breaking the yoke, cancelling the arrows, pulling down strongholds. In some places the language goes further, to ancestral curses, territorial spirits over a city, and altars from a grandfather's day that are said to speak against a family.

It would be lazy to wave all that away, and it would be just as lazy to bless it all. The late Ogbu Kalu, the Nigerian church historian, argued that African Pentecostalism shares with the older African worldview a belief in an unseen world that touches the material one. Burgess's study of Kalu reports his view that "Pentecostal prayer in Africa is a form of political praxis" and that Pentecostals "address the spirits that govern the structures of the public sphere" (Burgess, 2019). Kalu's point, as Burgess reads him, was that deliverance prayer has often been the language in which poor people confront the powers that crush them, and that critics who call it escapism miss that. The Nigerian theologian J. Ayodeji Adewuya argues in a 2012 article that African Pentecostal beliefs about spirits share common ground with the world Paul addresses in Ephesians 6, and in the same breath cautions against an uncritical application of Paul's teaching.

Byang Kato, the Nigerian evangelical whose *Theological Pitfalls in Africa* (1975) shaped a generation, did not treat the spirit world as a mere superstition. A paper on his work notes that he included deliverance from demons, and not only from sin, in his understanding of salvation. His warning, as Paul Bowers summarised it, was against a syncretism that treats African traditional religion as a source of authoritative revelation, legitimate worship or salvation. I think both men would recognise what I have been arguing. The reality of spiritual opposition is not in question. Where the Bible places the weight is the question.

And here the Christus Victor picture does real work. It tells us that what we have is not a technique. Some of our prayer language treats the devil as a power to be defeated afresh in each prayer meeting, as if the first victory had to be earned again on Wednesday. The Gospels give a different picture. When Jesus cast out a demon, he said that the kingdom of God had come upon the people, and compared himself to someone who binds the strong man in order to plunder his house (Matthew 12:28 to 29). Augustine, who read Revelation 20 the same way, thought the binding of Satan was something the first coming had already begun (*City of God*, Book 20, chapter 7). On that reading, the believer who says "I bind the strongman" is not starting a war. They are enforcing a result.

I also think the argument says something plain to the person who has given more hours to those who consult spirits of the dead, or to a prophet with a price list, than to the one who, according to Paul, shamed those powers in public at Calvary. There are people who know the name of Jesus as they know the name of a wealthy relative they have never met, useful in conversation but not really known. Know him as the one in whom the whole thing was decided, and ask where else you have been looking for protection.

## A Question for the Reader

Picture a woman in Ibadan, awake at one in the morning after a family quarrel that went badly, hearing the same list she has heard for ten years. You said that. You did that. You are not the sort of person God keeps. It sounds like her own conscience, and some of it may be. Some of it is a reading from a ledger, delivered in a voice with no authority to read it. She doesn't need to win the argument with the list. She needs to ask whose list it is.

If the old accuser has been reading you your own history this week, ask whose book he is reading from. Colossians says the record that stood against you was nailed to a cross and set aside. And if you have never asked the Lamb whether your name belongs in his book, you needn't wait for an answer to the grammar of Revelation 13:8. On either reading, the book is his, and so is the slaying. Talk to him tonight in plain words. The Lamb who was slain isn't surprised by you, and he hasn't been surprised by any of this from the beginning.
