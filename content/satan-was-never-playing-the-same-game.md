## The One Thing the Rulers Missed

> *"But we impart a secret and hidden wisdom of God, which God decreed before the ages for our glory. None of the rulers of this age understood this, for if they had, they would not have crucified the Lord of glory."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 2:7 to 8 (ESV)

Read that slowly, because it says something about the devil's intelligence that most of us never expect to hear. We tend to picture him as the cleverest figure in the story, always a move ahead, working in the dark while we scramble to keep up. Paul says the powers behind the cross did not understand what they were doing. Commentators argue over whether "the rulers of this age" means Pilate and the council, or the spiritual forces behind them, or both. I think both, and the argument below works whichever way you land.

Anyone who has sat through an all night programme in which the enemy was named more often than the Lord knows the assumption I'm talking about. The devil is always ahead, the believer is always catching up, and every setback is evidence of a masterstroke. It makes for lively prayer points. It also gives him a good deal more credit than Paul does.

The picture in the New Testament is odder and a good deal more comforting. A brilliant creature, real and dangerous, read the most important event in history and got it wrong. Worse for him, his reading is what made it happen. To see how, you have to look at what he could see, what had been settled before he arrived, and what he thought he was doing on the Friday.

## What the Devil Could See

I'll call him the devil here rather than Lucifer. That name comes from the Latin word for the morning star in the Vulgate's rendering of Isaiah 14:12, and the United Bible Societies handbook on Isaiah (Ogden & Sterk, 2011) reads that passage as a taunt aimed at the king of Babylon, with echoes of an older story about a figure thrown down from heaven. The church has long heard the devil's own fall behind those words, and Jesus did speak of seeing Satan fall (Luke 10:18). But the argument doesn't need Isaiah 14, and I won't hang anything on it.

What Scripture shows is a created being who rebelled and who is real. In Eden he read Eve's appetite and Adam's silence well enough to get what he wanted. In the wilderness he read a hungry man at the end of forty days and tried the same approach. That's the pattern of his intelligence: he is expert at what a creature wants, and what a creature fears, and what pride does to both.

Notice how he works in Genesis 3. His opening move is a question about what God said, and the whole conversation is about God's words and God's motives. He never has to attack God's power. He only has to plant a suspicion about God's character, and the first couple do the rest. Anyone who has watched a rumour do its work in a church or a family knows the method. It needs no miracle, only a listener who half believes it.

The wilderness is worth a closer look, because it shows both the extent of his claim and its limits.

> *"And the devil took him up and showed him all the kingdoms of the world in a moment of time, and said to him, 'To you I will give all this authority and their glory, for it has been delivered to me, and I give it to whom I will. If you, then, will worship me, it will all be yours.'"*
>
> — Luke 4:5 to 7 (ESV)

Jesus does not call it a bluff. Later he calls the devil "the ruler of this world" (John 12:31, ESV), so the title has some standing. Yet look at the grammar of the devil's boast. The authority "has been delivered to me." Delivered by whom? The passive voice is doing quiet work. The ruler of this world is a tenant with a borrowed key, and the one boasting of it also happens to be the liar. He offers all of it for a single act of worship, which tells you what he wanted more than the kingdoms.

Jesus answers with Deuteronomy, "You shall worship the Lord your God, and him only shall you serve" (Luke 4:8, ESV), and the exchange ends. It's worth noticing that the devil knows his Bible. In Matthew's version of the same wilderness scene he quotes Psalm 91 at Jesus (Matthew 4:6), and he quotes it accurately. Knowing the words was never his problem. What he lacked was the author's purpose, and a man can know every verse of a letter and still misread what the writer wants. Anyone who has watched a clever lawyer or a clever preacher lift a line out of its paragraph and make it say the opposite has seen the same trick. The devil is the oldest practitioner of it, and he is as likely to be fooled by it as anyone, because it is what he does to himself. Think about what that exchange assumes. The devil is not a rival god with a rival throne. He needs worship. He is a creature, which is why the book of Job has him asking for permission and given a limit on what he may touch (Job 1:12), and why Jesus can send him away with a sentence. A rival would have negotiated terms. A creature takes what it is allowed.

He had also heard his sentence, early. In Genesis, before Adam and Eve were out of the garden, God spoke to the serpent.

> *"I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and her offspring; he shall bruise your head, and you shall bruise his heel."*
>
> — Genesis 3:15 (ESV)

He could read the words. What he couldn't read was how a heel wound would be the thing that crushed his head. That depended on something he had no category for: a God who pays the cost of his own mercy.

## Where the Phrase Attaches in Revelation

This is where the text most often quoted on this theme has to be handled carefully, because the way English Bibles print it can mislead. Here is the verse in the English Standard Version as it reads in October 2026.

> *"And all who dwell on earth will worship it, everyone whose name has not been written from the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb who was slain."*
>
> — Revelation 13:8 (ESV)

Set that beside the New International Version, which ends the verse with "the Lamb who was slain from the creation of the world." The difference isn't in the vocabulary. In the Greek, the phrase "from the foundation of the world" sits at the very end of a long sentence, and it can hang on either of two verbs. Does it modify "written", so that the names were written from the foundation of the world? Or does it modify "slain", so that the Lamb was slain from the foundation of the world? The ESV takes the first reading and the NIV takes the second. The Christian Standard Bible, like the ESV, takes the first.

I should be honest about how this looks to the people who study it. As far as I could find, the difficulty is a matter of syntax and not of rival manuscripts. Reading Greek word order, the phrase is closest to "slain" and furthest from "written", and the older commentators were split down the middle. The English versions split too. The ESV, the New American Standard Bible and the New English Translation, all attach it to "written", while the New Living Translation goes the other way, as do the NIV, the King James and the New King James. The Pulpit Commentary, Ellicott, Jamieson, Fausset and Brown and Alford thought the plain order pointed to "slain." Bengel, Meyer, the Expositor's Greek Testament and the Geneva Bible's notes took it with "written" (these are all gathered on the Bible Hub page for the verse). The modern handbook for Bible translators by Bratcher and Hatton (1993) says most English translations connect the phrase with "written", relying in part on Revelation 17:8, and it recommends that reading. Yet it also lists the NIV, Phillips and the Revised English Bible among those who attach it to "slain", and names the commentators Sweet and Caird as supporters, pointing to 1 Peter 1:19 to 20 as a parallel. The New Bible Commentary of 1994 calls the reference "uncertain", argues that the word order does not favour "written", and keeps to the NIV (as quoted on a Logos forum thread). A 2025 article in the *Biblical Studies Journal* by A. Thirumurugan concludes that "written" is the more plausible reading on the strength of 17:8, and in its body cites G. K. Beale's commentary for the point that context can outweigh word order, though it also calls the other reading "equally valid" and suggests the ambiguity may be deliberate, "serving to emphasize both truths simultaneously".

The parallel in 17:8 is the strongest card for "written." There the ESV reads that the dwellers on earth are those "whose names have not been written in the book of life from the foundation of the world" (Revelation 17:8). Nothing is left to hang the phrase on but "written." If John used the same expression twice, he probably meant the same thing both times. The card for "slain" is the word order, and nothing in the sentence is wrong with it.

One more thing needs saying, because it affects anyone who has the ESV on a shelf or a phone. For years the ESV said "before the foundation of the world" in this verse. The Greek word is the ordinary preposition for "from." In its 2025 text edition the translation committee changed "before" to "from", one of thirty six changes touching forty two verses and sixty eight words (reported by Text and Translation, and by Protestia's summary of the announcement; the ESV site itself now prints "from"). So if your Bible or a website still says "before", it carries the older text, and the Greek reading is the plainer one. Some sites, Bible Hub among them, still show the 2016 wording.

None of this is a reason to give up the doctrine, and it is a reason not to stand on this one verse alone. Three other passages carry the weight, and none of them has the grammatical wobble.

> *"Even as he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him."*
>
> — Ephesians 1:4 (ESV)

Peter says it of Christ himself. Having told his readers they were ransomed by "the precious blood of Christ, like that of a lamb without blemish or spot," he adds that "He was foreknown before the foundation of the world but was made manifest in the last times" (1 Peter 1:19 to 20, ESV). And Paul, in the passage we began with, speaks of a wisdom "which God decreed before the ages" (1 Corinthians 2:7, ESV).

So here is what I'd say to anyone who has been told Revelation 13:8 proves it, or that it proves nothing. It doesn't prove it by itself, and it doesn't prove nothing. On either reading it puts the cross, or the book of those who belong to the Lamb, or both, before the foundation of the world. The Bible says the same thing three other ways, in verses that carry no such ambiguity. The ESV, as it now stands, is the less comforting version for anyone who wanted the neat slogan, and the more honest one.

What matters for our purposes is the plain consequence. A plan that was settled before the world began had no vacancy in it for the devil to fill, and no variable he could change. He arrived into a story that had already been told.

## Planned by God, Done by Lawless Men

A sharp reader will push back here, and the objection deserves its full strength. If the cross was decreed before the ages, the objector says, then the devil and Judas and Pilate were only doing what was written. Either they're innocent, since nobody can be blamed for following a script, or God has made himself the author of the worst crime in history. Perhaps this is a play, and the actors only think they're free.

I can't give a full map of where God's purpose ends and a creature's choice begins. Nobody can, and Peter doesn't try. He says both things in one sentence.

> *"This Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men."*
>
> — Acts 2:23 (ESV)

The plan is God's. The crucifying is theirs, and Peter charges them with it. The early church prays in the same shape when it gathers after Peter and John are released, and says that Herod and Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel gathered "to do whatever your hand and your plan had predestined to take place" (Acts 4:28, ESV). The same Gospel that says "Satan entered into Judas called Iscariot" goes on to say that Judas "went away and conferred with the chief priests and officers how he might betray him to them" (Luke 22:3 to 4, ESV). The devil moves, and the man deliberates. Both are in the text.

Think of a sting operation, since most Nigerians have a feel for how those work. Detectives who plan one to catch a thief don't become the authors of the theft. They knew it was coming, they built the room it would happen in, and the thief still walked in on his own two feet and took the money. I'd be careful with the comparison, because God didn't set a trap for something he wished would happen. But it shows how foreknowledge and guilt can sit together without either cancelling the other.

Nobody has solved the puzzle of how both can be true, and the apostles didn't feel obliged to. They preached the plan to comfort the frightened and the guilt to wake the careless, and they saw no conflict between the two sermons. I think we can live with the same tension, as we do whenever we thank God for a harvest and still plant the seed ourselves.

What it does to the devil is clear. He was never directing the cross. He was acting within it, and the part he thought was his own idea was already written into a plan that was older than he was.

## How the Devil Read Good Friday

Scripture doesn't tell us what the devil was thinking on that Friday, and I won't pretend it does. What we can say is that, by every measure he understood, the plan worked. The man who had claimed his territory, who sent him away in the wilderness and drove his servants out of the sick, was arrested at night. His friends scattered. A court found a reason, a governor found it convenient, and by mid afternoon he was dead. By evening he was buried, and a stone and a guard were set at the door.

If you read this the way a rival would, it's a clean win. In ludo, which most Nigerians have played on a cardboard board at some point, each player's pieces start in a yard, race the same track, and can be sent home by an opponent's piece landing on the same square. The game assumes equal players on one board. A player who thought the cross was that sort of contest would see the capture of the Son as the winning throw.

But the Lamb was never a piece on his board. He was the one who made the board, and he gave himself to be taken. The devil assumed a contest between rivals. What he had walked into was a different kind of event altogether, and he did not have the category for it.

There is a reason he didn't. A creature that wants to be worshipped measures glory by what it can grab and keep. A king who lets himself be arrested, mocked and nailed up is, by that measure, throwing the game, and nothing in the devil's way of reckoning allowed for a God whose glory is shown by giving himself away. The strange thing is how easily his mistake gets passed on. Scripture records that Peter, who had just confessed Jesus as the Christ, took Jesus aside and told him the cross must not happen, and that Jesus answered him as though the voice were Satan's own (Matthew 16:21 to 23). A good man who could not imagine a Messiah who suffers had picked up the devil's reading without noticing. That should make us humble. The misreading is not only in the pit. It sits in any pulpit that promises a Christ who spares us every cross.

He had also misjudged his own best weapon. Death was the devil's instrument, and the writer of Hebrews says so directly. The Son became flesh and blood "that through death he might destroy the one who has the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14, ESV). Jesus took on mortality in order to go into the very thing the devil held over everyone. The devil's strongest tool was the one the plan intended him to use.

The early church noticed this and reached for pictures. Augustine, the bishop of Hippo in North Africa, preached in the fifth century that the cross was a trap. In the Schaff edition of his sermon (as quoted online by Transfigurism) he says: "The Redeemer came and the deceiver was overcome." He goes on to say that Satan "could indeed shed that blood; but he deserved not to drink it," and that "by shedding the blood of One who was not his debtor, he was forced to release his debtors." It is a picture, and sermons are not creeds, but the picture catches something Paul states plainly in Corinthians. The powers did not see it coming.

Augustine was North African, and so was the man whose account of the cross shaped the Greek speaking church before him. Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote in his short book *On the Incarnation* that the cross had turned death on its head. Believers who once feared the grave now walked toward it, he says, without flinching, and the sight made the old enemy look like a tyrant who had been tied up and put on show in the street, mocked even by women who had once been taken in by him (Athanasius, *On the Incarnation*, section 27, in the English text hosted by the Bibliothek der Kirchenväter). That is my paraphrase, and the chapter repays reading in full. What strikes me is how little Athanasius thinks of the devil's cleverness. Two Africans, a generation or two apart, and neither of them is impressed by him.

Be careful with Augustine's picture on one point, though. It is easy to hear it as a story in which the devil legitimately held a contract over humanity and had to be paid off or outwitted. Scripture doesn't say that. It speaks of a debt, and the debt was owed to God.

## What the Cross Was Doing

Here is the turn, and it is the point at which I think the popular telling of this story goes wrong in the opposite direction. What looked like the devil's victory was, from the other side, the act that dealt with him. Paul says so in Colossians, and he uses the language of the ledger.

> *"And you, who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our trespasses, by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross. He disarmed the rulers and authorities and put them to open shame, by triumphing over them in him."*
>
> — Colossians 2:13 to 15 (ESV)

Anyone who has bought goods on credit from a neighbourhood shop knows the exercise book kept behind the counter, with names down the left and amounts down the right. Now picture that book. The question is who owns it. According to Paul, the record stood against us "with its legal demands", and God is the one who sets it aside. The debt was never the devil's to collect. What the devil had was the role of reading the entries aloud.

That reading is exactly what Scripture calls him. John hears a voice in heaven after the dragon is thrown down: "the accuser of our brothers has been thrown down, who accuses them day and night before our God" (Revelation 12:10, ESV). Accusing is what he does. For an accuser to have a case he needs a record, and Colossians says the record is gone. A man who goes on reading from a book whose pages the owner has torn out can still say the words, but the words no longer settle anything.

Jesus himself said where this would happen, and said it before it happened.

> *"Now is the judgment of this world; now will the ruler of this world be cast out. And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself."*
>
> — John 12:31 to 32 (ESV)

Lifted up is the cross. So the cross that the devil took as his triumph is, on the lips of Jesus, the hour his rule is cast out. Look at who knew it. The Lord of glory knew, and said so in advance, to the hearers who didn't catch it, and the rulers of this age did not know. That's the exact sense of Paul's sentence in Corinthians. If they had known, they would not have done it.

And then the writer of Hebrews gives the human side of it, which matters just as much as the cosmic side: that Christ might "deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong slavery" (Hebrews 2:15, ESV). The enemy had been holding people by the throat with the fear of dying. A hand on the throat is not the same as a legal claim, and the fear is what Christ went to remove.

So this is the real contrast with a rival's game. Nobody beat the devil at his own game on Friday, because the Lamb was not playing it. He was carrying out something older and larger, in which the devil's best effort played a part he would have refused if he had understood it.

## Standing Against a Beaten Enemy

None of this makes the devil harmless, and it would be a mistake to hear it that way. A beaten enemy can still do damage, as anyone who has lived through the final months of a war knows. Revelation says the victory over him is won in a particular way, and the way is not triumphant.

> *"And they have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony, for they loved not their lives even unto death."*
>
> — Revelation 12:11 (ESV)

The verse has a division of labour in it. The blood of the Lamb has done the work, and the testimony of the believers carries it into the ordinary day. And the cost isn't hidden. Anyone who has stood at the funeral of a Nigerian family after an attack on their village will not need convincing that the last clause is not a figure of speech. The victory is real, and it doesn't promise that nothing bad will happen to you.

That is why I'm wary of the kind of preaching that turns the cross into a guarantee of comfort. If sickness or poverty or a failed business were proof that you hadn't claimed your victory, the apostles would have been the biggest failures in the church. The cross did not remove the enemy's access to your Tuesday. It removed his case against you. These are very different gifts, and only one of them was promised.

What does it change, then? Mainly the question of who you're afraid of. If the devil is a rival who is always a step ahead, then every bad week is a loss in a game you may be losing, and the right response is to scramble. If he is a creature who misread the most important day in history, and whose accusations have lost their standing, then you are not in his game at all. You can be sober about him. You can recognise what he is doing when the accusation arrives, and you can refuse to treat the old record as the last word, because the one who owns the book has already cancelled it.

I'd like to end with the verse we began with, because there is a small hinge in it that doesn't depend on which way the Greek goes. The names that are not in the book belong to those who worship the beast. The book itself belongs to the Lamb. Whether the phrase "from the foundation of the world" attaches to the writing or to the slaying, the owner of the book is the same one who was slain.

Picture a woman in Ibadan, awake at one in the morning after a family quarrel that went badly, hearing the same list she has heard for ten years. You said that. You did that. You are not the sort of person God keeps. It sounds like her own conscience, and some of it may be. Some of it is a reading from a ledger, delivered in a voice with no authority to read it. She doesn't need to win the argument with the list. She needs to ask whose list it is.

So if the old accuser has been reading you your own history this week, ask whose book he's reading from. And if you have never asked the Lamb whether your name belongs in his book, you needn't wait for an answer to the grammar. Talk to him tonight, in plain words. The Lamb who was slain isn't a surprise to him, and neither are you.
