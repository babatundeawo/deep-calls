## Eight Billion Stories at Once

Picture a mother in Kano at three in the morning. Her child has a fever, the clinic wants money she won't have until Thursday, and she is praying a prayer she doesn't know how to finish. Somewhere in Ogun State an old man sits in a plastic chair outside his house while the light goes, smiling at a memory from 1987 that died with the only other person who was there. Neither of them will be on anyone's screen. Neither will make a history book.

Now add the rest. Worldometer, working from the United Nations estimates, put the world at about 8.3 billion people in early November 2026, and every one of them is living something like that right now. Not a headline, a whole inner life. The thought that formed this morning and got swallowed before it reached the mouth. The argument with yourself in the dark, when you weren't sure the ceiling was listening either. The decision made last Thursday that will bend the next twenty years.

The Bible says that none of this is hidden from God, and that he isn't skimming it. He knows it in detail, all of it at once. Most of us have heard that so often it has gone flat, like a hymn sung at so many funerals that nobody hears the words any more. Slow it down and it turns out to be very nearly impossible to believe. This essay takes the claim seriously enough to test it. It asks what the Bible means by God's knowing, why the size of the sky seems to count against it, and what the Christian answer to that objection is. The answer turns out to be a person.

## What Psalm 139 Says God Knows

> *"O LORD, you have searched me and known me! You know when I sit down and when I rise up; you discern my thoughts from afar. You search out my path and my lying down and are acquainted with all my ways. Even before a word is on my tongue, behold, O LORD, you know it altogether."*
>
> — Psalm 139:1 to 4 (ESV)

David wrote that in the first person, and we read it that way, as one man and his God. Fair enough. But nothing in the psalm says he is an exception. A shepherd who became a king is describing what it is to be a person standing in front of the One who made him, and if it is true of David it is true of the mother in Kano.

Look at the verbs. The one the ESV renders "search out" in verse 3 belongs to the farm. The lexicons give its sense as to toss about, to winnow, which is what you do with a handful of grain on a windy day, throwing it up to see what is wheat and what is chaff. Translators fuss over how to put it in English (the old King James has "compassest"), and I wouldn't build a doctrine on one verb. But the picture fits the rest of the passage. "Acquainted" is the word for knowing something because you have handled it for years. God is not watching your life from a respectable distance. He has had his hands in it.

The psalm does not stop at thoughts and words. Later it asks where a person could go to get away from this presence, and answers that heaven, the grave, the far side of the sea and the dark itself all fail, because the darkness is not dark to him. It then says that this knowing began before birth, when David was put together in his mother's womb and the days of his life were written in God's book before one of them came to be. So the knowledge reaches backwards to the beginning of a life as well as inwards to its thoughts.

Hold that against 8.3 billion and see what happens. I can't. Nobody can. That is the first honest thing to say about divine knowledge. It isn't ours made bigger.

And it can sound frightening. Anyone who has lived beside a neighbour who knows everybody's business will understand why a God who knows everything strikes some people as surveillance and not comfort. David feels that too. He asks where he could run. Hold onto the fact that he doesn't leave it there.

## Knowing a Person Is Not Holding Data

There is a difference between a God who has a complete file on you and a God who knows you. Most of us carry the first picture around without noticing. We imagine omniscience as an immense database with a very fast search function, which is why the number 8.3 billion feels like a strain on its capacity.

Scripture talks about knowing in a different register. When it says that Adam "knew" Eve it is speaking of the closest relationship a person can have. When God says to Israel through Amos that of all the families of the earth "you only have I known" (Amos 3:2, ESV), he is not saying that he has no information about the other nations. He is saying that he has chosen this one. When Jesus says in John 10 that the good shepherd knows his sheep and they know him, he does not mean he can recite their number. And in Matthew 7:23 the terrible sentence "I never knew you" cannot mean that the judge lacked the facts, since the people in front of him have just listed their works. In each case knowing is a relationship held in the open, with attention and care in it.

So when Psalm 139 says that God knows my sitting down and my rising up, the claim is of the second kind. A database holds a record of my posture. A person has been attending to me, and has known me the way a parent knows the sound of a particular child coming up the path. If that is what divine knowledge is, then the problem of scale looks different. A database strains under 8.3 billion entries. A person who is infinite does not strain, because attention is not the sort of thing that has to be rationed across his creatures.

## The Stranger at the Fuel Queue

> *"And he made from one man every nation of mankind to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their dwelling place."*
>
> — Acts 17:26 (ESV)

Paul says this in Athens to a room of philosophers who prided themselves on knowing how the world worked. The word translated "boundaries" is the language of surveyors and border markers. The when and the where of a human life, the family, the town, the decade, were set by someone who knew what he was doing. You did not arrive by accident of delivery.

If that is so, the points where lives cross are not accidents either. Anyone who has spent two hours in a fuel queue in Nigeria has shared a stretch of road with people whose names they'll never learn. One of them may be a hinge in somebody's story, perhaps yours. I can't tell you which. Scripture doesn't promise the particulars. It says no meeting happens outside his sight.

Paul goes further, and I think he meant it plainly. "In him we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:28, ESV). The space where those billions of stories unfold isn't neutral ground with God watching from outside. It rests on him, and Colossians says as much, that "in him all things hold together" (Colossians 1:17, ESV).

African thought has long insisted that nobody's story is private. John Mbiti, the Kenyan scholar whose *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969) shaped a generation of African theology, is remembered for the formula that "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am" (as quoted in Njoroge, n.d.). Whatever one makes of the rest of his work, he saw something the individualist West tends to miss, that a life is a thread in other lives. The Bible agrees, and then adds the part that Mbiti's formula can't supply by itself. The whole weave is held, and the one who holds it knows each thread.

## Why Nobody Is a Background Character

There is a particular loneliness in believing your story is small. Every child who grew up in a small town and watched Lagos on someone else's television knows it. The real events seem to be happening up there, on stages and screens, and you are down here in the margin of somebody else's plot.

The Bible keeps ruining that assumption, and one example will do.

> *"Now the Syrians on one of their raids had carried off a little girl from the land of Israel, and she worked in the service of Naaman's wife. She said to her mistress, 'Would that my lord were with the prophet who is in Samaria! He would cure him of his leprosy.'"*
>
> — 2 Kings 5:2 to 3 (ESV)

She has no name. She was taken in a raid and put to work in the house of the enemy's commander. Her whole recorded speech is nineteen words. And a powerful man travelled to another country because of them. Nobody in that house would have called her the main character, and the writer did not bother to give her a name. Yet the story turns on her.

Jesus says something similar about creatures lower down the ladder than a slave girl. Luke reports it this way.

> *"Are not five sparrows sold for two pennies? And not one of them is forgotten before God. Why, even the hairs of your head are all numbered. Fear not; you are of more value than many sparrows."*
>
> — Luke 12:6 to 7 (ESV)

Matthew has the same saying in a slightly different form, with two sparrows sold for a penny, and adds that not one of them falls to the ground apart from your Father (Matthew 10:29 to 31). Two things about the sayings. The verb for "numbered" is in the perfect tense in the Greek, which means the counting is already done, finished and on file. And the sparrows still fall. Jesus says this to disciples he has just warned will be dragged before courts, so the verse is not a promise that nothing bad happens. I read it as a promise that nothing bad happens unseen, and that the one it happens to is never alone in it. None of that explains the fever in Kano. It does mean the mother is not a footnote.

Notice, too, the arithmetic of the price. Five sparrows for two pennies means that the fifth bird was thrown in free, the item nobody bothered to count in the sale. Luke has Jesus say that even that one is not forgotten before God. The bird that doesn't make the invoice does not escape the notice that matters.

## The Objection from the Size of the Sky

There is a serious argument against all of this, and a clever one. Put as well as a sceptic would put it, it runs like this. We now know the observable universe is about 93 billion light years across. The Milky Way alone holds somewhere between 100 and 400 billion stars, and astronomers still argue over how many galaxies there are. In 2016 a team led by Christopher Conselice put the figure around two trillion. In 2021 a study using New Horizons, the probe that flew past Pluto, measured the faint glow of the whole sky and found far too little light for that many, closer to the hundreds of billions. Either figure is past imagining. Against that, says the sceptic, the idea that whoever made it keeps count of the hairs on one head in Kano is vanity, a small species flattering itself.

I want to give the objection its due before answering it. Nobody who has stood outside in a village with no electric lights, and seen how many stars there actually are, will find it silly. A great many people lose their faith in that kind of moment, and a great many others find it there. The Bible knows this feeling and doesn't wait for the telescope to feel it. The opening verse of this essay is David's version of the objection, asked with the naked eye. What is man?

The reply starts with a concession. The scale is real, and Scripture does not shrink it. Isaiah, who had no photographs of anything, asks who has measured the heavens.

> *"Who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand and marked off the heavens with a span, enclosed the dust of the earth in a measure and weighed the mountains in scales and the hills in a balance?"*
>
> — Isaiah 40:12 (ESV)

A span is the stretch of an open hand, roughly nine inches. Whatever the true number of galaxies, the prophet's answer is that the heavens are handled the way a tailor handles cloth. Job gets the same treatment when God answers him out of the storm. "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding" (Job 38:4, ESV). God goes on to ask whether Job has entered the storehouses of the snow. Earlier Job himself had conceded that these things are "but the outskirts of his ways" and that "how small a whisper do we hear of him" (Job 26:14, ESV). The Psalms add that God counts the stars and gives all of them names (Psalm 147:4), and a God who has names for the stars has no difficulty with the name of one woman.

The second part of the reply is about a hidden assumption. The argument from size assumes that God's attention works like ours, as a limited supply that has to be rationed, so that more stars must mean less for any one person. That is how finite creatures work. A teacher with sixty pupils in a Nigerian classroom really does have less for each one. But nothing in the argument shows that infinite knowledge runs on a budget, and everything in Scripture says it doesn't.

The third part of the reply is that the sceptic's own reasoning turns against him. If size were the measure of worth, then a galaxy would matter more than a physicist. Yet it is the physicist who knows about the galaxy, and the whole argument depends on a small creature on a small planet being able to weigh the sky. The sceptic has to treat the human mind as important enough to count the stars while arguing that a human being is too small to matter. I do not say this to score a point. I say it because the question David asked, what is man, is genuinely a question that science makes sharper without answering.

Blaise Pascal, the French mathematician, thought about this in notes he left unfinished when he died in 1662. He described the human being as "A Nothing in comparison with the Infinite, an All in comparison with the Nothing, a mean between nothing and everything" (Pascal, Pensées, in the Trotter edition). He was not calculating galaxies. He was holding two truths and refusing to drop either one.

## What Divine Knowledge Does Not Mean

Two misunderstandings need clearing away before going on, because each of them turns this doctrine into something it isn't. The first is that a God who knows everything must be the cause of everything, so that the hairs on your head were numbered the way a prisoner's days are numbered. Christian thinkers have long distinguished knowing from causing. Whether God's knowledge of what people will freely do is the same as his determining it is a question on which Reformed, Arminian and other believers differ, and nothing in this essay depends on settling it. What the passages above require is that nothing is hidden from him and that the people he knows are not abandoned. They do not say that every event is a message with your name on it.

The second misunderstanding is the opposite one, that a God who sees everything must approve of everything. Psalm 139 itself turns, a few verses after the passage we have read, to David's hatred of the wicked and to his prayer to be led away from any grievous way in himself. To be known by God is to be seen as you are, including the parts you hide from your pastor and your spouse. That ought to be alarming, and it is, but it is not the alarm of a person who is about to be thrown out. The same attention that sees the failure also sees what the failure is made of, and what it could become. A mother who sees her son lying about where he was last night has not stopped being his mother.

## The Author Who Walked In

Telling the sceptic that God is bigger than his question gives him a good sentence and no reason to change his mind. Here is where I think he goes wrong. He is right about the scale, and wrong about what we should expect God to do with it. If the God of Isaiah 40 simply watched, if he sat at the top of eight billion stories and kept perfect records, the sceptic's picture of a distant, magnificent, indifferent power would be close enough to the truth. The Christian claim is stranger than that.

> *"And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we have seen his glory, glory as of the only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth."*
>
> — John 1:14 (ESV)

Authors don't usually appear in their own books. This one did. He took a body in a particular year, in an occupied province at the edge of an empire, and grew up in a village so slight that a man who would later follow him could ask, "Can anything good come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46, ESV). He spent about thirty years as the kind of person nobody would put on a poster. He sat tired beside a well. He cried at a friend's grave. He knew what it was to have a story that looks, from the inside and from the outside, impossibly small.

Think about what that does to the argument from scale. If God wanted to know what a human life is like from the inside, he did not need to, since Psalm 139 says he already knows. What the incarnation adds is that he chose to live one of them. He took one life out of the billions and lived it all the way through, with the hunger and the tiredness, the dust of the road and the grief at a graveside. The letter to the Hebrews draws the conclusion that we have a high priest who can sympathise with our weaknesses, since he has been tempted as we are (Hebrews 4:15). Knowing every life from above is one thing. Having been inside one is another. The God who can count the hairs on a head also knows what it is to have a head that aches.

There is something worth noting about the one life he picked. It was not the life of an emperor, whose story everyone would have been watching anyway. It was a carpenter's life in a poor province under an occupying army, which is closer to the life of most people who have ever lived. If God wanted to show that the small story matters, this was how to show it.

Then he was put to death on a cross outside Jerusalem, and the church has always said that what he carried there was the weight of everything that had gone wrong in those billions of stories: the secret that turned to shame, the choice that knocked a life off course, the wound someone else inflicted. A love that sees every story and does nothing is not a love the New Testament recognises. The one who counted the hairs came to the head they grew on, and was crowned with thorns.

Notice what this does and doesn't answer. It doesn't tell the mother in Kano why her child has a fever. It does mean that the one who sees the fever has himself stood at a graveside and has been poor in an occupied country. When she prays the prayer she can't finish, she is not addressing a power that has only ever watched.

That changes what the size of the sky means. The sceptic read the stars as proof that we are too small to matter. The gospel reads the stars as proof that whoever came for us did not have to. It was never a matter of whether the numbers allowed it. It was a matter of whether he would.

## What to Do With Being Known

The hymn writers have been here before us. In 1934, as the story goes in the account Wycliffe Canada tells, an English missionary named Stuart Hine was crossing the Carpathian Mountains, handing out Bibles, when a storm rolled across the peaks. He had learned a Russian version of a Swedish poem that Carl Boberg wrote in 1885, and he set about writing English words for it. The thunder shaped the first verse. For fourteen years that version had only three verses, and the third turns away from the thunder and toward the cross. Hine was not doing systematic theology. He was doing what good theology is supposed to lead to, which is wonder with a destination. The psalm that began this essay ends the same way in its own last line, with David's cry that the name of the Lord is majestic in all the earth (Psalm 8:9).

I would like to end with David again, because Psalm 139 doesn't stop where we left it. He has spent twenty two verses overwhelmed by being known, and then he does something I find remarkable. He opens the door himself.

> *"Search me, O God, and know my heart! Try me and know my thoughts! And see if there be any grievous way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting!"*
>
> — Psalm 139:23 to 24 (ESV)

He is asking for what has already happened. God did not need the invitation, and David knew it. Yet the asking turns knowledge that might feel like exposure into something closer to company.

So if you've been carrying a chapter you're ashamed of, or a Tuesday that quietly changed everything, or a prayer you stopped praying because you had decided nobody was on the other end, try it tonight. Say the prayer, and say it badly if you must. The mother in Kano didn't know how to finish hers either. You will not be telling him anything he hasn't heard. Even before a word is on your tongue, he knows it altogether, and that is the best reason I know to start talking.
