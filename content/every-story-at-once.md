## The Infinite Cinema Nobody Has Noticed

Somewhere above the noise of Lagos traffic, above the smog of cities and the silence of villages and the particular hush of a hospital corridor at 3am, there is a view that no human eye has ever fully taken in. Not the view from a satellite, which can show you rooftops and coastlines and the curvature of the earth. Something further. Something that does not merely see where you are but *who* you are — every thought, every wound, every private argument you have had with yourself in the dark when nobody was listening and you were not entirely sure the ceiling was either.

Imagine, for a moment, that you could see what God sees.

Not history. Not nations. Not the broad sweep of civilisations rising and collapsing like tides. Zoom past all of that and go straight to the granular, the irreducibly personal: eight billion individual stories unfolding simultaneously. The mother in Kano who has not slept in three days because her child has a fever and there is no money for the clinic and she is praying a prayer she does not know how to finish. The teenager in São Paulo who made a decision last Thursday that he has told no one about and that will alter the next twenty years of his life in ways he cannot yet calculate. The old man in a village in Ogun State sitting in a plastic chair outside his house, watching the sun go down, thinking about something that happened in 1987, smiling to himself for a reason that died with the only person who would have understood it.

Every single one of them, fully known. In full detail, in real time, without the image compressing or the data dropping or the signal losing fidelity.

That is not a figure of speech. That is the explicit claim the Bible makes about God, and it is worth sitting with the full weight of it before you move on because most of us have heard it so many times we have forgotten how impossible it actually is.

## The Passage Everyone Quotes and Almost Nobody Finishes Reading

> *"You have searched me, Lord, and you know me. You know when I sit and when I rise; you perceive my thoughts from afar. You discern my going out and my lying down; you are familiar with all my ways. Before a word is on my tongue you, Lord, know it completely."*
> — Psalm 139:1–4 (NIV)

David wrote this Psalm in the first person, and we tend to read it that way as a personal, intimate statement about the relationship between one man and his God. Which it is. But David is not special in this regard. He is representative. The Psalm is not describing an exceptional experience reserved for kings and prophets. It is describing the baseline condition of every human being who has ever drawn breath.

Every person who has ever lived has been searched and known in this manner. Every sitting and rising, every thought forming three seconds before the mouth opens, every word that was almost said and then swallowed back down, every decision made in the half-second before anyone was watching — all of it, perceived. All of it, familiar to God.

Not filed away in some divine database. *Familiar.* There is intimacy in that word that the English translation can almost but not quite hold. The Hebrew root behind "you are familiar with all my ways" reaches toward the idea of winnowing — of a farmer turning grain over in his hands, examining every piece of it, knowing it the way only someone who has worked the field all season can know a harvest. God does not observe your life from a respectable distance. He turns it over. He has examined every grain of it.

Now multiply that intimacy by eight billion, and hold it there without flinching.

You cannot. That is rather the point.

## The Map Nobody Has

The other thing God sees — and this is where the vertigo truly begins — is the map of how those eight billion stories connect.

You passed someone on the street last week. Two seconds of shared space. You moved on. They moved on. Neither of you thought about the other again. But what if that person whose name you never learned, whose face you have already forgotten — is carrying a storyline that is about to reshape the lives of a hundred people you have never met? What if the stranger you almost bumped into at the fuel queue was a link in a chain that, four years from now, changes your life in a way you will spend the rest of that life trying to explain?

What if the most consequential encounter of your decade happened on a Tuesday you did not think to mark?

This is not mysticism. This is the testimony of Scripture.

> *"From one man he made all the nations, that they should inhabit the whole earth; and he marked out their appointed times in history and the boundaries of their lands. God did this so that they would seek him and perhaps reach out for him and find him, though he is not far from any one of us. For in him we live and move and have our being."*
> — Acts 17:26–28 (NIV)

Paul, standing on Mars Hill in Athens, tells a room full of Greek philosophers something that should have stopped every one of them cold: the God who made everything has also arranged the specific coordinates of every human life. When. Where. In what family. In what century. He marked it out — the Greek is *horothesías*, boundary-setting, border-marking, the deliberate act of someone with a plan who knows exactly what they are doing. Not randomly. Not arbitrarily. Sovereignly.

And then, as though the first claim were not already breathtaking enough, Paul adds: *in him we live and move and have our being.* Not near him. Not beneath him. *In* him. As though the space in which all those eight billion stories are unfolding is not neutral territory but the very fabric of God himself. As Colossians puts it without ceremony: *"in him all things hold together"* (Colossians 1:17). The universe is not running on its own momentum. It is being held together, right now, by the same One who knows the word forming on your tongue before you form it.

The map of human interconnectedness is not a mystery to God. It is his architecture. Every storyline that crosses another storyline does so within his sight, inside his sovereignty, as part of a narrative whose ending he has already written.

## Nobody Is a Background Character

There is a particular loneliness — and if you have ever felt it, you know exactly what I am describing without needing me to labour the point — of believing that your story is small. That you are one of the billions rather than one of the significant ones. That history is happening somewhere else, to someone else, and you are simply living in the margins of it.

Every small town in Nigeria knows this feeling. Every child who grew up in a village and watched the city lights from a distance. Every person who looked at the people on screens and stages and thought, somewhere below the level of conscious articulation, that the real narrative was happening up there, and they were down here.

West African communal wisdom has always recognised what the text confirms: no one's story is self-contained. You are someone else's plot point. You are someone else's turning point. You are the answer to a prayer someone prayed for years before you ever met them, and you may never know it this side of eternity. The background character in your own story is the main character in someone else's entirely.

Jesus understood this, which is why he said what he said:

> *"Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground outside your Father's care. And even the very hairs of your head are all numbered. So don't be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows."*
> — Matthew 10:29–31 (NIV)

The hairs of your head are numbered. Not counted once and archived. *Numbered* — an ongoing, continuous accounting. The kind of attention a goldsmith gives to gold, not the kind a bureaucrat gives to a census. If that order of attention is extended to the sparrow, what does it say about the attention extended to the one made in God's own image?

Your story is not small. You simply cannot see its own size from the inside.

## If This Is Only the Beginning

Now step back further much further.

If this earth alone holds eight billion stories, each one infinitely complex, each one known in full by God, and if the observable universe contains an estimated two trillion galaxies, and if each of those galaxies holds hundreds of billions of stars, and if even this staggering arithmetic is as the Book of Job quietly suggests merely the outer fringe of what God has made, then what does that do to your picture of him?

Isaiah was not writing in the age of the James Webb telescope. He had no photographs of nebulae, no data on the distance between galaxies, no concept of a universe 93 billion light-years in observable diameter. And yet someone breathed these words through him with perfect sufficiency:

> *"Who has measured the waters in the hollow of his hand, or with the breadth of his hand marked off the heavens? Who has held the dust of the earth in a basket, or weighed the mountains on the scales and the hills in a balance?"*
> — Isaiah 40:12 (NIV)

The rhetorical questions pile up in that chapter like waves, each one larger than the last, each one designed to do one thing: reduce the listener to silence. Not the silence of defeat. The silence of genuine awe the kind that comes when language reaches its outer edge and can go no further.

When God answers Job from the whirlwind in chapter 38, he does not explain himself. He does not offer a theological treatise on suffering or a careful account of divine providence. He simply asks questions. *Where were you when I laid the earth's foundation? Have you comprehended the expanse of the earth? Have you entered the storehouses of the snow?* (Job 38:4, 18, 22). The questions stack on top of each other like geological strata, and by the time God is finished, Job has nothing left to say not because he has been silenced by a superior argument, but because he has glimpsed something so far beyond the scale of his previous thinking that argument itself becomes inadequate.

Blaise Pascal, the French mathematician and philosopher, wrote in the seventeenth century about two infinities the infinitely large and the infinitely small and the human being suspended between them, too vast for the atom and too small for the cosmos. He called it the condition of man: a Nothing compared with the Infinite, an All compared with the Nothing. He was not yet calculating the observable universe. He was simply thinking carefully about the God he already knew. And he arrived, as every honest thinker eventually does, at wonder.

The One who holds that universe — who measures its expanse in the breadth of his hand, who calls every star by name (Psalm 147:4) and not one of them is missing — is the same One counting the hairs on your head. The same One reading the prayer you have not yet found the words to pray. The cosmic and the intimate are not two different activities for God. They are the same breath.

## The Most Staggering Plot Twist in the Saga

Here is where the story turns.

We have been imagining a God who watches. Who sees. Who knows. An infinite, sustaining Observer of infinite stories everywhere at once, missing nothing, holding all of it together from the incomprehensible vantage point of eternity. And if that were the whole story, it would be more than enough to stop you mid-sentence and send you to your knees. A God of that scale, who nevertheless bothers to count your hairs and mark the sparrow's fall, would already be more than any theology can fully contain.

But that is not the whole story.

The whole story is this: the Author entered the narrative.

> *"The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us. We have seen his glory, the glory of the one and only Son, who came from the Father, full of grace and truth."*
> — John 1:14 (NIV)

In the most staggering plot twist in the history of creation, the One who was watching from eternity stepped into the cinema. He became one of the stories. He took on a particular body, in a particular time, in a particular place — not Rome, not Athens, not the centre of any empire, but a margin province of an occupied people, born in borrowed shelter, raised in a village so unremarkable that one of his own future disciples asked whether anything good could come out of it (John 1:46).

He did not enter as a spectator experiencing the narrative from a safe distance. He entered as a fully present participant — hungry when there was no bread, tired when the road was long, moved to tears when a friend died, sweating drops of blood when the weight of what was coming pressed down on him in a garden at night. He knew what it was to have a story that looked, from the inside, impossibly small. He knew what it was to be the background character in the version of events everyone around him was watching.

And then, on a cross outside Jerusalem, he took the weight of every story that had ever gone wrong. Every secret that had ever curdled into shame. Every choice that had ever knocked a life irreversibly off course. Every wound that had ever been opened by another person's cruelty or one's own. He bore all of it. Not because an abstract justice required a transaction to be completed, but because the love that sees every story in full is not the kind of love that watches a story break and does nothing.

We did not find the gospel. The gospel found us.

The God who binge-watches eternity did not stay in his seat. He came down.

## What to Do With a View Like That

There is a response that this kind of knowledge is supposed to produce, and it is not smugness. It is not the self-satisfied calm of someone who has ticked the box marked "theology of divine omniscience" and moved on. It is not even, primarily, intellectual certainty, though intellectual certainty is part of it.

The response is worship. Not worship as a Sunday activity you perform when the music is good. Worship as reorientation — the ongoing act of putting yourself correctly in relation to something whose size you have finally, partially, glimpsed.

David landed here after the same journey. After staring at the heavens and asking who on earth he was in relation to all of it, he did not spiral into existential insignificance. He landed, somehow, on wonder at being known:

> *"Lord, our Lord, how majestic is your name in all the earth!"*
> — Psalm 8:9 (NIV)

Not a theological proposition to be filed correctly. A declaration that has been earned by looking at the vastness, and then at the intimacy, and then at the cross, and arriving at the only word large enough to hold all three.

Stuart K. Hine, a British missionary, translated the Swedish hymn *How Great Thou Art* into English after walking through the Carpathian mountains during a thunderstorm in the 1920s. He heard the thunder, felt the scale of creation wrap around him from every direction, and wrote what any honest person would write in that moment: *O Lord my God, when I in awesome wonder, consider all the worlds Thy hands have made.* He was not doing systematic theology. He was doing what systematic theology is, at its best, supposed to produce.

You are living inside the greatest story ever told — a story told simultaneously across billions of lives and billions of light-years, a story in which every person is known by name, every sorrow is witnessed, every moment is held in the consciousness of One for whom time itself is not a constraint. And that same One — at the hinge of history, on a hill outside a city wall — decided that watching was not enough.

He came for your story specifically. For the chapter you are ashamed of. For the Tuesday that silently changed everything and the years that followed when nothing felt like it was being redeemed. For the prayer you stopped praying because you had stopped believing anyone was listening.

Someone was listening. Someone has always been listening.

The view from eternity is not indifferent. It is not clinical. It is not the detached observation of an experimenter who set the universe running and stepped back to take notes.

It is love. Inconceivable in scale. Particular in focus.

Oh Lord our God how great thou art.
