## A Sunday Morning That Looks Like Business

Anyone who has driven past a big church in Lagos or Abuja or Port Harcourt at ten o'clock on a Sunday knows the scene. Marshals in matching vests wave the cars into rows. A guard checks the gate. The windows are tinted, a generator hums somewhere behind the hall, and the signboard carries a logo a branding agency would be proud of. Inside, the air conditioning works, which is more than can be said for the clinic down the road. Somebody has thought hard about your experience, and about whether you'll be back next week.

None of that is sinful on its face. Visitors should be welcomed, car parks need managing, and a sound system that cuts out in the middle of a prayer helps nobody. My complaint begins further in, with a change of assumption that nobody announced and a great many joined. In too many places the marketplace stopped being a tool the church used and became the picture of what a church is. The congregation turned into a clientele. And the man in front became something between a shepherd and a chief executive, with a special line in promises about heaven.

We didn't fall into this. We walked in, one reasonable decision at a time. Which means we can walk out the same way.

## What Jesus Found in the Temple Courts

Selling sacred things is older than Nigeria, and older than television. On Matthew's account, Jesus walked into the temple in Jerusalem in the last week before His death and drove out those who were buying and selling there. Mark and Luke tell it in the same week. John places a cleansing at the start of His ministry instead, and scholars still argue about whether there were two such days or one placed differently by John for his own purposes. I'm following Matthew here, and the argument doesn't depend on settling it.

Be fair to the traders for a moment. The standard picture in the commentaries is that a pilgrim who had travelled a long way needed an animal fit for sacrifice and the right coins for the annual temple tax, and somebody had to supply both. The service was real. The scholar Phillip Long (2014), summarising the debate, notes that the tax was paid in Tyrian coins, which is why money changers were needed at all. He also notes that D. A. Carson finds no evidence that the sellers or the changers were cheating anybody, so the popular sermon about extortionate rates is on thinner ground than preachers admit. Jerome Murphy O'Connor, he adds, has pointed out something awkward for another popular claim. The Tyrian coin carried the image of the god Melkart, so it wasn't chosen to avoid idols.

What, then, was wrong? Several readings are on offer and I think they overlap. Andreas Köstenberger proposes that the trade took over the outer court, the one part of the temple where people who were not Jews could come near to pray. That fits Mark, who records Jesus quoting Isaiah that the house should be called a house of prayer "for all the nations", and who adds that He would not allow anyone to carry goods through the temple. Commerce had taken the room that belonged to the people God most wanted in. Barry Smith, in Long's summary, puts the stress on distraction: noise and haggling where there should have been prayer. And Long himself reads the whole thing as a prophetic protest in the line of Jeremiah.

Jeremiah matters because Jesus borrowed his words.

> *"It is written, 'My house shall be called a house of prayer,' but you make it a den of robbers."*
>
> — Matthew 21:13 (ESV)

Notice the phrase He chose. A den is where robbers retreat to feel safe, not where they do the robbing. In Jeremiah 7 the prophet stands at the temple gate and tells the worshippers that God will let them stay in the land only if they truly amend their ways and do not oppress the sojourner, the fatherless or the widow. They went on stealing and swearing falsely and then came and stood in the house and said, "We are delivered!" Hence the question God puts to them.

> *"Has this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?"*
>
> — Jeremiah 7:11 (ESV)

So the charge was never only about prices. It was about a house of worship being used as cover. That is the reading that should trouble a church with a spotless financial report and a hidden contempt for its poorer members. John records words that fit a Nigerian Sunday uncomfortably well, said to the men who sold doves.

> *"Get these out of here! Stop turning my Father's house into a market!"*
>
> — John 2:16 (NIV)

John adds that His disciples remembered a line from the Psalms about zeal for God's house consuming Him. He didn't set up a review committee, and He didn't send a carefully worded letter. He overturned tables. I'm not recommending that as a method of church reform. I'm pointing out how seriously the gentlest person in the Gospels took the matter. A management inefficiency doesn't bring a man to that. A desecration does.

## The Man Who Tried to Buy the Spirit

Acts 8 gives a shorter scene with the same smell. Simon had been the celebrated sorcerer of Samaria. Luke says he believed Philip's message and was baptised. Then Peter and John arrived from Jerusalem, the Samaritans received the Holy Spirit as the apostles laid hands on them, and Simon saw a business opportunity. He offered money and asked for "this ability so that everyone on whom I lay my hands may receive the Holy Spirit" (Acts 8:19, NIV).

> *"May your money perish with you, because you thought you could buy the gift of God with money!"*
>
> — Acts 8:20 (NIV)

Peter didn't negotiate. He went on to tell Simon that he had no part in this ministry because his heart wasn't right before God, that he should repent and pray for forgiveness, and that he was full of bitterness and captive to sin. Simon, to his credit, asked the apostles to pray for him. Whether his faith was ever real, Luke leaves open. What the text does show is a man who had spent his life selling spiritual power, who watched the Spirit arrive, and whose first thought was a price. The old church word for buying and selling church office, simony, takes its name from him.

Peter had taught the opposite in practice earlier in the same book. At the temple gate he told a lame man he had no silver or gold, and gave him what he did have. The apostles' power had no price list because it wasn't theirs to price. Paul later reminded the elders of Ephesus that he had coveted nobody's silver or gold, and that his own hands had supplied his needs and those of his companions. He didn't think this was heroic. He thought it was what the work required, so that nobody could say the gospel had a fee.

Simon's error didn't die in the first century. Any ministry that treats healing, breakthrough or the Spirit's presence as stock to be released by the right offering is standing in Simon's shoes, whatever its brochure says.

## How One Model Sells to Two Crowds

The model adjusts itself to whoever is sitting in front of it. I'm describing a pattern here, and I'm not pointing at any pastor, because plenty of people who preach this way believe every word of it.

Picture an assembly where most of the people are carrying unemployment, debt and sickness. The message on offer there is often about curses and spiritual debts that must be cleared before anything can move. A great grandmother's visit to a traditional diviner, three generations back, is said to have opened a door in the bloodline, and a prophetic encounter (with a seed attached, naturally) is the way to shut it. The congregation arrives with little and goes home with less, holding a promise that something has been broken. Fear is the product, and the sale is made when somebody is afraid enough to pay.

Now picture the other room, where the cars in the car park suggest that breakthrough has already arrived. The menu changes. There are worship artists, motivational speakers with a verse on the slide, business summits and networking breakfasts dressed as discipleship. The seed is still sown, but between a testimony from a successful entrepreneur and a well produced video. Here the product is aspiration, and the sale is made when somebody wants more than they already have.

One version takes its money through fear and the other through ambition. Neither is selling what the gospel was given to deliver. And when somebody in either room asks where the money goes, some platforms have a ready answer, which is that the questioner is rebelling, and that "rebellion is as the sin of witchcraft" (1 Samuel 15:23, KJV). Samuel said that to King Saul, who had set aside the word of the Lord. It was a rebuke to a king and was never meant as a shield for a platform. As a piece of machinery it's efficient all the same. It silences the critic, frightens the room and confirms the authority of the person asking not to be questioned, all in one sentence.

Ezekiel had no platform to protect, which may be why this passage appears so seldom on the projection screens of large churches.

> *"Woe to you shepherds of Israel who only take care of yourselves! Should not shepherds take care of the flock? You eat the curds, clothe yourselves with the wool and slaughter the choice animals, but you do not take care of the flock. You have not strengthened the weak or healed the sick or bound up the injured. You have not brought back the strays or searched for the lost. You have ruled them harshly and brutally."*
>
> — Ezekiel 34:2 to 4 (NIV)

Some of us read that as an indictment. A few corners of the church seem to have filed it as a business plan.

## The Best Case for Prosperity Teaching

I owe the other side a fair hearing, so here is the case as a thoughtful prosperity preacher would put it, in words he would accept.

God is a good Father and not a stingy one. Poverty is not a virtue and sickness is not a spiritual qualification. The Bible promises blessing again and again, and Jesus said He came that people might have life and have it abundantly. A gospel that tells a hungry man to suffer beautifully sounds like a cruel joke, especially in a country where this is not an abstract matter. The World Bank's Nigeria Development Update of April 2026 put the national poverty rate at 63 per cent in 2025, up from 61 per cent the year before, which means about 140 million people living below the national poverty line (TechEconomy, 2026). Faith, on this account, is the thing that lifts a person's eyes from that arithmetic. Giving a seed is an act of trust in a God who multiplies what is put in His hands.

That case isn't invented in a boardroom, and the people who believe it aren't fools. A Pew survey reported in 2010 found that in most of the countries it covered more than half of Christians believed God would grant wealth and good health to those with enough faith (Pew Research Center, 2010). *Christianity Today*, reporting on an earlier Pew study of Pentecostals and charismatics, said that 95 per cent of Nigerian Pentecostals agreed (*Christianity Today*, 2007). The same report quotes the historian Vinson Synan saying that for most Africans prosperity means "a roof over their heads and clothes," and records Allan Anderson's warning that only the uninformed brand every prosperity leader a greedy manipulator. I'd add my own observation. In a place where the clinic is far, the school fees are due and the government has not shown up, a church that says "God cares about your rent" is meeting a real need, and it would be strange to expect poor people to prefer a theology that ignored it.

Scripture agrees with a good deal of this. The law of Moses expected there to be no poor among a faithful people and told Israel to open its hand to the needy (Deuteronomy 15). The early church in Jerusalem made sure that no one lacked (Acts 4:34). James has no patience for the believer who says "go in peace, be warmed and filled" to a hungry brother and gives him nothing (James 2:15 to 16). The Bible is not embarrassed by bread. Anybody who says a church should not care about its members' money has not read it.

## Where the Case Runs Out of Scripture

The trouble starts when particular verses are asked to carry more than they say. Solomon Ademiluka (2022), a Nigerian scholar writing in a South African journal, observes that the prosperity message has been prominent in Nigeria since the early 1980s and that its preachers frequently quote passages with little regard for their literary or historical setting. Four examples show what he means, and I'd add that each one sounds convincing until you read the paragraph around it.

The blessing of Abraham is the favourite. Paul does say that Christ redeemed us so that the blessing of Abraham might come to the Gentiles. But he finishes the sentence himself, and the blessing he names is "the promised Spirit through faith" (Galatians 3:14, ESV). Paul's Abraham is the father of all who believe, and his inheritance is a righteousness and a Spirit, with no mention of a bank balance. Next, 3 John 2 ("I pray that you may enjoy good health and that all may go well with you," NIV) is the kind of wish any letter writer of that day put at the front of a letter, much as we write "I hope you are well." It sets no doctrine. Then 2 Corinthians 8:9, where Christ became poor so that we might become rich, sits in a passage in which Paul is asking the Corinthians to give money to hungry believers in Jerusalem. The riches he has in mind are the ones Christ gives, and the example he draws is generosity, not accumulation. Finally, Malachi 3:10 was said to a nation that was cheating the temple of its storehouse offerings under a law that required them, and a seed given this Sunday to unlock a harvest by Wednesday is a long way from the world of that verse.

Two larger problems sit under these. The first is that Jesus and His apostles were not wealthy. Jesus had nowhere to lay His head. Paul describes his own life in hunger and thirst, in cold and exposure, and says he has learned to be content with plenty and with want. If faith guaranteed riches, the New Testament's leading believers were failures of faith, and its writers never said so. The second problem is the direction the teaching pushes. A message that says prosperity follows faith says, by implication, that poverty follows its absence. Ademiluka (2022) argues exactly this, that the teaching fuels greed and the view that poverty signals a lack of righteousness. The poor believer who gave his last bag of rice and is still poor is told, or left to conclude, that the fault is his.

Another Nigerian scholar, Efe Ehioghae (n.d.), points to a tension inside the practice itself. He argues that many pastors press the tithe on the congregation with Old Testament texts and expectations of material return, even while treating the Old Testament as obsolete in other matters. He also asks why the preachers appear affluent while the poverty around them persists. I'd put his point more gently. It's reasonable for a believer to ask a church to be consistent about which part of the law it keeps, and about what it does with the proceeds.

The Bible's own teaching on giving is plain enough that a child can follow it. Give generously, give gladly, and give without keeping a ledger of what you will get back. Paul tells the Corinthians to give as each has decided in his heart, not under compulsion, because God loves a cheerful giver (2 Corinthians 9:7). The reward is promised to the generous, but it is never offered as the price of a transaction.

## What Paul and Jesus Said About Money

Paul wrote to Timothy about people who think "godliness is a means to financial gain" (1 Timothy 6:5, NIV), and then described where that road goes.

> *"But godliness with contentment is great gain. For we brought nothing into the world, and we can take nothing out of it. But if we have food and clothing, we will be content with that. Those who want to get rich fall into temptation and a trap and into many foolish and harmful desires that plunge people into ruin and destruction. For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil. Some people, eager for money, have wandered from the faith and pierced themselves with many griefs."*
>
> — 1 Timothy 6:6 to 10 (NIV)

Read it slowly, because it's so often misquoted. Money is not the root of all evil. The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil, which is a more uncomfortable sentence, since most of us can say with a straight face that we don't love the stuff, we only appreciate it warmly. Paul's picture of the damage is physical. People who chase wealth pierce themselves through.

Mark where the passage sits. It begins with false teachers who "think that godliness is a means to financial gain," so Paul is describing leaders first and the crowd second. Ademiluka (2022) is careful here, and I'd follow his care. The passage does not forbid Christians to be rich, and a few verses later Paul tells the rich to do good, to be generous and ready to share. What it condemns is the desire to be rich and the use of religion as a way of getting there. A study of 1 Timothy 6 among Nigerian Christian youth by Chidinma Ukeachusim (2022) draws the line from materialism in the churches to cybercrime, fraud, ritual activities and kidnapping among young people who profess the faith. Whatever one makes of the causal chain, her instinct is right that a church which preaches wealth as the sign of blessing shouldn't be surprised when its young people go looking for it by any means.

Paul's standard for leaders was equally clear elsewhere. An overseer must not be a lover of money (1 Timothy 3:3), and Peter tells the elders to shepherd the flock willingly and not for shameful gain (1 Peter 5:2). The apostles expected greed in leaders to be the thing the church would watch for. They would be surprised to hear that it has become a spiritual gift.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, writing in Germany in 1937, gave the name cheap grace to forgiveness handed out without repentance and without following Christ, a grace you could hold as a possession. The prosperity version has a different price tag, but it makes a similar promise. A blessing you can buy with the right seed is also something you can have without following anyone. Bonhoeffer thought costly grace was the only kind that was grace at all, and I think a believer can apply that test to any message. Ask what it costs the hearer to follow it, and whether the answer ever includes the cross.

Jesus put the matter more bluntly than either of them.

> *"No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money."*
>
> — Matthew 6:24 (NIV)

He said "cannot". He was describing a job with two employers, where the arithmetic never works out. An institution that builds its whole operation on the idea that it does work out is using the vocabulary of Jesus with the logic turned round.

## Why Churches End Up Competing

You can test a church by how it reacts when a member joins the one down the road. In a healthy movement, a soul saved anywhere is good news, whether it happens in a hall with a thousand seats or a sitting room with six chairs. Paul, writing in chains, said he was glad whenever Christ was preached "whether from false motives or true" (Philippians 1:18, NIV).

A church that runs on market logic can't say that. The neighbouring church is a rival for market share. The family that moves across town is a customer lost. A new branch opened near yours is an invasion of territory. The vocabulary of branding and customer experience has slipped into church growth talk so easily, and with so little alarm, because the assumption behind it has been quietly accepted. It has very little to do with the shepherd who left ninety nine sheep to go after the one.

C. S. Lewis saw the consumer coming long before the branding agencies did. In *The Screwtape Letters* (1942) the senior devil is delighted when the new Christian becomes what he calls a connoisseur of churches, wandering about to find the congregation that suits his taste. Letter XVI contrasts two churches near the patient's home. One has a vicar who thins out his sermons to draw a bigger crowd, and Lewis has the devil admire the result. Screwtape wants the patient treating church as something to rate and to criticise, with no thought of belonging, and he is quite content for the house of God to be turned into a place where religious people shop. Lewis wrote that as comedy, and he meant the diagnosis seriously. The danger isn't in the singing or the vicar. It's in the attitude of the person at the back who is quietly scoring the service as a customer.

I should be careful to say that this runs in both directions. A congregation can behave like customers, and a leader can treat them like customers. The two habits feed one another. A pastor who must keep the numbers up to pay for the building will be tempted to preach what keeps people coming, and a congregation shopping for a blessing will drift towards whoever promises the most. Nobody planned it. It is an ordinary incentive, and ordinary incentives work on saints and sinners alike.

## The Fair Objection From the Church Treasurer

Picture a church treasurer in Ibadan, tired, with the diesel invoice in one hand and the list of teachers' salaries in the other. He has heard enough. "Do you know what it costs to keep this place running? The pastor has a family. And your own Bible says the labourer deserves his pay." (This is a scene, built to put the objection in its strongest form.)

He's right about the bills, and he's right about Paul.

> *"In the same way, the Lord has commanded that those who preach the gospel should receive their living from the gospel."*
>
> — 1 Corinthians 9:14 (NIV)

A church needs money, and I'd go further and say a church needs honest, well kept accounts more than it needs to be poor. Poverty isn't a spiritual qualification. Nobody should read this and conclude that a generator, a salary or a branded T shirt is a sin.

But look at what Paul does with his own right. A few verses on, he says that his reward is that "in preaching the gospel I may offer it free of charge, and so not make full use of my rights as a preacher of the gospel" (1 Corinthians 9:18, NIV). He grants the principle, and then he refuses to cash it in where cashing it in would put a price on the message.

So the line isn't between churches that handle money and churches that don't. It runs through the questions nobody enjoys asking. Is the blessing priced? Does the sermon get written to move the offering? Ask whether a member can request the accounts without being called a witch, and you'll learn a good deal. A church can have an air conditioner and a conscience. The trouble starts when the air conditioner begins to write the sermon.

## What the Law Can and Cannot Fix

Nigeria's law has been circling this ground, and this year has been a busy one.

The new tax laws, the Nigeria Tax Act and the Nigeria Tax Administration Act of 2025, which began to apply at the start of 2026, draw the line the treasurer would rather blur. Dele Oye (as reported by ThisDay in January 2026) read them as making the commercial side of religious bodies taxable. A church or mosque bookstore, for instance, counts as a retail outlet, and the schools, hospitals and printing presses run by religious bodies are the activities most affected by the changes in VAT (Emejo, 2026). Tax exempt "public character" status is tied to profits not being shared among individuals and being put back into charitable aims, and records have to be traceable through digital filing linked to tax, national identity and bank verification numbers. BusinessDay (2026) put the same shift more sharply. Tithes and offerings remain voluntary gifts and are not directly taxed, but how the money is used is now looked at more closely, and personal gifts to clergy may be treated as income if they are not clearly separated from the institution's assets. Those are readings by commentators and advisers. Church finance officers will want the Acts themselves in front of them.

The other front has been the state's power over churches. The Companies and Allied Matters Act of 2020 let the Corporate Affairs Commission suspend the trustees of religious bodies and appoint interim managers. The Christian Association of Nigeria went to court over it. Its first suit was struck out in December 2021 on a technicality over the name in which it had sued (TheCable, n.d.; ThisDay, 2023). In March 2023, Justice Inyang Ekwo of the Federal High Court in Abuja restrained the Commission from using those powers against the association and churches, while declining to strike the sections out for everybody (Gazette NGR, 2023). Separately, in a suit by the constitutional lawyer Emmanuel Ekpenyong, Justice James Omotosho struck down sections 839 to 848 of the Act as too great an intrusion on the affairs of incorporated trustees (ThisDay, 2023). The Commission appealed. In early October 2026 the Court of Appeal in Abuja, in a unanimous judgment read by Justice Okorowo, upheld the nullification in substantial part, holding that the sections infringed freedom of religion and of association and that voluntary associations manage their own internal affairs (ThisDay, 2026). It stopped short of a blanket nullification of section 851, ruling that one unconstitutional only to the extent that it let an administrative committee decide disputes in place of the Federal High Court.

Television ministries have lived under a different kind of rule for years. The scholar Asonzeh Ukah (2011) studied the National Broadcasting Commission's policy on unverified miracle claims and the disputes it provoked after broadcasting was opened up in 1992.

There are good arguments on both sides of these fights, and I won't pretend to settle them. A church that once had to explain nothing to anyone is being asked to account for its money, and a state that wanted to put managers into churches has been told to leave the pulpit alone. Reasonable people can be glad of both. But look at what they add up to. The courts have narrowed the power of government to run a church from outside. The revenue service will ask for receipts. Neither can ask what a congregation ought to ask itself, which is whether anyone left the service closer to God. And with the state stepping back, the audit falls back on the elders and the members. Nobody else is going to examine a church's conscience for it.

Here I have to turn, because a tidy answer would be a false one. The books can be made honest. The question Jesus asked in the temple courts was about whose house this is.

## The Church That Is Still Standing

If I stopped there, I'd be handing you despair and calling it analysis. The church hasn't been swallowed. That claim needs the same hard look I've given the criticism.

Picture a woman who drives to a poorer part of town every Saturday to teach children whose parents can't pay school fees. She does it in Jesus' name, there's no camera, and nobody has asked her to give a testimony. Picture a small congregation, in the same week that a big church somewhere charges for a business summit, taking up an unannounced offering for a widow and pressing every naira into her hand with no thanks required. Picture an elder who has served the same small fellowship for thirty years and has never once asked what he would get for it. These scenes are constructed, but anyone who has spent time in Nigerian churches will recognise them. This kind of church rarely makes the news, which is part of how it survives.

The church Jesus said He would build has outlived every corruption thrown at it. It outlived the years when European Christians looked at the transatlantic slave trade and found a way to fit the gospel and the whip into one theology, a wound so deep that "the white man's God" still carries a charge in conversations across West Africa, and rightly so. It outlived the Donatist and Arian quarrels, the selling of church offices and the selling of indulgences. It will outlive the export of the prosperity gospel and its enthusiastic local franchises too. Not because any institution is indestructible (history is littered with Christian institutions that fell and deserved to) but because the Head of the church is a person who hasn't abandoned His people, even when they've misrepresented Him badly.

Augustine of Hippo, a North African who knew both the grandeur and the failures of the church from the inside, wrote near the beginning of his *Confessions* that God had made us for Himself.

> *"Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee."*
>
> — Augustine, Confessions I.1 (E. B. Pusey, Trans.)

Not until we find the right seed. Not until we spot the prophetic key. Until it rests in God. That is the language of longing and return, and it can't be franchised, branded or collected in a strategic offering moment. The heart knows it, even when the pew has forgotten.

## The Question Luke Leaves Open

Luke tells us why Jesus told the parable of the widow and the unjust judge: to show His disciples "that they should always pray and not give up" (Luke 18:1, NIV). She had no money, no influence and no one to speak for her. She kept coming to the judge's door until he gave in. And then Jesus asks something that He doesn't answer.

> *"However, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?"*
>
> — Luke 18:8 (NIV)

A business can't produce that sort of faith. It can produce attendance figures, stadium events and sermon clips that do well online. It can't produce the widow at the door, who keeps coming because there's nowhere else to go and the God is real. That kind of faith is what remains when the air conditioning fails, the guest speaker cancels, the seed hasn't yielded and Sunday comes anyway.

So here is a small thing to try. At the next meeting of your church where the accounts are read, if there is one, listen to the numbers and ask the question you would ask of any household you were helping to run. Then, before the next Sunday, find the quietest corner of your house, switch off the phone, and pray without a microphone or a service to hold it up. See who answers. I think He will.
