## A Murmur at the Edge of the Table

Luke 15 begins with a murmur. Tax collectors and sinners have pushed in close to hear Jesus, and the Pharisees and scribes, standing at the edge with their arms folded, have found something to say about it.

> *"And the Pharisees and scribes murmured, saying, This man receiveth sinners, and eateth with them."*
>
> — Luke 15:2 (KJV)

Anyone who has sat through enough church services knows that murmur. Nobody shouts it. It comes as the voice behind the hand at the back of the hall, asking who that woman is sitting with and whether the pastor knows what she did. It needs no microphone, and it has outlived every revival.

Everything else in the chapter is Jesus answering it. He tells three stories, one after another, and they are not aimed at the sinners at his table. They are aimed at the people at the edge of the room. That changes how the last story has to be heard, because the church has preached it for so long as the story of the boy who left that we have mostly forgotten who it was told against.

We know the younger son by heart. The inheritance, the far country, the pigs, the walk home with a rehearsed speech. A preacher can feel the altar call coming from the first verse. The elder brother gets a few uncomfortable minutes at the end, and then we sing. And the father, who is the one actually running the story, gets treated as the stage on which his sons look interesting. Two thousand years in, we have still not preached all of it.

I think the story holds three sons, though the text names only two. Reaching the third takes the whole chapter, and it takes the cross.

## Three Stories With One Target

Jesus opens with a shepherd.

> *"What man of you, having an hundred sheep, if he lose one of them, doth not leave the ninety and nine in the wilderness, and go after that which is lost, until he find it?"*
>
> — Luke 15:4 (KJV)

Any sensible farmer would write that off as an acceptable loss, and Jesus knows it. The sums are wrong on purpose. This shepherd leaves ninety nine in open country and goes after one, and when he finds it he doesn't drive it home with a stick. He "layeth it on his shoulders, rejoicing" (Luke 15:5, KJV), then calls his friends and neighbours to come and celebrate.

Anyone in his audience who knew their Scriptures would have heard a second voice under his. Ezekiel had told Israel's shepherds off for failing to look after their flock ("the lost you have not sought," Ezekiel 34:4, ESV), and then God had said of himself, "Behold, I, I myself will search for my sheep and will seek them out" (Ezekiel 34:11, ESV). So when the Pharisees, who thought of themselves as the shepherds of Israel, heard a story about a shepherd who goes after the lost one, they were being measured against what God said a shepherd is.

There is a way of preaching this parable that I have heard in Nigerian churches and found useful, so long as it stays on a leash. It runs like this. An animal that belongs to a shepherd is the shepherd's responsibility. A sheep that is tied or penned is inconvenienced, perhaps, and it may envy the free ones. But the free one answers only to itself, and nobody is watching for the wolf on its behalf. To live outside Christ is to be responsible for yourself, with no one running toward you when you wander toward the edge. Matthew notes that when Jesus needed an animal to ride into Jerusalem he sent for one that was "tied" (Matthew 21:2, ESV), a colt set aside for a purpose, and the preacher draws the line from belonging to the shepherd to being positioned for use. I would hold the point loosely. The lost sheep in Luke 15 is not tied, the colt in Matthew is not a sheep, and no parable can be asked to carry every image we attach to it. But the central claim holds up. In this story the lost sheep belongs to the shepherd before it is found, which is why he goes.

The second story is a woman with ten coins who loses one. She lights a lamp, sweeps the house, and keeps looking until it turns up, and then she too calls her friends and neighbours. A coin cannot repent. It didn't choose the crack in the floor, it can't find itself, and nobody expects it to. The only action in the story belongs to the woman with the broom. Here is something the shepherd story doesn't show. Not everyone who is far from God got there by a dramatic act of rebellion. Some are simply lost: drifted, overlooked, misplaced, useless where they lie. The woman's reply to a missing coin isn't a lecture. It is a lamp and a broom and a refusal to stop.

Watch the numbers as well. The shepherd loses one in a hundred, the woman one in ten, and the father one in two. The loss gets closer to home each time, and the effort grows with it. By the third story the thing at risk is half of everything the man has.

Put the three side by side and a pattern emerges. Something is lost, someone goes out to look, and the finding ends in a party. The lost thing does very little. We have a habit of preaching repentance as though the sinner takes the first step and God, impressed by the effort, takes the second. Luke 15 begins by saying otherwise. Before anything else happens, someone has gone out.

Hold that pattern in mind, because the third story quietly breaks it. There is a father who goes out for each of his two sons. But there is no brother who goes out. I'll come back to that.

## What the Younger Son Really Asked For

The story is short on explanation. A man has two sons, and the younger says to him, "Father, give me the portion of goods that falleth to me" (Luke 15:12, KJV). The same verse reports the father's answer.

> *"And he divided unto them his living."*
>
> — Luke 15:12 (KJV)

Kenneth Bailey, a New Testament scholar who spent decades teaching in the Middle East and read the parables beside the villagers he knew, heard in the younger son's request a wish that the old man were already dead. The text doesn't say so, and I would not push it further than Luke does. But the wisdom of the period leaned the same way. Ben Sira, in a Jewish book written some two centuries before Christ, advised a man not to hand over his property to his children while he was still alive, because the day would come when he needed to ask them for it back. In that world, a father who divided his estate in his own lifetime was giving up his security. So when the son asks, he is asking a living man to treat the family property as something to cash out.

Anyone who has watched relatives arrive with calculators before the burial rites are finished knows how raw such a request is when the man who built the estate is still sitting in the next room. Nigerian families know the law of this too. An eldest son in many households is expected to hold the family together, and where the estate is concerned, the rules about who gets what have caused more silence at the table than almost any other subject. The Supreme Court of Nigeria had to rule in 2014, in *Ukeje v Ukeje*, that an Igbo custom barring daughters from inheriting their late father's estate violated Section 42 of the Constitution (Nairametrics, 2014). I mention it only because it shows how much weight a household places on who inherits, and how much is lost when it is done badly.

In Jesus' world the rule had a Scripture behind it. Deuteronomy 21:17 gave the firstborn a double portion, which meant that in a family of two sons the elder would receive two thirds of the estate and the younger one third. Keep that arithmetic. It becomes important at the end of the story.

Two details in the sentence deserve more attention than they usually get. The first is that the father agrees. He doesn't argue, or lock the safe, or tell the boy to wait for the funeral. The second is easy to miss. He divided his living *unto them*. Both sons received a share. The elder brother's inheritance was settled that day, in the same sentence as the younger's.

The father's consent has a cost, and the story wants us to feel it. He lets the boy go. He doesn't send servants after him with a letter, and he doesn't cut off the money in the second month. Some fathers are strong because they hold on, and some are strong because they let go, and the second sort is much more frightening to a child, because it means the choice is real. God does not keep anyone home by force. When we insist on what we think we want, he has been known to let us have it. A good many people learn the price of a thing only by paying it, and the school of getting exactly what we asked for has taught more of us than the school of being refused.

Sister Esther Diya puts the next part sharply: the love of money can make a person senseless. The text bears her out. The younger son does not reach the pigsty in one moment of collapse. He gets there by a series of decisions that each made sense when he took them, and Luke 15:13 gives the whole slide in one verse (KJV): he "wasted his substance with riotous living."

## Why the Far Country Is Always Far

The distance matters. Luke 15:13 says he "took his journey into a far country" (KJV). He didn't move to the next village. He travelled far enough that nobody who knew his father could stand across a road and ask what he thought he was doing.

Tomilola Adedeji names this exactly: foolishness drives a person away from accountability. The far country is a strategy before it is a place. It is a decision to get far enough from the people who know you that you can stop answering to them.

We understand this in Nigeria. Plenty of people have left for Britain, Canada or the United States for ordinary reasons and worked harder abroad than they ever could at home. But everyone has also heard the other story, told in low voices at family gatherings. Someone leaves, and within a year the restraint that looked like character turns out to have been held up by who was watching. Take away the aunties, the church elders and the neighbours who knew your mother, and a person finds out how much of what he called conviction was really proximity.

> *"Whoever isolates himself seeks his own desire; he breaks out against all sound judgment."*
>
> — Proverbs 18:1 (ESV)

Isolation is not neutral. The proverb gives it a direction. The isolated person seeks something, and what he seeks is his own desire, with no friction from anyone close enough to say, wait, is this who you want to be?

It works in the other direction as well. Think how many people have stayed away from home, from church, from a phone call, for no better reason than shame. They aren't in love with the pigs. They simply can't picture the walk back, and the longer they stay, the more the picture fills with faces: the cousin who will say he always knew, the pastor who will want a testimony, the mother who will cry. A far country is partly a place and partly a way of avoiding a conversation.

Money does a good share of the damage on the way. Paul's warning has been quoted so often that the quotation has gone slightly wrong.

> *"For the love of money is the root of all evil: which while some coveted after, they have erred from the faith, and pierced themselves through with many sorrows."*
>
> — 1 Timothy 6:10 (KJV)

A correction first. Sermons often insist that Paul wrote "a root" and not "the root," on the grounds that the Greek has a particular article. It has none. The noun stands bare, so "a root" and "the root" are both fair renderings, and modern translations choose the first because Paul is stating a generalisation and not claiming that every evil grows from money. Either way the sentence says what it says about love of money, which the younger son shows better than any commentary. He didn't love the far country. He loved what he thought the far country could give him, and when the money ran out, the far country had nothing else to offer.

Then the famine arrives, and nobody had planned for that. Tomilola Adedeji calls a famine like this a revelation of stupidity, and I take her to mean that circumstances can strip the show off the flamboyant and leave a man standing in the wreckage of his own decisions with nothing to say. Suffering does not prove sin, and I would never preach that a famine is a verdict. But in this story the famine exposes something already present. Luke sets it down in his plainest sentences: the boy "joined himself to a citizen of that country," and was sent to feed pigs, and "would fain have filled his belly with the husks that the swine did eat: and no man gave unto him" (Luke 15:15 and 16, KJV). A Jewish boy among unclean animals, hungry enough to envy them, with no one in the whole country willing to give him anything. That last phrase finishes the picture of what a life of unaccountable freedom purchases.

Paul's description in Romans 1 of people God "gave over to a reprobate mind" (Romans 1:28, KJV) is the far country in every generation. It was never only ancient. It wears different clothes in a different decade and eats the same husks.

## Coming to Himself in the Pigsty

> *"And when he came to himself,"*
>
> — Luke 15:17 (KJV)

The Greek is *eis heauton elthon*. He came into himself. Hunger did what a father's pleading could not have done. It took the noise away long enough for him to see his own situation. This is what repentance looks like before it is a theological category: a person who has been living at a distance from his own soul stops for long enough to look at the state he is in.

Many of us put off that moment, because we aren't ready to see. Before we meet Christ we assemble a version of ourselves that is basically fine. Managing. Getting by. If we saw our spiritual condition without the noise and distraction, the actual poverty and the distance from the father's house, we would understand why the far country, however glamorous its packaging, is always a pigsty by another name. The younger son is not unique. He stands for every person who has yet to come home, and what the road home needs of him is exactly what he did in that field. He looked at where he was, and he turned.

I would resist reading too much devotion into what comes next. His speech, as the next verses give it, is a business proposal. He has worked out that his father's hired hands eat better than he does, and he plans to apply for a job: "Father, I have sinned against heaven, and before thee, And am no more worthy to be called thy son: make me as one of thy hired servants" (Luke 15:18 and 19, KJV). It is thin repentance, mixed with appetite, and Luke doesn't pretend otherwise.

That is exactly where the doctrine of grace begins to bite. Reformed theology says that no one comes to God unless God moves first, and Arminian theology says that God's grace goes before every person and makes a response possible. Both traditions have a name for this, and in each case the claim is that the initiative lies with God. The older word is prevenient grace, meaning grace that comes before. The two traditions differ about whether the prepared heart can still say no. They don't differ about the order. In the parable the son's repentance is mixed and the father's welcome is not. Paul says as much when he asks whether anyone despises "the riches of his goodness and forbearance and longsuffering" without knowing that the goodness of God "leadeth thee to repentance" (Romans 2:4, KJV). The father at the gate was a cause of the walk home as well as its reward.

## The Father on the Road

> *"And he arose, and came to his father. But when he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him."*
>
> — Luke 15:20 (KJV)

Read it slowly, and the first thing to notice is that the father saw him while he was still a great way off. You can only see a figure on the horizon if you are already looking at the horizon. Nobody had sent word that the boy was coming. Whatever the father had been doing for months, he had also been watching the road.

Many sermons here tell the story of the *kezazah*, a ceremony in which the village would break a pot at the feet of any Jewish boy who had lost his inheritance among Gentiles and declare him cut off from his people. In the sermon the father runs to get to his son before the village does. One published version has the community smash a large pot in front of the returning son and announce, "You are now cut off from your people!" (Biola Magazine, 2010). It is vivid, and it has been told so often that it now travels as plain fact. It needs checking before it is repeated.

The ceremony came into popular use through Kenneth Bailey, whose book *The Cross and the Prodigal* (revised edition 2005) is thoughtful and well worth reading, and who drew on his years among village communities in the Middle East. The difficulty is that when people went looking for the ceremony in ancient texts, they found less than the sermons suggest. The rabbinic passages usually cited belong to the Talmud, which was put together centuries after Jesus, and as those who have examined them report, the cases they describe involve a man selling ancestral land or marrying someone unsuitable, not a boy who had wasted his money among Gentiles (Sleepless Theology, n.d.). Amy Jill Levine, a Jewish New Testament scholar who writes on the parables, observes that the parable itself gives no hint of any such rite (as reported in Spinale, 2015a).

So I won't build on the broken pot. I don't need to. The father doesn't have to be racing a mob to be astonishing. A wealthy man who has been wronged as badly as this father has been wronged is entitled to wait on the veranda and let the boy come to him. Bailey adds that a man of standing in that culture would have to gather up his robe to run, and that the bare legs were a humiliation. That comes from observation of Middle Eastern village life and not from an ancient text I can check, so I hold it loosely, though it sounds right to me. The text itself is enough. The father *ran*. He *fell on his neck*. He *kissed him*.

There is a second thing here worth knowing, because it does not depend on the kezazah at all. Deuteronomy 21:18 to 21 describes a rebellious son, a glutton and a drunkard, whom his parents bring to the elders of the town to be stoned. Nothing in Luke 15 says the village was reaching for the stones. But anyone in Jesus' audience who knew the Law knew what the Law said about a son like this one. The younger son is not a naughty boy. He has humiliated the family, abandoned the land, and gone to live with the unclean. Whatever the village was ready to do, the father meets him ahead of it.

And notice the order. The son begins his speech after the embrace. "And the son said unto him, Father, I have sinned against heaven, and in thy sight, and am no more worthy to be called thy son" (Luke 15:21, KJV). Look at what is missing. The clause about being made a hired servant never appears in the telling. The text doesn't say why. It simply goes on at once to the father's orders. He hasn't earned the hug by rehearsing well. By the time he opens his mouth, he is already being held.

> *"But the father said to his servants, Bring forth the best robe, and put it on him; and put a ring on his hand, and shoes on his feet:"*
>
> — Luke 15:22 (KJV)

Hired hands do not get the best robe. The father is dressing a son, and he is doing it in front of the servants and the neighbours, so that nobody has to wonder where the boy now stands. The ring is authority, the shoes are a free man's, and the calf that comes next is a village feast. Every item reinstates the sonship the boy had tried to surrender, and the father does all of it before the boy can finish a sentence about earning it.

Paul puts the same logic into a single line, and he wrote it about the cross and not about a gate.

> *"but God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us."*
>
> — Romans 5:8 (ESV)

Grace gets there first. That isn't because repentance doesn't matter, since the boy did get up and walk. Repentance is what makes the welcome receivable. It doesn't make the welcome possible. The love was already on the road before the speech was ready.

## What the Welcome Costs the Father

A careful reader will already have an objection. Luke 15 contains no blood, no altar, no substitute. The father simply forgives. If a parable can set out the whole of God's mercy with no cross in it, why did Christ need to die at all? Liberal theology since Peter Abelard has often drawn exactly this conclusion, that the cross shows us a love that needed no payment, and that the parable is the plainer teaching. It is a serious objection and deserves a serious answer.

The first answer is that a parable is a single lens. Luke has Jesus tell a story about a woman, a lamp and a coin, and nobody concludes that salvation is a matter of housekeeping. The three stories were told to answer one complaint, that Jesus eats with sinners, and they answer it. They were never meant to be the whole doctrine of the atonement, and Luke himself, who wrote the whole Gospel, ends it at a cross.

The second answer is harder to dismiss. Read slowly, the parable is full of cost, and the cost falls on the father. He loses a third of the estate at the first request. He is publicly shamed by a son who has dishonoured the family and carried its name into a pigpen. He takes the long walk down the road on the boy's behalf. And when the feast begins, whose property is on the table? The estate was divided already. The calf, the robe and the ring come out of what remains, and what remains, by the logic of verse 31, belongs to the elder son. The father pays for the party with his other child's inheritance. That is one of the hardest facts in the story, and I will come back to it. For now it is enough to see that forgiveness here is not cheap. Somebody pays, and the one who pays is the one who forgives.

This is how the parable meets the doctrine of the cross, and it is why Bailey titled his book *The Cross and the Prodigal*. The traditions of the church have described the cross in several ways. The older view, associated with Irenaeus and Gregory of Nyssa and recovered by Gustaf Aulén, calls it a victory over the powers that held us. The view associated with Anselm calls it satisfaction, the honour of God restored by the Son's obedience. The Reformers sharpened that into penal substitution, in which Christ takes on himself the penalty our sin deserved, and Abelard's followers stressed the moral influence of a love that wins us back. Each tradition wants to say something true. None of them says that forgiveness cost God nothing. Paul's own account of how God could remain righteous and still welcome the guilty is that Christ's death was displayed "so that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26, ESV). The father in the parable shows us the justifier. The cross shows us how the justice is also met.

Run the parable that way and it stops looking like a rival to the cross. The father who runs and absorbs the shame, who spends his estate on a feast for a son who has shamed him, is a picture in miniature of the God who did not wait on a veranda for us. That is the cost, and the story asks us to look at it before we ask what the younger son is worth.

## The Son Who Worked and Never Arrived

The elder brother is where the parable becomes uncomfortable, because Jesus does not end it at the party. He turns the camera to the field.

> *"Now his elder son was in the field:"*
>
> — Luke 15:25 (KJV)

He is working, as he has been all these years. When he comes near the house he hears music, and a servant explains that his brother has come home and the fatted calf has been killed. The elder brother is "angry" and will not go in. So the father does something he did not have to do: "Therefore came his father out, and intreated him" (Luke 15:28, KJV). That is the second time in one story the father leaves a party and goes out onto a road for a son. He runs to the first and pleads with the second.

Now listen to the elder brother properly, because his complaint is a reasonable one and I would like to put it in words he would accept. I stayed. I have been here every day. When there was no money I was the one who found it. When the roof leaked I climbed up. I never once embarrassed you in front of the neighbours, and in all these years you never gave me a goat for me and my friends. Then this boy, who has eaten your money with prostitutes, walks in smelling of pigs and you kill the best animal you own.

Anyone who has watched a family reunion in Nigeria knows the face behind those words. (Picture it as a scene.) One sibling has paid the electricity bill and driven the old woman to hospital for a decade. Another arrives from abroad to a house full of food and relatives falling over themselves, and the first sits in the kitchen doing the washing up. Most of us have been one or the other, and a few of us on different days have been both. If your first instinct is to dismiss the elder brother as a villain, I don't think you have listened to him yet. His arithmetic is fine.

What's wrong lies under the arithmetic, and the father puts his finger on it. Marvellous Oluwadunsin says the elder brother doesn't know the father because he has been working and not serving. The difference isn't a matter of vocabulary. Working for someone means the relationship is defined by what you produce for him. Serving someone means the relationship is defined by who he is to you. Somewhere in the long years in the field, the father's house became the place where the work led, a headquarters and no longer a home.

Look at his own words. "Lo, these many years do I serve thee" (Luke 15:29, KJV), and the Greek verb is the one for a slave's labour. He has lived in the house all his life and organised his entire relationship with the man who owns it around wages. His father is a boss whose approval he is saving up for. And listen to how it comes out of him: "this thy son," he says of his brother (Luke 15:30, KJV). Not my brother. Yours. He has disowned the relationship in a phrase. The father quietly takes the words back at the end: "this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found" (Luke 15:32, KJV). What the elder brother handed away, the father hands back.

Marvellous Oluwadunsin adds, rightly, that zeal without knowledge is dangerous. The elder brother is zealous. He is also entirely wrong about what his father is like. He has lived in the father's house and does not know the father's heart. He thought the father kept accounts like a ledger, and he thought the family's resources were a reward scheme. That is the state of a person who has done everything religious and still has not met the one who is being served.

I need to be careful here, because this point can be heard the wrong way. Some of the things the elder brother does are good things. Fasting and long prayer, giving, early mornings, vigils, showing up when nobody is watching. None of that is the problem, and the best sons do all of it. It becomes a ledger when it turns into overtime, when you fast twenty one days and privately expect God to settle the account. The same habit is a delight when you do it as a child in your father's house, and a grievance when you do it as a labourer in somebody else's. The difference lies in what you think you are doing it for.

It is worth asking why Jesus told the parable this way. Many early readers heard the two sons as Israel and the nations, and that reading has its place, since the church has always sensed that the elder son's resentment of the feast is the resentment that some in Israel felt about Gentiles. But Luke gives the occasion in the first two verses, and the occasion fixes the target. The Pharisees and scribes are the audience, and they have just muttered that Jesus eats with sinners. They are the elder brother. They have the Law, they have stayed in the house, and when sinners come home they stand outside and complain.

The church can become this too. A community so invested in its own compliance that it can't celebrate a sinner coming home. A congregation fluent in the language of working for God that has forgotten the language of knowing him. A people with the anointing oil, the worship band, the prayer vigils and the seed offering, who cannot tell the Father's house from a religious headquarters.

## An Inheritance Still Unopened

The father's answer to the elder son is one of the quietest and hardest lines in the New Testament.

> *"And he said unto him, Son, thou art ever with me, and all that I have is thine."*
>
> — Luke 15:31 (KJV)

Remember the estate was divided in verse 12, *unto them*. When the younger son left, everything that remained was already the elder son's. The calf in the pen was his calf. The robe in the cupboard was his robe. The father, strictly speaking, is throwing the feast out of his elder son's property, and the elder son has never complained about that because he has never thought of it as his own. He grumbles that his father "never gavest me a kid." He never asked. Any day in those years he could have walked into the pen and taken a goat, and the father would have said, of course, it's all yours.

And the share was the larger one. Under the Deuteronomy rule the firstborn's double portion was sitting untouched. He had lived in his father's house like a servant who did not know he was a son, working to earn what was already his by birth.

Ancient brothers are only part of it. The story describes a Tuesday morning in a good many churches, in Lagos and Abuja and Ibadan and London and Houston, anywhere believers have been in the faith long enough to build a theology of earning what they should simply receive. We pray more, fast more, give more, attend more, serve more. These are not bad things. The mistake lies in treating the disciplines of a relationship as conditions of the inheritance. So we watch a new believer receive healing, joy, peace and a restored marriage, things we have circled in prayer for years, and instead of celebrating we stand outside the party wondering when it will be our turn. It was always our turn. The inheritance is not in a queue.

Paul gives the same truth its proper name. Under the old arrangement, he says, a child was no better than a slave, but in Christ there is a different status.

> *"So you are no longer a slave, but a son, and if a son, then an heir through God."*
>
> — Galatians 4:7 (ESV)

I have to add a caution, because in Nigeria the word *inheritance* can be turned into a different sermon altogether, in which the faithful are told to claim a house, a car and a visa by faith. That is not what the father says. He doesn't promise the elder son a goat on demand. He says that everything the father has is his, which is a statement about relationship and access, and it can be received only by someone who stays in the house and knows the father. The goods in the story are a feast, a robe and a brother. They are not trophies.

Temiloluwa Adedeji makes a practical point that sharpens the matter. If the elder brother does not rise to the occasion, the servants could come to claim what belongs to the son. The point reaches past inheritance law into identity. A son who doesn't know he is a son, who relates to the father's house as an employee, will in the end find that he has voluntarily taken a servant's place in what was always his own home. The tragedy is not that someone else took what was his. The tragedy is that he gave it away by never living in it.

## Fasting and Prayer in a Father's House

A question circles this kind of teaching like a moth round a lamp, and it deserves a straight answer. If the inheritance is already ours, if the father is already running toward us, if grace is not bought with discipline, what are fasting and prayer for?

The disciples asked a version of it. They had been sent out by Jesus and had come back rejoicing that even the spirits submitted to them. Then they met a boy tormented since childhood, and nothing happened. Jesus delivered the boy, and afterward they asked him privately why they could not cast the spirit out. Matthew records his answer in two parts. First, "Because of your unbelief" (Matthew 17:20, KJV). Then, in the older English text, "Howbeit this kind goeth not out but by prayer and fasting" (Matthew 17:21, KJV).

An honest teacher has to say something here about the text. Most modern translations do not print verse 21 in the main text, and the parallel in Mark 9:29 reads, in the ESV, "This kind cannot be driven out by anything but prayer" (Mark 9:29), with fasting added in some later copies. Whether the words "and fasting" came from Jesus' lips or from a scribe who knew the church's practice, I cannot settle, and I won't build a doctrine on an insecure phrase. But the point doesn't depend on them. Jesus himself fasted, and he assumed his followers would (Matthew 6:16 to 18 begins "when you fast" in the ESV, with no "if"), and the question of what it does is a fair one.

The popular answer is that fasting and prayer are a spiritual technology for stubborn demons. Pray long enough and fast hard enough and the demon must give way. There is a problem with that, and it appears when you look at what fasting did and did not do for Jesus. Before his public ministry he went into the wilderness for forty days without food. The tempter came to him there, at the end of the fast or during it (Matthew 4:1 to 4, KJV, and honesty requires admitting the text leaves the timing open). The fast did not repel the devil. Satan came anyway. He walked up to the most spiritually prepared human being who ever lived and offered him bread, kingdoms and a spectacle.

What the fast did was prepare Jesus. It sharpened him. When the temptation came he met it from clarity and not from weakness, and every one of his three replies came out of Deuteronomy, Scripture that was alive in him long before the fast began and fully at hand in the moment of trial. Fasting did not give him new power. It cleared the noise and kept his attention on what he already knew.

Isaiah says as much about those who wait on God.

> *"But they that wait upon the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint."*
>
> — Isaiah 40:31 (KJV)

Waiting on the Lord in prayer, in fasting, in the patient study of his Word looks passive from outside. It is the deliberate act of a person who knows that spiritual capacity grows. The person who was crawling begins to walk, and the one who was walking begins to run. Fasting and prayer work less like a mechanism for repelling outside forces and more like a workshop for building inner ones.

So when Jesus told his disciples what he did, I take him to be addressing their faith, as the first half of his answer says plainly. They had been casting out spirits on borrowed authority, riding on the momentum of a commission without cultivating the inner life that makes the authority last. They had the assignment without the workshop. Faith that has been built in the secret place does not collapse when it meets something it has never seen.

That puts fasting in its proper place in the story we have been reading. The younger son did not need a discipline to earn the robe. The elder son's disciplines, practised for wages, left him outside. The only fasting that sits comfortably in a father's house is the fasting of a child, who goes without bread because he wants to hear his father better and not because he is paying a debt. The inheritance is real. The father is running. The disciplines are not the price of entry. They are how a son becomes the kind of person who can handle what he is given.

## What an Elder Brother Owes the Family

The elder brother ought to have mirrored the father to the lost son. In the household Jesus describes, the eldest son was more than the more obedient sibling. He was the household's representative, the one whose conduct told everyone watching what kind of family this was. When the younger son came home, the elder should have been the first one running, not in competition with his father but alongside him, because a person who knows the father's heart cannot sit still when the thing the father loves most comes back.

Nigerian families grasp this without being told. When a cousin has disappeared into a bad crowd in Lagos, the eldest is the one who takes the bus, finds the address and brings him home. When two uncles are quarrelling, the eldest sits down between them. It is one of the heavy duties of being first. In Luke 15 the eldest is the one who has to be fetched.

Instead the elder brother stays in the field, absent from the homecoming, unavailable to receive the returning brother. When he does arrive, he stands outside in the dark with a list of grievances.

How we speak shows who we think we are carrying. "This son of yours" has a long life in Christian conversation. It comes out as *that girl*, *that boy*, *that family*, *those people who left the church*. Listen to how a family talks about a daughter who came home pregnant, or a son who came out of prison, and within a minute you can tell which of them the speaker thinks belongs inside and which has been put on the other side of the gate. The moment a lost person becomes *their* problem, their sin, their consequence, we have stepped out of the father's posture and into the yard. To say *my brother* is to take on the burden of the one who goes out. It costs more than the other word, and it is the only one the father uses.

It may be that church is the place where this is hardest. We can organise a thanksgiving service for a new car at very short notice. A man who has been away eight years and wants to come back into the fellowship gets a long silence and a seat at the back. That is rarely the fault of one or two unkind people. It is what a system does when it has grown very good at working for God and has lost the habit of knowing him, and anyone willing to cross the room and sit down beside him can fix it.

## The Brother Nobody Sends Out

The story stops, and it stops in a strange place. Jesus doesn't tell us whether the elder brother went in. There is no last line about the music or the dancing. The father has just said "thy brother," and then the camera goes off with the elder son still standing in the yard. Jonah ends the same way, with a prophet sulking outside a city God has spared and God asking him a question he never answers (Jonah 4:11). In both books the silence belongs to the reader.

The silence is deliberate. The people the story is told to are the Pharisees at the edge of the crowd, and what Jesus leaves unfinished is exactly what he is asking them to finish.

But I think the silence also has a shape, and the shape is a missing person. In the first two stories someone goes out: the shepherd into the wilderness, the woman with her broom. In the third the father goes out, twice, once for each son. A household like this one needs more than a father. It needs an eldest son whose job is to go. In Luke 15 the person who ought to be that brother is the one standing in the yard.

That is where Christian readers have always heard a third son. Let me be careful about what I am claiming. Amy Jill Levine has warned that parables are not allegories, and she is right to say so. Luke 15 contains no character with Jesus' name on him, and a reading that finds one has to be argued for and not assumed. But look at the occasion. The one telling the story is, at that very moment, sitting at a table with the sinners the Pharisees are complaining about. He is doing precisely what the elder brother refuses to do. The empty chair in the story is occupied in the room where the story is told.

The New Testament says this about him in so many words.

> *"For he who sanctifies and those who are sanctified all have one source. That is why he is not ashamed to call them brothers,"*
>
> — Hebrews 2:11 (ESV)

The writer adds that "he had to be made like his brothers in every respect" (Hebrews 2:17, ESV). And on the morning of the resurrection, when the men who scattered at his arrest are hiding behind a locked door, the first message he sends them uses the word the elder brother could not say: "go to my brothers" (John 20:17, ESV).

Shiao Chong, reading Luke 15 with Tim Keller's *The Prodigal God* (2008), makes the point in his own way. Keller argues that both brothers are trying to be their own saviour, one by rebellion and one by moral performance. Chong adds that the elder brother should have gone looking for his lost brother, and that Jesus is the true elder brother who does, at the cost of the cross. That is Chong's reading and not Keller's, and I find it hard to resist.

African Christian thought has a long history of reaching for the same idea. Timothy Palmer's survey describes how Charles Nyamiti, in *Christ as Our Ancestor* (1984), proposed that Christ is the brother ancestor in the fullest sense, and how Kwame Bediako argued in 1995 that Christ may rightly be called, in African terms, the supreme ancestor. Palmer is careful to add that not everyone agrees. Some respected African theologians did not use the language, and the students he asked mostly rejected it, on the sensible ground that Christ is alive and ancestors are dead. Travis Myers, writing on Hebrews, takes a more modest route, using the African role of the eldest brother as a limited illustration of what Hebrews means by calling Christ the firstborn, and warning that the illustration must never replace the text. I am not asking anyone to adopt a vocabulary. But anyone who grew up in a Nigerian family understands the instinct underneath it. A family needs an elder brother who goes out. And the only way the Christian answer escapes the charge of borrowing from ancestor religion is the one Palmer and Myers both insist on, that this brother is alive, that he went out to find us at his own cost, and that he is not ashamed of us.

Henri Nouwen's meditation on Rembrandt's painting of the return of the prodigal ends somewhere many of us would not expect. Whichever son you see yourself in, you are being asked to become the father. "The return to the Father is ultimately the challenge to become the Father" is how he puts it, in the passage quoted by Kevin Spinale in *America* (Nouwen, 1992, p. 123, as cited in Spinale, 2015b). I would add only what Luke 15 itself suggests, that the father in this story spends most of it on the road. To stand where he stands is to be someone who goes out.

## The Door Is Still Open

If you are the younger son, hear what the story says about the road. You don't have to arrive with a better speech. The man who is watching for you has been watching for a good while, and the robe is already in the house.

If you are the elder son, which is to say you have been faithful for years and are tired and feel unseen, the story has something harder and gentler to say to you. Your father is not unaware of what you have done, and he doesn't dispute your arithmetic. He tells you the whole estate is yours and has been all along. You can go and take the goat. You can also go in and sit down beside a brother whom the father has already named as yours.

The story ends with a verse that ends in the middle of a conversation: "this thy brother was dead, and is alive again; and was lost, and is found." The music is still going. The calf is on the fire, the younger son is washed and dressed and probably still half in shock, and somewhere out in the yard a man with his arms folded is deciding whether to go in. Jesus doesn't say what he decided, and I think the reason is that the Pharisees were still deciding, and so are we.

So here is a small suggestion. Think of the person you have been calling *this son of yours*. Perhaps it is a cousin who went abroad and stopped answering the phone, or a woman in your church whose story everyone knows, or a father you haven't spoken to in six years. Someone in your life has been lost long enough that you have stopped saying their name. The third son in this story is the one who goes out. It is what Jesus did for us, and it is also what he asks of us.

The music is still playing. There is a chair for you, and next to it is the one belonging to the brother you have been refusing to name. Go in and sit beside him.
